Strategic Report

About Us

HSBC Innovation Bank Limited (‘the Bank’, formerly
Silicon Valley Bank UK Limited, ‘SVBUK’), the financial
partner to the innovation economy, helps entrepreneurs,
investors and the world’s most innovative companies
achieve their ambitious goals. Our mission is to empower
technology and life science/healthcare businesses at
every stage with the expertise, energy, and connections
they need to share their vision with the world.

We offer a range of flexible banking and financing
solutions for start-ups, growth businesses, corporates and
investors, including traditional term loans, asset-based
loans, revolving lines of credit, warehouse facilities,
recurring revenue facilities, mezzanine lending, acquisition
finance facilities, corporate working capital facilities and
credit card programs. These loans may be unsecured or
secured by clients’ assets or future cash flows.

Our client treasury management products and services
include a wide range of deposit services including current
and term deposit accounts, payments and cash
management solutions.

Through close collaboration with the global HSBC
Innovation Banking network and other HSBC Group
entities, we connect clients to international markets and
enable clients to access products that are not available
within the Bank, including foreign exchange services, on
an arms-length basis.

Our goal

We are dedicated to supporting entrepreneurs and clients
of all sizes and stages throughout their life cycles. Our
vision is to create an innovation centre of excellence that
will support breakout early-stage companies up to large
public and privately-held corporates, and the venture
capital (‘VC’) and private equity (‘PE’) funds that provide
capital to help these organisations grow and scale.

Our clients

We provide products and services to serve the needs of
our clients in the UK and through our representative
offices in Sweden and Denmark.

Our technology clients operate in the industries of
enterprise software and hardware (such as infrastructure
software, applications, software services), consumer
software and internet (such as digital content and
advertising technology), frontier technologies (such as
semiconductors, communications, data, storage and
electronics), financial technologies, and climate
technology and sustainability. Our life science/healthcare
clients operate in the industries of biopharma, healthtech,
medical devices, healthcare services and diagnostics and
tools.

We serve our clients throughout their life cycles, beginning
with the early-stage and progressing through later stages
of their development as their needs mature and expand.
We serve these clients primarily through five groups.

Early Stage Business

Clients in our Early Stage Business ('ESB’) are generally
privately-held companies in the start-up or early stages of
their life cycles and funded by friends and family, "seed" or
“angel” investors or have gone through an initial round of
venture capita! financing. They are typically engaged in
research and development activities and may have
brought only a few products or services to market, if any.

Venture and Growth Banking

Clients in our Venture and Growth Banking are privately-
held companies in the intermediate or later stages of their
life cycles, and are often dependent on venture capital for
funding. Our Venture and Growth clients generally have a
more established product or service offering in the market
and may be in a period of expansion.

Corporate Finance

Our Corporate Finance Practice primarily serves our large
corporate clients, which are more mature and established
companies. These clients are generally publicly-held or
large privately-held companies and have a more
sophisticated product or service offering in the market.

Sponsor Finance

Our Sponsor Finance group provides debt financing in
support of PE-sponsored company acquisitions, primarily
those operating in the Enterprise Software industries.

Strategic Fund Solutions

Our Strategic Fund Solutions (‘SFS') business serves
clients in the alternative investments community, many of
whom are investors in the portfolio company clients to
whom we provide banking services. Our SFS business
provides banking products and services primarily to our
global PE and VC clients. In particular, we provide credit
facilities to these clients, including capital call lines of
credit, the repayment of which is dependent on the
payment of capital calls or management fees by the
underlying limited partner investors in the funds managed
by the firms.

Since our founding, we have cultivated strong
relationships within the VC community, which has over
time expanded to relationships within the PE community.
We believe our network helps to facilitate deal flow
opportunities between these PE/VC firms and the
companies within the markets we serve.

Acquisition by HSBC UK

The Bank was acquired by HSBC UK Bank pic (‘HSBC
Uk’), the UK ring-fenced bank of HSBC Holdings plc, on
13 March 2023, following a liquidity event that impacted
our former parent, Silicon Valley Bank (‘SVB US'), which
had a contagion-like impact on our business in the UK.
The event in March and the subsequent integration into
HSBC UK have had an impact on the short-term
performance of the business, which is reflected in our
2023 financial results.

Business review

Market and business overview

2023 was a challenging year for the innovation sector, as
investors and founders faced persistent economic
uncertainty, geo-political tensions, and an industry-specific
banking crisis. High interest rates, valuation pressures,
and constrained funding and VC investment culminated in
sustained period of market correction and significant
slowdown in activity.

Our core UK and European markets however, whilst not
immune to these trends, continue to mature and attract
investors and founders.

VC investment is a key driver of our deposit growth, and
we faced pressures in 2023 as firms overcame the
challenges of a tight funding environment whilst
experiencing high cash bum. Deposit diversification trends
post the March crisis further challenged our growth
trajectory. However, high quality and resilient firms
continue to attract investment which, coupled with a
gradual recovery in VC investment volumes, still offers
major opportunities for deposit growth. Demand for
lending has remained as firms seek to extend cash
runways.

Despite difficult market conditions impacting almost every
company in the sector, credit losses remained low due to
our underwriting model, diligent portfolio management,
and deep knowledge of our clients and markets.

Entering the second half of 2023, we saw initial signs of
recovery by growing clarity around the direction of rate
policy and broader macro stabilisation. Our outlook for the
sector is positive, supported not just by external
stabilisation, but also by strong levels of new company
creation, which has driven positive client acquisition,
investor interest in early-stage firms, and the mainstream
arrival of Al and sustainability-linked tech.

We are well positioned within HSBC Group to tap into
these unique growth opportunities in the innovation
economy at a global level, through utilising our respective
knowledge, sector focus, banking platform, and network to
support the success of our clients and partners.

Other balance sheet items

Since our acquisition by HSBC UK in March 2023 we have
taken steps to align our interest rate and cash
management practices with that of other subsidiaries
within the HSBC Group. This process led to changes in
our balance sheet structure which leaves us well
positioned for future growth.

During the year, we disposed of our portfolio of high-
quality liquid assets (‘HQLA’) eligible financial investments.
We also closed our reserve account with the Bank of
England and discontinued our access to the Sterling
Monetary Framework. As a result, our financial
investments and cash and balances with central banks
were nil as at December 2023.

Our excess cash balances are now placed with HSBC UK
in interest bearing accounts, leading to an increase in
loans and advances to banks. Additionally, we now utilise
a funding line from HSBC UK to fund our balance sheet,
leading to an increase in deposits by banks.

These changes in balance sheet structure were enabled
by our inclusion in the HSBC UK Domestic Liquidity Sub-
group (‘DoLSub’). Our liquidity position is managed as part
of the HSBC UK Liquidity Group, under which members
agree to provide liquidity support to one another where
necessary.

The HSBC UK DoLSub held a significant balance of High
Quality Liquid Assets ('HQLA’) throughout 2023 of £94.8bn
(calculated on an average basis) and maintained an
average Liquidity Coverage Ratio (‘LCR’) of 201%.

Anti-bribery and corruption

Bribery is the act of giving or receiving anything of value
with the intent to obtain or retain a business advantage.
Corruption is the abuse of entrusted power for private
gain. Bribery and corruption risk arises from day-to-day
banking operations involving client, third parties and
employees.

We take a zero-tolerance approach towards bribery and
corruption and consider such activity to be unethical and
contrary to good corporate governance. We are committed
to complying with all applicable legal and regulatory
requirements, primarily the UK Bribery Act 2010.

The HSBC Group Global Financial Crime Policy requires
that all activity must be:

- conducted without intent to bribe or corrupt;
— reasonable and transparent;

- considered to not be lavish nor disproportionate to the
professional relationship;

- appropriately documented with business rationale; and
- authorised at an appropriate level of seniority

It further requires that we identify and mitigate the risk of
our clients and third parties committing bribery or
corruption. Among other controls, we use client due
diligence and transaction monitoring to identify and help
mitigate the risk that our clients are involved in bribery and
corruption. We perform anti-bribery and corruption risk
assessments on third parties that expose us to this risk.

We require that all staff, the Board of Directors (the
‘Board') and associated persons comply with the principles
in the HSBC Global Financial Crime Policy and annual
mandatory anti-bribery and corruption (‘AB&C’) training is
provided to all staff, with additional targeted training,
tailored to the roles of individuals.

Oversight

Overall accountability of the management of risk lies with
our Board. However, it is the Money Laundering Reporting
Officer ((MLRO’), in their role as Senior Manager Function
(‘SMF17'), who is accountable for our compliance with our
AB&C obligations. The MLRO, together with the Financial
Crime Team, provide oversight of our compliance with the
HSBC Group Global Financial Crime Policy.

We acknowledge the bribery and corruption related risks
that we face, and these are discussed regularly at our
Financial Crime Committee.

We ensure that independent, risk-based oversight and
assurance activity is in place across all three lines of
defence. This allows us to determine the effectiveness of
our AB&C framework which facilitates compliance with the
relevant legal and regulatory obligations in the UK.

Risk review

Our approach to risk

As the events of March 2023 have proven, no
proportionate approach to risk management can deal with
the kind of extreme contagion event experienced by the
business. However we recognise there are a number of
risks in the normal course of business providing services,
lending, deposit taking and FX activities within the
innovation economy. To effectively manage these risks,
our approach to risk is based on the following set of
overarching principles:

1. Implementing an effective governance and oversight
function that creates clear accountability, effective
challenge, and decision making based on the concept
of "three lines of defence”

2. Fostering a risk culture where employees at all levels
understand their roles, drive responsibility,
accountability, and ownership of risk management

3. Setting and operating within a risk appetite framework
that facilitates our strategy and balances appropriate
risk-taking monitored within defined metrics and limits

Risk Management Framework

During the second haif of 2023, we started transitioning
from our previous risk management framework to the
HSBC Group Risk Management Framework (‘RMF’). The
fundamental components of both frameworks are similar.
This risk management section is set out on the basis of
our existing Framework, since that is how our internal
reporting operated for 2023. For the 2024 Annual Report
and Accounts, in line with our internal reporting, risk
management will be set out based on the HSBC Group
RMF. Since the RMF transition is an ongoing rather than a

IFRS9) our approach is already fully aligned with HSBC

Three lines of defence model

Our approach to defining risk roles, responsibilities, and
authorities is to operate under a “three lines of defence”
risk management model. Collectively, the three lines of
defence (‘LOD’) establish a system to control, optimise risk
decision making and are responsible for keeping
management and the Board informed of changes or
threats to our risk profile so that the Board can advise and
act, as appropriate, and provide challenge to
management's recommendations and decisions.

The three lines of defence are summarised below:

The first line of defence owns the risks and is responsible
for identifying, recording, reporting and managing the risk
in line with risk appetite, and ensuring that the right
controls and assessments are in place to mitigate them.

The second line of defence monitors adherence to our risk
appetite, supports and develops our risk culture through
review and challenge of the first line of defence regarding
effective risk management and provides advice and
guidance.

The third line of defence is our internal Audit function,
which provides independent assurance on the overall
state of internal controls, including whether our risk
management approach and processes are designed and
operating effectively and if our processes and actions are
in line with our values and ethics.

Risk appetite

Risk appetite is defined as the amount and type of risk we
are prepared to accept in pursuit of our business and
strategic objectives. Our risk appetite is considered in
decision-making and incorporated in the Financial
Resource Plan (‘FRP’).

Risk appetite includes a Risk Appetite Statement (‘RAS')
and associated limits and metrics which are approved by
the Board and with ongoing oversight from Board Risk
Committee and Board. These risk appetite limits and
monitoring metrics are monitored by management and
executive level committees, are oversighted by the Board,
and drive management actions where early warning trends
are indicating a concern.

Risk culture

Our risk culture includes clearly identifying accountability
and ownership of specific risks and risk areas. It is
collaborative and supportive to enable open
communication of risk matters across all business units
and functions as well as ali layers of management,
underpinned by our corporate values.

An integral part of creating organisational accountability is
clear, consequential, and transparent action taken in
response to behaviours that are contrary to our desired
risk culture. Appropriate risk-taking behaviours are
encouraged and rewarded whilst inappropriate or
excessive risk-taking behaviours are challenged, and
appropriate employee action is taken dependent on the
severity of the issue. Expectations are set for individuals to
be personally accountable for the risks they take, and
protocols are established to challenge individuals’
decisions. An incentive compensation structure that
incorporates risk management actions supports effective
and prudent risk management.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the risk of financial loss if a borrower or
counterparty to a financial instrument fails to meet its
contractual obligations and arises principally from loans
and advances to clients and financial investments.

The credit profiles of our clients vary across our loan
portfolio, based on the nature of our lending to different

market segments.

Investor dependent loans

Many of our loans, particularly in our V&G portfolios, are
made to companies with modest or negative cash flows
and/or no established record of profitable operations,

primarily within the technology and life science/nealthcare
industries. Consequently, repayment of these loans is
often dependent upon receipt by our borrowers of
additional financing from venture investors and, in some
cases, a successful sale to a third party, public offering or
other form of liquidity or “exit” event. The effects of
geopolitical instability, inflationary pressures, acute
recession risks and tightening monetary policy have
resulted in a decline in certain client company valuations,
thereby reducing the rate of financing or other “exit” events
for these clients, which could have an adverse effect
impacting their ability to repay their loans to us. For
example, volatility in financial markets may make IPOs
less attractive to investors seeking an “exit” event. In such
cases, investors may provide financing in a more selective
manner, at lower levels and/or on less favourable terms,
potentially resulting in an adverse effect on our borrowers’
ability to repay their loans.

Larger loans and syndicated loans

A portion of our loan portfolio is comprised of larger loans
and represents an increasing portion of our total loans
over time. They include Sponsor Finance loans as well as
other loans made to our later-stage and larger corporate
clients, and may be made to companies with higher levels
of debt relative to their equity, balance sheet liquidity or
cash flow. Additionally, we participate in larger syndicates
agented by other financial institutions. In those
arrangements where we do not act as the lead syndicate
agent, our control or decision-making ability over the credit
facility is typically limited to our participation interest.

Loans dependent on third parties

The repayment of financing arrangements we enter into
with our clients may be dependent on the financial
condition or ability of investment funds to meet their
payment obligations to our clients. For example, we enter
into formula-based financing arrangements that are
secured by our clients’ accounts receivable from third
parties with whom they do business. We extend recurring
revenue-based lines of credit, where repayment may be
dependent on borrowers’ revenues from third parties.
These third parties may not be able to meet their financial
obligations to our clients or to us which, ultimately, could
have an adverse impact on us.

Capital call lines of credit

The majority of our loan portfolio consists of capital call
lines of credit to our Strategic Fund Services clients, the
repayment of which is dependent on the payment of
capital calls or management fees by limited partner
investors in the funds managed by these firms. These
facilities are generally governed by meaningful financial
covenants orientated towards ensuring that the funds’
remaining callable capital is sufficient to repay the loan,
and larger commitments are typically secured by an
assignment of the general partner's right to call capital
from the fund's limited partner investors.

Methodology

Four economic scenarios are used to capture the current
economic environment and to articulate management's
view of the range of risks and potential outcomes. Each
scenario is updated with the latest economic forecasts and
estimates every quarter.

Three of the scenarios, the Upside, Central and Downside,
are drawn from consensus forecasts, market data and
distributional estimates of the entire range of economic
outcomes.

The Central scenario is deemed the ‘most likely’ scenario,
and usually attracts the largest probability weighting, while
the outer scenarios represent the tails of the distribution,
which are less likely to occur. It is created using the
average of a panel of external forecasters.

Market risk

We are exposed to non-traded market risk in the form of

Interest rate risk

IRRBB is the risk of an adverse impact to earnings and
capital arising from changes in market interest rates.

Where possible we seek to match assets and liabilities
creating a natural interest rate hedge. To better ensure risk
exposure stays within risk limits residual positions that can
be hedged are transferred to our parent's Market Treasury
function via a structural hedge.

Our primary IRRBB metrics are Net Interest Income (‘NII’)
and Economic Value of Equity (‘EVE’) risk measures.

NIl at risk measures the 12-month impact to net interest
income arising from a 100 basis point parallel shift to the
yield curve. This metric captures short term sensitivity of
earnings to a change in interest rates.

EVE at risk measures the change in the net present value
of our assets and liabilities under an immediate 200 basis
point parattel shift to the yield curve. !t considers the
sensitivity of the Present Value (‘PV’) of our assets and
liabilities and hence equity, to a change in interest rates
over a longer time horizon.

Additionally, EVE sensitivity is measured against the six
PRA prescribed interest rate shack scenarios.

IRRBB metrics are monitored monthly. Board risk appetite
is set annually based on a forward view of NIl and EVE at
risk. This forward view considers the forecasted changes
in assets and liabilities balanced with perceived market
volatility.

Over the previous year, several strategic actions have
been carried out which have resulted in changes in both
the EVE and Nil sensitivities. This includes the disposal of
our fixed rate bond portfolio, the review and revision of
behavioural assumptions in respect of our non-maturing
deposits portfolio and the execution of structural hedges
that stabilise our net interest income. The combination of
these strategic actions to simplify the balance sheet, a
shortening of behavioural customer duration and the
reduction in non-maturing deposits, led to an increase in
the interest rate risk position.

2. Summary of material accounting policies
(a) Income and expense
Operating income

Interest income and expense

Interest income and expense for all financial instruments, excluding those designated at fair value, are recognised in ‘Interest
income’ and ‘Interest expense’ in the income statement using the effective interest method. Interest on credit-impaired financial
assets is recognised by applying the effective interest rate to the amortised cost (i.e. gross carrying amount of the asset less
allowance for ECL).

Non-interest income and expense

HSBC Innovation Bank Limited generates fee income from services provided over time, such as account service and card fees,
or when it delivers a specific transaction at a point in time, such as import/export services. With the exception of certain
performance fees, all other fees are generated at a fixed price. Variable fees are recognised when all uncertainties are
resolved. Fee income is generally earned from short-term contracts with payment terms that do not include a significant
financing component.

(d) Impairment of amortised cost financial assets

ECL are recognised for loans and advances to banks and clients, other financial assets held at amortised cost, and certain loan
commitments and financial guarantee contracts. At initial recognition, an allowance (or provision in the case of some loan
commitments and financial guarantees) is recognised for ECL resulting from possible default events within the next 12 months,
or less, where the remaining life is tess than 12 months ('12-month ECL’). In the event of a significant increase in credit risk, an
allowance (or provision) is recognised for ECL resulting from all possible default events over the expected life of the financial
instrument (‘lifetime ECL’). Financial assets where 12-month ECL is recognised are considered to be ‘stage 1’; financial assets
which are considered to have experienced a significant increase in credit risk are in ‘stage 2’; and financial assets for which
there is objective evidence of impairment, and so are considered to be in default or otherwise credit impaired are in ‘stage 3’.

Credit impaired (stage 3)

HSBC Innovation Bank Limited determines that a financial instrument is credit impaired and in stage 3 by considering relevant
objective evidence, primarily whether contractual payments of either principal or interest are past due for more than 90 days,
there are other indications that the borrower is unlikely to pay such as that a concession has been granted to the borrower for
economic or legal reasons relating to the borrower's financial condition, or the loan is otherwise considered to be in default.

If such unlikeliness to pay is not identified at an earlier stage, it is deemed to occur when an exposure is 90 days past due.
Therefore, the definitions of credit impaired and default are aligned as far as possible so that stage 3 represents all loans that
are considered defaulted or otherwise credit impaired.

Interest income is recognised by applying the effective interest rate to the amortised cost amount, i.e. gross carrying amount
less allowance for ECL.

Write-off

Financial assets (and the related impairment allowances) are normally written off, either partially or in full, when there is no
realistic prospect of recovery. Where loans are secured, this is generally after receipt of any proceeds from the realisation of
security. In circumstances where the net realisable value of any collateral has been determined and there is no reasonable
expectation of further recovery, write-off may be earlier.

Forbearance

Loans are identified as forborne and classified as either performing or non-performing when HSBC Innovation Bank Limited
modifies the contractual terms due to financial difficulty of the borrower. Non-performing forborne loans are stage 3 and
classified as non-performing until they meet the cure criteria, as specified by applicable credit risk policy (for example, when the
loan is no longer in default and no other indicators of default have been present for at least 12 months). Any amount written off
as a result of any modification of contractual terms upon entering forbearance would not be reversed.

Performing forborne loans are initially stage 2 and remain classified as forborne until they meet applicable cure criteria (for
example, they continue to not be in default and no other indicators of default are present for a period of at least 24 months). At
this point, the loan is either stage 1 or stage 2 as determined by comparing the risk of a default occurring at the reporting date
(based on the modified contractual terms) and the risk of a default occurring at initial recognition (based on the original,
unmodified contractual terms).

A forborne loan is derecognised if the existing agreement is cancelled and a new agreement is made on substantially different
terms, or if the terms of an existing agreement are modified such that the forborne loan is a substantially different financial
instrument.

Significant increase in credit risk (stage 2)

An assessment of whether credit risk has increased significantly since initial recognition is performed at each reporting period
by considering the change in the risk of default occurring over the remaining life of the financial instrument. The assessment
explicitly or implicitly compares the risk of default occurring at the reporting date compared with that at initial recognition, taking
into account reasonable and supportable information, including information about past events, current conditions and future
economic conditions. The assessment is unbiased, probability-weighted, and to the extent relevant, uses forward-looking
information consistent with that used in the measurement of ECL. The analysis of credit risk is multifactor. The determination of
whether a specific factor is relevant and its weight compared with other factors depends on the type of product, the
characteristics of the financial instrument and the borrower. Therefore, it is not possible to provide a single set of criteria that will
determine what is considered to be a significant increase in credit risk and these criteria will differ for different types of lending.
However, unless identified at an earlier stage, all financial assets are deemed to have suffered a significant increase in credit
risk when 30 days past due. In addition, loans that are individually assessed and included on a watch or worry list, are included
in stage 2.

Measurement of ECL

The assessment of credit risk and the estimation of ECL are unbiased and probability-weighted, and incorporate all available
information which is relevant to the assessment including information about past events, current conditions and reasonable and
supportable forecasts of future events and economic conditions at the reporting date. In addition, the estimation of ECL should
take into account the time value of money and considers other factors such as climate-related risks.

HSBC Innovation Bank Limited calculates ECL using three main components: a PD, a LGD and the EAD.

The 12-month ECL is calculated by multiplying the 12-month PD, LGD and EAD. Lifetime ECL is calculated using the lifetime
PD instead. The 12-month and lifetime PDs represent the probability of default occuring over the next 12 months and the
remaining maturity of the instrument respectively.

The EAD represents the expected balance at default, taking into account the repayment of principal and interest from the
balance sheet date to the default event together with any expected drawdowns of committed facilities. The LGD represents
expected losses on the EAD given the event of default, taking into account, among other attributes, the mitigating effect of
collateral value at the time it is expected to be realised and the time value of money.

Period over which ECL is measured

Expected credit loss is measured from the initial recognition of the financial asset. The maximum period considered when
measuring ECL (be it 12-month or lifetime ECL) is the maximum contractual period over which HSBC Innovation Bank Limited
is exposed to credit risk. However, where the financial instrument includes both a drawn and undrawn commitment and the
contractual ability to demand repayment and cancel the undrawn commitment does not serve to limit HS8C Innovation Bank
Limited's exposure to credit risk to the contractual notice period, the contractual period does not determine the maximum period
considered. Instead, ECL is measured over the period that HSBC Innovation Bank Limited remains exposed to credit risk that is
not mitigated by credit risk management actions. This applies to credit cards, where the period is the average time taken for
stage 2 exposures to default or close as performing accounts, determined on a portfolio basis and ranging from between two
and six years. In addition, for these facilities it is not possible to identify the ECL on the loan commitment component separately
from the financial asset component. As a result, the total ECL is recognised in the loss allowance for the financial asset unless
the total ECL exceeds the gross carrying amount of the financial asset, in which case the ECL is recognised as a provision. For
overdraft facilities, credit risk management actions are taken no less frequently than on an annual basis.

to calculate unbiased expected credit loss in most economic environments. In certain economic environments, additional
analysis may be necessary and may result in additional scenarios or adjustments, to reflect a range of possible economic
outcomes sufficient for an unbiased estimate. The detailed methodology is disclosed in ‘Measurement uncertainty and
sensitivity analysis of ECL estimates’ on page 12.

Valuation

Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market
participants at the measurement date. It does not reflect the economic benefits and costs we expect to flow from an
instrument's cash flow over its expected future life. Our valuation methodologies and assumptions in determining fair values for
which no observable market prices are available may differ from other companies.

Loans and advances to banks and clients

Fair value is based on observable market transactions, when available. When they are unavailable, fair value are estimated
using valuations model incorporating a range of inputs assumptions. These assumptions may include: forward-looking
discounted cash flow madels, taking account of expected client repayment rates, using assumptions that HSBC Innovation
Bank Limited believes are consistent with those that would be used by market participants in valuing such loans; and new
business rates for similar loans.

The fair value of loans reflects expected credit losses at the balance sheet date and the fair value effect of repricing between
origination and the balance sheet date. For credit impaired loans, fair value is estimated by discounting the future cash flows
over the time period they are expected to be recovered.

Contingent liabilities

HSBC Innovation Bank Limited has financial guarantees contracts in issue that would require the Bank to make specified
payments to reimburse the holder for a loss incurred because a specified debtor fails to make payment when due, in
accordance with the original or modified terms of a debt instrument. The maximum exposure represents amounts at risk should

expire without being drawn upon, the total of the nominal principat amounts is not indicative of future liquidity. The maximum
exposure to financial guarantees is £35,224k (2022: £3,200k).

Legal Proceedings

HSBC Innovation Bank Limited is party to legal proceedings and regulatory matters arising out of its normal business
operations.

In May 2023, First-Citizens Bank & Trust Company (‘First Citizens’) brought a lawsuit in the US District Court for the Northern
District of California against HSBC UK and HSBC Innovation Bank Limited, certain other HSBC companies and seven US-
based HSBC Group employees who had previously worked for SVB. The lawsuit seeks $1bn in damages and alleges, among
other things, that the HSBC companies conspired with the individual defendants to solicit employees from First Citizens and
that the individual defendants took confidential information belonging to SVB and/or First Citizens. In January 2024, the court
denied the defendants’ motion to dismiss in part and granted it in part, and directed the plaintiff to amend its complaint to
specify its allegations as to each defendant. In February 2024, First Citizens filed its amended complaint. This action is ongoing.

Based on the facts currently known, it is not practicable at this time to predict the resolution of this matter, including the timing
or any possible impact on HSBC Innovation Bank Limited, which could be significant.
