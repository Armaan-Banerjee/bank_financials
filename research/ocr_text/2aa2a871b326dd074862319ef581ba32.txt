Business review of 2021

Brown Shipley has performed well in 2021 as the continuing impact of COVID-19 was felt by all our employees,
clients and across the wider business environment. The current year has seen us continue to adapt our ways
of working and communicating, as such many of the approaches developed in the early stages of the
pandemic are now seen as part of our longer term strategy for delivering a good service to existing clients and
growing the business now and in the future.

Financial markets have been more positive in 2021, despite the continuing Coronavirus concerns, coupled
with supply side issues and the inflationary pressures which emerged during the year. Stock markets around
the world showed strong gains during the year with the MSCI World index of equities up 20% in the year,
whilst the UK market experienced its strongest performance since 2016 finishing the year 14% up on 2020.

Against this backdrop Brown Shipley has also seen client portfolios deliver strong absolute performance
across our five core strategies, and whilst performance was mixed over 1 year against competitors it remains
strong over 3 and 5 years. The year has also seen Brown Shipley take an active role in further developments
of the Quintet Group’s sustainable investment strategy as we have partnered with industry leaders such as
Amundi, DWS, and Robeco to create innovative solutions previously not available to private investors and
more recently with the launch of Quintet Earth the world’s first multi asset climate neutral fund. These
developments underpin a key aim of the Quintet Group and Brown Shipley to make sustainable investing our
default strategy for the majority of our clients.

Net operating income

Total net operating income increased from £79.1m to £90.9m (+14.9%). This includes income of £3.6m from
the sale of our pension administration activities in January 2021 and transfer pricing income of £8.3m from
the recharge to the parent of staff employed by BSCO but undertaking Quintet Group roles. The latter
revenues are recognised for the first time in 2021 following the decision that as part of the implementation of
a full transfer pricing policy, such staff in the UK would be employed by BSCO and would be recharged to the
parent company with a cost mark up of between 5% and 15%, dependent on the service provided. Both 2021
and the prior year have seen downward adjustments to deferred acquisition costs on acquisition of £3.0m
(2020: £3.6m), which under IFRS, has to be reported in the net operating income line, whilst the prior year
also saw dividend income of £1.1m from NWB and proceeds from the sale of the non-core employee benefits
division of £0.6m.

Fee and Commission income increased from £61.1m to £63.2m (3.4%) as asset based fees benefited from
stronger markets during the year. This was partially offset by a reduction in Pension fee revenue following the
sale of the majority of the activities in this line at the start of the year.

The banking element of the business continues to grow in line with the Company’s strategy of offering
complimentary banking services to wealth management clients. Customer deposits showed a further 19%
(2020: 19%) increase to £971m and growth of the customer loan book continued with an additional £72m of
net new lending taking the drawn book to £566m. The Company continues to invest its clients’ deposits very
cautiously. The loan to deposit ratio ended the year at just under 60%, with the remaining deposits invested
either at the Bank of England, in a portfolio of high quality liquid assets or in a limited amount of short-term
unsecured lending to highly rated banks. The Company currently lends a maximum of 60% of its customer
deposits and if lending growth is constrained by that limit, it has in place a funding line from its parent to grow
its loan book further.

The Bank of England decision in March 2020, in response to the pandemic, to reduce the UK Base Rate to
just 0.10% has impacted negatively on interest margins in 2020 and 2021 despite this balance sheet growth.
Spreads were compressed because of this and margins ended down £1.3m (almost 13%), although the
decision to increase the rate to 0.25% in December 2021, coupled with the further 0.25% rises in February
and March 2022 to 0.75% will see this reverse as we go forward. We have successfully transitioned from
LIBOR over the last 18 months in line with regulatory requirements, although LIBOR was not a reference rate
widely used by the Company.

Costs

In headline terms costs rose from £76.5m to £85.5m. However, much like the increase in net operating income
there are also a number of one-off costs included that contribute materially to this increase, such as the
inclusion of staff costs related to employees recharged to the Group, following the implementation of the full
transfer pricing policy. That implementation has also resulted in a further increase in the transfer pricing
expense charged to Brown Shipley from its parent company, Quintet Private Bank (Europe) S.A. (“Quintet”)
from £2.5m to £6.5m, to fully reflect the services received by Brown Shipley and its share of any group-wide
project costs.

In addition, in the prior year there were also a number of one-off items, including project costs in relation to
migrating the NWB business onto the Company’s IT platform and the restructuring of the business and front
office target operating model amounting to £3.9m.

There was also a revision to the estimated deferred consideration payable on The Roberts Partnership (“TRP”)
of £2.3m in 2020 as outlined in Note 26. This is an item that under accounting standards has to be reflected
as a cost. These costs are included within general administrative expenses.

The Company also made a provision for the defined benefit pension transfer redress plus associated legal
and professional fees of £5.6m (2020: £2.1m), net of expected PI Insurance recoveries of £3.1m (2020:
£0.8m). See Note 28 and Note 19 for further details.

Average staff numbers increased by 6 during the year. This is only due to the inclusion of 39 Group staff as
discussed above. Excluding the Group staff average FTE's fell from 380 to 347 on a like for like basis partly
due to the departure of 16 employees on 4 January 2021 following the sale of our Pension administration
activities to Dentons.

Brown Shipley’s services

Brown Shipley’s vision is to be recognised as a trusted partner to its clients and as a leading private bank by
providing integrated wealth management solutions to those clients. Brown Shipley’s mission is to preserve
and grow each client's wealth across generations. The Company aims to increase its client base and funds
under management through organic growth, marketing, intermediary development and acquisitions.

Brown Shipley continues to provide wealth management services comprising investment management,
financial planning and pension advice and banking services to its client base, which mainly consists of private
individuals.

Managing clients’ funds on a discretionary basis is at the heart of the Company's offering. Having full discretion
with securities held by our nominee and cash held by us as an authorised bank, allows our investment
managers maximum flexibility and enables us to provide quality services efficiently and at a reasonable cost.
Increasingly the service provided to clients is that of Wealth Management, a combination of Investment
Management along with ongoing Wealth Planning. These services enable the Company to provide clients
with advice on all aspects of their financial affairs. The Company also provides advisory management and
execution only investment management services.

Brown Shipley is an authorised bank providing a range of banking services to its clients. Current accounts
and a charge card are offered alongside term deposit facilities. In recent years the Company has focused on
developing its lending business as a complementary service to wealth management. Loans are only offered
to enhance the service provided to existing customers or to establish new private banking relationships. Loans
are made on a secured basis, for a maximum of five years and at conservative loan to value ratios. Security
held for loans comprises mainly investment management portfolios and UK residential property assets.

Clients — Our clients are at the centre of everything we do, given our role as trusted adviser to them. We
communicate in many ways, meeting face to face, through quarterly valuations, by email, phone and post,
through our website and our client portal. Members of the Board meet with clients at organised events and
we run client surveys and round tables periodically to learn what we do well and what we could improve on.

Different ways of communicating have developed since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic and we are
pleased that regular contact has been made easy by channels such as Microsoft Teams and Zoom and by
the increasing take up of electronic and paperless services by clients. We are also active members of trade
bodies, PIMFA and UK Finance and this enables us to be fully aware of any emerging client considerations.

We know that Sustainability and Climate Change are key issues for clients and whilst we are already
signatories to the UN Principles for Responsible Investment (“PRI”) and a member of the Climate Action 100+
Initiative, we are, in conjunction with Quintet, changing our investment approach to put this at the forefront of
our activities, as described further in the Directors’ Report.

Colleagues — It is critical that employees are connected directly to our strategy and aspirations as a Bank.
Management regularly engages with colleagues in a number of ways, including Chief Executive Officer
("CEO") round tables, regular business updates, townhalls, social events (COVID-19 permitted) and monthly
line manager calls. In May 2021, Brown Shipley employees participated in a Quintet wide employee
engagement survey (participation across Brown Shipley was 65% with 692 free text comments received).
Following this the Brown Shipley Executive Committee ("ExCo”) members undertook deep dives with their
teams to obtain deeper insights into the results. In parallel, Brown Shipley employees participated in focus
groups held across Quintet to provide further insight and proposals for action to increase engagement. A
number of actions were implemented to address some of the feedback e.g. quarterly updates delivered by the
CEO and ExCo team members which inform on performance, project activity and other key areas of focus for
the Company.

In addition the Company operates a Whistle Blowing policy which aims to increase staff awareness of being
able to speak out about bad practice within Brown Shipley or report potential wrongdoing without the fear of
any reprisals.

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic we have seen almost all colleagues working from home for extended periods
of 2020 and 2021. We have therefore heightened our communications to ensure colleagues feel connected,
making use of all technology available. Where requested, we have provided colleagues with appropriate
equipment such as laptops, screens and ergonomic chairs to ensure that their work from home environment
is as comfortable as possible. We have also introduced ‘Well-being Wednesdays’, where we encourage all
colleagues to take an hour out of their working day each Wednesday to switch off from work and do something
for themselves, be that a cycle ride, a run, walking the dog, reading a book or whatever else they choose!

Our goal is to support employees to take ownership of their own development. In 2021, we provided access
to the e-content library from LinkedIn Learning and going forward we will expand and increase the diversity of
our online education offerings. In addition, a number of apprenticeship courses were launched across Brown
Shipley as part of the “Next Gen2" programme. This was established as part of our commitment to support
colleagues’ personal development and lifelong learning as part of the Government Apprenticeship Scheme.
The programme offers all colleagues the opportunity to study towards industry-recognised professional
qualifications, while continuing to work in their usual role. These included Leadership Management, Coaching
and Management, Excelling in Operational Management and Data Analytics.

We are committed to ensuring we attract, develop and engage a diverse employee base. We are signatories
of the Women in Finance Charter and have agreed a new target of 40% of women in senior leadership
positions by December 2023, having already met our initial target of 25% by December 2020. Currently we
are on track to meet this revised target, having reached 32% as of December 2021. We also continue to
develop our Diversity & Inclusion ("D&l”) strategy. During 2021, D&l working groups focusing on
gender/LGBTQ+, social mobility and ethnicity were established to identify priorities and activities to increase
diversity and inclusion. In order to continue to address our gender pay gap, we will continue to focus on
initiatives to increase the recruitment, retention and promotion of women.

Outlook

In the past two years the focus in this part of my report has been around the uncertainties brought about by
COVID-19. This year uncertainty remains, but it is now geo-political in the form of the terrible events in the
Ukraine. Whilst our direct exposure is limited, it is unclear how long this situation will last or what a solution
will look like and how it will impact on our business, or the UK and global economy. The business continues
to assess the situation and its impact, as it must, and further comments are made in the going concern note
in the Directors’ Report, but it is safe to say, our thoughts are with the people of the Ukraine at this time.

The business is well positioned going forward having made a number of management changes and strategic
hires in early 2022 with a focus on further growth of AUM and lending.

pay versus fixed compensation (1:1). Subject to shareholder approval, the applicable bonus cap threshold
can be increased (2:1).

The Company also recognises that within the industry it is normal practice for client facing managers to receive
a higher element of variable remuneration than non-income producing managers and in some circumstances

the bonus vests immediately and then 40% is deferred equally over the next 3 years (in all cases each vested
amount is 50% in cash and 50% in phantom equity shares in Quintet which can be retained). Where an MRT
in not on the firm’s Executive Committee a de minimis of £100,000 applies before deferral is triggered. The
phantom equity shares are aligned to the net asset value of Quintet and therefore serve as a proxy for group
performance. Variable compensation awards of less than the de-minimus limit are included within the
standard variable compensation scheme for all other employees.

Risk Philosophy and Risk Management Framework

The philosophy of the Company is that all members of staff have a responsibility for risk. The Board carries
the ultimate responsibility, as stated in the Enterprise Risk Management Framework, and is supported by the
Risk Control function. The Framework also describes the “Three Lines of Defence” model operated by the
Company and the responsibilities at the various levels within the organisation:

¢ The first line of defence consists of business line management and staff who are responsible for the risks
inherent in their business area activities and who, supported by the first line Business Risk Management
(BRM) function, ensure that appropriate systems and controls are in place to effectively mitigate the day-
to-day risks in the business. They are also responsible for compliance with policies and procedures and
for the escalation of issues to senior management and risk governance functions, where relevant.

e The second line of defence is provided by the Company's Risk Control and Compliance functions. These
functions set the overarching risk and compliance frameworks, monitor adherence to the firm’s approved
risk appetite, and are responsible for providing support and challenge to the first line in their risk
management activities.

e The third line of defence is the independent assurance provided by Internal Audit.

Risk Appetite

Risk appetite defines the amount and type of risk, both financial and non-financial, that the Company is able
and willing to accept in the pursuit of its business objectives and strategies. The overall objective is to protect
the Company from unacceptable levels of risk, while supporting and enabling the firm’s overall business
strategy (including the assessment of new business opportunities). The Company’s core franchise is as a
wealth manager, and consequently, the Company adopts a conservative appetite for non-strategic risk, to
ensure high confidence that it can survive any potential threats in both normal and stressed conditions.

‘Brown Shipley has developed individual risk appetite statements for all of the Company's key risks. The
individual risk appetites define the risk boundaries for the Company's services and operating parameters.
These risk appetites are typically articulated via Key Risk Indicator (KRI) metrics. These metrics have risk
appetite limits and triggers for management action to address any causes of heightened risk that threaten the
stated appetite.

By setting explicit thresholds for the key risk areas the Board can clearly document what level of risk it is
prepared to accept in normal business activities, and conversely, what level of risk is unacceptable. The setting
of both a timit and trigger provides more granular guidance for management, with the limit being a hard stop
but the trigger being an early warning notification prompting corrective action.

Risk Control Function

Overseen by the CRO, the Risk Control function comprises of Financial, Non-Financial and Credit Risk teams.
The Risk Control teams provide advice and challenge to the first line business areas and monitor risks,
providing regular independent reporting to the ERC and BRCLC, including adherence to approved risk
appetite.

The Risk Control function has a close working relationship with Finance, especially through the management
and oversight of financial risks. The teams work closely together in the production and review of the annual
capital and liquidity regulatory assessment processes. This is also illustrated in the Asset and Liability
Management Policy, which describes the responsibilities at a detailed level and the escalation processes to
the Asset and Liability Committee.

Non-financial risks

Regulatory risk

The banking and financial services sector in which we operate is heavily regulated. Failure to comply with

material risk, which is likely to increase with the higher profile of the Company as it grows. There is also a risk

financial crime controls and monitor proposed changes to regulation internally and through industry bodies,
to assess the impact any changes may have on our business and always ensure we have sufficient resources
to meet the requirements.

People risk

The Company is a people focused business and so we are exposed to risks relating to the attraction and
retention of appropriately skilled colleagues to deliver our business objectives. Risks have evolved in the
period with a greater focus on flexible working and employee wellbeing, accelerated by experiences in the
pandemic, and enhanced regulatory focus on workplace diversity and inclusion. The Company mitigates
these risks by seeking to maintain competitive remuneration packages, with long term incentives to align key
colleagues with the values and success of the Company, supported by clear HR policies. The Company has
actively supported colleague engagement and continues to promote diversity and inclusion initiatives.

Technology risk

The continuing delivery of high quality services to clients is to a large extent dependent on a robust and flexible
IT infrastructure. Failure of an [T strategy or implementation would have an adverse impact on the business.
Monitoring, improving and supplementing existing systems is a high priority. Additionally the Company keeps
abreast of cyber-crime and information security developments and works closely with its parent company
Quintet, which provides core system and network infrastructure to the Company.

Outsourcing risk

A key activity or service contracted out by us to a third party will be defined as outsourced where it is deemed
to be critical or important to the performance of the Company’s business. Outsourcing risk is the risk of third
party or supplier failure on a scale that can cause disruption, financial costs and reputational damage. The
Company outsources elements of its day to day operational activities to various third parties, including its
parent company Quintet. All material outsourcing relationships are closely monitored, with formal oversight
structures in place, and are subject to an annual due diligence process.

Operational risk

Operational risk is the risk of direct or indirect losses resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes,
people and systems, or from external events. This includes fraud risk, legal risk, business continuity risk and
data security risks. It also includes the risks associated with the outsourcing of critical activities and in
particular IT and Operational Hub services through our parent company Quintet and the Group’s outsourcing
partner Lombard Odier.

The Company actively manages these risks through the implementation of sound policies and robust first line
procedures and controls, supported by a strong risk culture and oversight from the second line of defence.

Group Risk

As part of a European banking group, the Company is exposed to risks relating to its relationships with other
entities within the Quintet group. These risks are managed through transparency of reporting and the
engagement of ExCo and senior colleagues in Quintet management and project committees. Formal
intragroup services agreements, with associated SLAs and structured oversight models, are in place for
services provided by other group entities.

Energy efficiency actions
Brown Shipley are committed to responsible energy management and will practice energy efficiency
throughout the organisation. The Company recognises that climate change is one of the most serious
environmental challenges currently threatening the global community and it has a role to play in reducing
greenhouse gas emissions.

In September 2016, the group entered into a partnership with Egencia, a business travel solutions provider.
Consequently, we track, trace and seek to reduce our carbon footprint. At the same time, our policy is to
favour and actively promote business-related travel by public transportation. Despite the ongoing Covid
related travel restrictions during the year our flight carbon footprint by CO2 emissions increased by 32% from
10,448 kg in 2020 to 42,890kg in 2021, as compared to 108,315 kg in 2019. Whilst it is clear that business
travel patterns will continue to change once all restrictions are lifted, Brown Shipley will continue to encourage
the use of virtual meetings both with clients and internally and to encourage the use of lower carbon methods
of travel wherever possible. :

In addition as current leases come up for renewal, energy efficiency will be a key criteria in the decision to
extend a lease or relocate to another location. Hence in Nottingham, the Company moved to a building that
utilizes a Photovoltaic (solar power) array on the roof to reduce the requirement to source electricity from the
national grid, whilst in 2021 the move to the new Manchester office enabled the Company to move to a Building
Research Establishment’s Environmental Assessment Method (“BREEAM’) Certified building. This will enable
Brown Shipley to track and monitor the buildings environmental, social and economic sustainability
performance using an internationally recognized standard. BREEAM certification means Brown Shipley are
in a more sustainable environment that enhances the well-being of our people who work there and help protect
natural resources by responsibly sourcing partners and suppliers in a coordinated manner with the landlord.
The move to new premises in June 2021 also demonstrated a positive environmental impact in terms of
energy use, thus providing a saving in cost and carbon emissions though lower use.

The company also has a long-term commitment to reduce paper usage; in line with best practices, printers
are preconfigured to print in black and white and recto-verso, reducing paper and ink usage. We only use
FSC-certified paper, ensuring that the wood within the product is from FSC-certified material, recycled material
or controlled wood. The group has also developed and established a long-term internal awareness campaign
to reduce unnecessary and/or excessive printing. As part of this campaign the Company has continued to
encourage clients to utilise the secure online platform “My Brown Shipley” and by November 2021 36% (2020:
31%) of clients were registered on the platform and 30% (2020: 27%) receiving all documents digitally.

Our application of materiality
We apply the concept of materiality in planning and performing the audit, in evaluating the effect of identified
misstatements on the audit and in forming our audit opinion.

Materiality

The magnitude of an omission or misstatement that, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be
expected to influence the economic decisions of the users of the financial statements. Materiality provides a
basis for determining the nature and extent of our audit procedures.

We determined materiality for the Company to be £0.75m (2020: £0.33m), which is 1% of revenue (2020:
5% of profit before tax, excluding one-off iterns). We believe that revenue provides us with the most
important financial metric on which shareholders would judge the performance of the Company, and
accordingly this is an appropriate metric to determine materiality.

We have changed the basis of materiality for the 2021 audit because many of the Company's key
performance indicators revolve around net new money and FUM, along with revenue growth. We consider
the revenues of the Company to be the primary focus of the users of the financial statements.

During the course of our audit, we reassessed initial materiality and concluded that no adjustment was
deemed necessary.

Performance materiality

The application of materiality at the individual account or balance level. It is set at an amount to reduce to
an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements
exceeds mateniality.

On the basis of our risk assessments, together with our assessment of the Company's overall control
environment, our judgement was that performance materiality was 75% (2020: 75%) of our planning
materiality, namely £0.56m (2020: £0.25m). We have set performance materiality at this percentage based
on our understanding of the control environment that indicates a lower risk of material misstatements, both
corrected and uncorrected.

Reporting threshold

An amount below which identified misstatements are considered as being clearly trivial.

We agreed with the Board Audit Committee that we would report to them all uncorrected audit differences in
excess of £0.04m (2020: £0.02m), which is set at 5% of planning materiality, as well as differences below
that threshold that, in our view, warranted reporting on qualitative grounds.

We evaluate any uncorrected misstatements against both the quantitative measures of materiality discussed
above and in light of other relevant qualitative considerations in forming our opinion.

1 Statement of Accounting Policies

a. Basis of Preparation
These financial statements have been prepared in accordance with UK-adopted International Accounting
Standards (‘IFRS’).

The Company has taken advantage of the exemption under IFRS 10 (Paragraph 4(a)) and has not prepared
consolidated financial statements. The Company therefore accounts for its Investment in Subsidiaries in its
own statement of financial position at their cost less impairment.

The financial statements have been prepared under the historical cost convention, except for certain financial
assets held at fair value.

The accounting policies set out below which have been adopted are consistent with those used in the previous
financial year.

Going Concern

The Directors have made an assessment of the Company's ability to continue as a going concern and are
satisfied that the Company has the resources to continue in business for at least 12 months from the issuance
of these financial statements. This assessment has fully evaluated the situation in the Ukraine, any continuing
impact of COVID-19 and other potential risks on the business. Furthermore, management is not aware of any
material uncertainties that may cast significant doubt upon the Company's ability to continue as a going
concern. Therefore, the financial statements continue to be prepared on the going concern basis.

Our annual ICAAP uses a stressed market scenario of greater magnitude than the one we have been through
during the COVID-19 pandemic and shows that the Company is able to survive this severe stress by taking
credible management actions. The ICAAP takes the three year business plan which covers the 36 months
from the date of this report and models the impact of a severe but plausible stress. In addition the Company
undertakes reverse stress testing and detailed regular reforecasting of its business plan and has modelled a
number of different adverse scenarios of increasing severity over the next 12 months which again it is able to
survive. Many different variables are stressed in these scenarios, the key one being the level of stock markets
which impacts on both AUM and depositor-outflows.

b. Provisions

Provisions are set aside for present obligations which arise from past events, where it is expected that a
payment will be required to settle the obligation and the amount of the provision can be reliably estimated.
Provisions ‘are charged to the profit and loss account and are not discounted where not material.

Full details of provisions are shown in Note 28.

c. Foreign currencies

Monetary assets and liabilities denominated in foreign currencies have been translated at the rates of
exchange prevailing at the statement of financial position date. Income and expenditure in foreign currencies
have been translated at rates of exchange prevailing at transaction dates. Foreign exchange gains and losses
are recognised in the income statement. The functional and presentational currency of these financial
statements is in Pounds Sterling.

d. Significant accounting judgements and estimates

In the process of applying the Company's accounting policies, management has used its judgement and made
estimates in determining the amounts recognised in the financial statements. The most significant use of
judgement and estimates are as follows:

Fair value of financial instruments

Where the fair values of financial assets and financial liabilities recorded on the statement of financial position
cannot be derived from active markets, such as for the company's holding of Visa shares at FVPL and
derivatives held for economic hedging purposes, they are determined using a variety of valuation techniques
that include the use of mathematical models. The input to these models is taken from observable markets
where possible, but, where this is not feasible, a degree of judgement is required in establishing fair values.

f. Derecognition of financial assets and financial liabilities

Financial assets are derecognised when the rights to receive cash flows from the asset have expired, or the
Company has transferred its rights to receive cash flows from the asset or has assumed an obligation to pay
the received cash flows in full without material delay to a third party under a ‘pass-through’ arrangement, and
the Company has transferred substantially all the risks and rewards of the asset. The asset is recognised to
the extent of the Company’s continuing involvement in the asset. Continuing involvement that takes the form
of a guarantee over the transferred asset is measured at the lower of the original carrying amount and the
maximum amount of consideration that the Company could be required to repay.

Financial liabilities are derecognised when the obligation under the liability is discharged or cancelled or
expires. When the financial liability is replaced by another from the same lender on substantially different
terms, or the terms of an existing liability are substantially modified, such an exchange or modification is
treated as a derecognition of the original liability and the recognition of a new liability, the difference in the
respective carrying amounts is recognised in the income statement.

g. Determination of fair value

The fair value for financial instruments traded in active markets at the statement of financial position date is
based on their quoted market price or dealer price quotations (bid price for long positions and offer price for
short positions), without any deduction for transaction costs.

For all other financial instruments not listed in an active market, the fair value is determined by using
appropriate valuation techniques. Valuation techniques include net present value techniques, comparison to
similar instruments for which market observable prices exist, options pricing models and other relevant
valuation models.

i. Impairment of financial assets

Under IFRS 9 the Company’s loan loss impairment method uses a forward-looking ECL approach. The
Company has been recording the allowance for expected credit losses for all loans and other debt financial
assets not held at FVPL, together with loan commitments and financial guarantee contracts, in this section all
referred to as ‘financial instruments’.

The ECL allowance is based on the credit losses expected to arise over the life of the asset (the lifetime
expected credit loss or LTECL), unless there has been no significant increase in credit risk since origination,
in which case, the allowance is based on the 12 months’ expected credit loss (12mMECL) as outlined below.
The Company’s policies for determining if there has been a significant increase in credit risk are set out in
Note 37 (Risk management)

The 12mECL is the portion of LTECLs that represent the ECLs that result from default events on a financial
instrument that are possible within the 12 months after the reporting date.

Both LTECLs and 12mECLs are calculated on either an individual basis or a collective basis, depending on
the nature of the underlying portfolio of financial instruments.

The Company performs an assessment, at the end of each reporting period, of whether a financial
instrument's credit risk has increased significantly since initial recognition, by considering the change in the
risk of default occurring over the remaining life of the financial instrument. This is further explained in Note 38.

Based on the above process, the Company groups its loans into Stage 1, Stage 2 and Stage 3, as described
below:

- §tage 1: When loans are first recognised they are categorised as Stage 1 and the Company recognises
an allowance based on 12mECLs. Stage 1 loans can also include facilities where the credit risk has
improved and the loan has been reclassified from Stage 2.

- Stage 2: When a loan has shown a significant increase in credit risk since origination, the Company
records an allowance for the LTECLs. Stage 2 loans can also include facilities, where the credit risk has
improved and the loan has been reclassified from Stage 3.

- Stage 3: If a loan is subsequently considered credit-impaired (as outlined in Note 37). The Company
records an allowance for the LTECLs.

To assess whether the credit risk of a financial instrument has increased significantly, a combination of
quantitative and qualitative factors is considered. These include the analysis of the term structure of the
probability of default as well as watchlist surveillance based on reasonable and supportable information. The
credit risk assessment is symmetrical in nature, allowing financial assets to move back to stage 1 if the
variation of credit risk is no longer considered a significant increase or if the financial assets have been
forborne (to be assessed case by case).

The period for which assets remain in Stage 2 before being transferred back to Stage 1 depends on whether
an exposure was Classified as ‘unlikely to pay’ or ‘forborne’. If it is neither then the cure is considered
immediate, otherwise there is a one year probation period.

m. Property and equipment
The cost of an item of property and equipment shall be recognised as an asset only if it is probable that future
economic benefits associated with the item will flow to the Company and the cost can be measured reliably.

Property and equipment are recognised at cost less accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment
losses. Depreciation is charged on such assets so as to write off the cost of assets over their estimated useful
lives, using the straight-line method. In practice, leasehold improvements are depreciated over the remaining
life of the relevant lease, office equipment and computer hardware is depreciated over two to five years and
right of use (leased) assets are depreciated over the remaining lease term applicable to each property.

At each year end, the Company reviews the carrying amount of its tangible assets. Impairment is recognised
if the carrying value of an asset exceeds the recoverable value (the higher of the value in use or the net selling
value). Impairment is recognised in the income statement.

Gains and losses on disposal are determined by comparing the proceeds on sale with the carrying amount of
the asset, and are recognised in the income statement.

n. Goodwill

Purchased goodwill represents the excess of the cost of an acquisition over the Company's interest in the fair
value of identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed. Goodwill is capitalised at cost and reviewed for
impairment at least annually. Any impairment is recognised immediately in the income statement and ts not
subsequently reversed.

oO. Intangible assets
Intangible assets represent either computer software or acquired customer relationships. These are
recognised at cost less accumulated amortisation and accumulated impairment losses.

An intangible asset relating to the configuration or customisation of software provided to the company under
a Software as a Service (SaaS) arrangement will only be capitalised in the event that the company has the
right to obtain all the future economic benefits from the asset and to direct the use of the asset.

Amortisation is calculated using the straight line method to write down the cost over the estimated useful life
of the asset. Computer software is amortised over two to five years. Customer relationships are amortised
over ten to fifteen years depending on their nature.

At each year end, the Company reviews the carrying amount of its intangible assets. Impairment is recognised
if the carrying value of an asset exceeds the recoverable value (the higher of the value in use or the net selling
value). In assessing the value in use of an asset, the present value of future cash flows is assessed over the
remaining useful economic life of the asset. Any impairment is recognised in the income statement.

Gains and losses on disposal are determined by comparing the proceeds on sale with the carrying amount of
the asset, and are recognised in the income statement.

p. Taxation

Current tax assets and liabilities for the current and prior years are measured at the amounts expected to be
recovered from or paid to the taxation authorities. The tax rates and tax laws used to compute the amount are
those that are enacted or substantively enacted at the statement of financial position date.

Deferred tax is provided on temporary differences at the statement of financial position date between the tax
bases of assets and liabilities and their carrying amounts for financial reporting purposes.

Deferred tax liabilities are recognised for all taxable temporary differences, except where the deferred tax
liability arises from the initial recognition of goodwill or from an asset or liability in a transaction that is not a
business combination and, at the time of the transaction, affects neither the accounting profit nor taxable profit
or loss and, when temporary differences are associated with investments in subsidiaries and associates,
where the timing of the reversal of the temporary differences can be controlled and it is probable that the
temporary differences will not reverse in the foreseeable future.

Deferred tax assets are recognised for all temporary differences, carry forward of unused tax credits and
unused tax losses, to the extent that it is probable that taxable profit will be available against which the
deductible temporary differences, and the carry forward of unused tax credits and unused tax losses can be
utilised. The only exception is where the deferred tax asset relating to the deductible temporary differences
arises from the initial recognition of an asset or liability in a transaction that is not a business combination and,
at the time of the transaction, affects neither the accounting profit nor taxable profit or loss and, when
temporary differences are associated with investments in subsidiaries and associates.

The carrying amount of deferred tax assets is reviewed at each year end and reduced to the extent that it is
no longer probable that sufficient taxable profit will be available to allow all or part of the deferred tax asset to
be utilised. Unrecognised deferred tax assets are reassessed at each year end and are recognised to the
extent that it has become probable that future taxable profit will allow the deferred tax asset to be recovered.

q. Income recognition
Revenue is measured at the fair value of the consideration received or receivable and represents amounts
receivable in the normal course of business net of value added tax.

Under IFRS 9, Interest income and expense is recognised in the income statement for all instruments
measured at amortised cost. Interest is calculated using the effective interest method. The effective interest
rate is the rate that exactly discounts estimated future cash flows through the expected life of the financial
instrument to its net carrying amount. When calculating an effective interest rate, the Company estimates the
future cash flows considering all contractual terms of the financial instrument but does not consider future
credit losses. The calculation includes all fees and commissions paid or received between the parties to the
contract that are an integral part of the effective interest rate, transaction costs and all other premiums or
discounts.

Recurring fees and commissions related to investment management, fund management, wealth management
and pensions are recognised on an accruals basis when the service has been provided, i.e. over the time the
ongoing service was provided.

e Fees charged to clients for investment management services are billed quarterly in arrears based on
the agreed tariff per the client's contract. This tariff is multiplied by the client's average FUM.

e Fund management fees are based on an annual management charge (AMC) levied on a daily basis
across the five Brown Shipley Funds and paid to the Company on a monthly basis by Smith &
Williamson who are the Authorised Corporate Director of the funds.

« Wealth planning fees are charged on the same basis as investment management fees if the assets
of the client are on the Brown Shipley Platform based on an agreed Wealth planning tariff. The
Company also receives ongoing adviser charges from client’s FUM held on external platforms which
are received monthly in arrears from most providers. This income is also linked to the value of the

e Pension’s advisory fees for former pension administration clients are charged six monthly in advance

r. Pensions and other employee benefits

The Company operates a defined contribution scheme for employees, which most employees are members
of. In a number of instances it makes contributions to employees’ self-invested personal pensions. In addition,
it has a legacy defined benefit scheme which was closed to the future accrual of benefits in 2005.

Defined contribution schemes - a defined contribution scheme is one under which the Company pays a fixed
contribution into a separate entity, usually a privately administered pension insurance plan. The Company has
no legal or constructive obligations to pay further contributions to such schemes under any circumstance.
Contributions are recognised as an employee benefit expense when they are due.

Defined benefit scheme - a defined benefit scheme is one that defines an amount of pension benefit that an
employee will receive on retirement, usually dependent on one or more factors such as age, years of service
and salary. The pension expenses and obligations are calculated according to IAS19. Actuarial gains and
losses are recognised by the Company in full through the statement of comprehensive income. The deficit
recognised in the statement of financial position is the present value of the defined benefit obligation at the
yearend less the fair value of the scheme assets. The present value of the defined benefit obligation is
calculated using the Projected Unit Credit Method with actuarial calculations carried out on an annual basis.

Under IAS 19, other short-term employee benefits (to be settled within 12 months) are recognised when the
employee has rendered the service and are measured at the undiscounted amount of benefits expected to be
paid in exchange for that service. This applies to the employee's salary and associated employer social
security costs, other benefits provided by the company such as the annual discretionary bonus, life insurance,
private health and medical insurance.

Long term employee benefits arrangements have conditions attached to secure their vesting. This typically
applies to employee bonus schemes not wholly settled within 12 months after the period in which the employee
renders the related service. The Company offers several types of bonus schemes that fall into the category
of long term benefits and recognises these benefits over the vesting period of the bonus scheme. For the
Retention and Long term Incentive plan awards, this is on a straight line basis over the vesting period (typically
three years). For the Material Risk Takers (MRT) scheme, which is paid in tranches, the Company applies the
‘benefit formula’ option and recognises the amounts in the Income Statement of the years in which they vest.
This equates to 60% recognition in the year awarded, with the remaining 40% recognised over three years on
a straight line basis. For further information on the vesting period of the MRT scheme refer to page 12 of the
Directors’ Report.

s. Leases

IFRS 16 sets out the principles for the recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure of leases and
requires lessees to account for all leases under a single on-balance sheet model similar to the accounting for
finance leases under IAS 17. The standard includes two recognition exemptions for lessees ~ leases of ‘low-
value’ assets (e.g. personal computers) and short-term leases (i.e. leases with a lease term of 12 months or
less).

At the commencement date of the lease, a lessee recognises a liability to make lease payments (i.e. the lease
liability) and an asset representing the right to use the underlying asset during the lease term (i.e. the right-
of-use asset). Lessees are required to separately recognise the interest expense on the lease liability (Note
3) and the depreciation expense on the right-of-use asset (Note 20).

Lessees are also required to remeasure the lease liability upon the occurrence of certain events (e.g. a change
in the lease term, a change in future lease payments resulting from a change in an index or rate used to
determine those payments). The lessee recognises the amount of the remeasurement of the lease liability as
an adjustment to the right-of-use asset.

market. To control the level of risk taken, the Company assesses the counterparties using the same

Goodwill

All Goodwill originates in the UK and relates to investment management businesses acquired in 2002 and
2003. The Goodwill is held at cost, less any impairment and historical amortisation charged (prior to adoption
of IFRS) and is tested for impairment on an annual basis.

The impairment review was carried out as at 31 December 2021 and an impairment of £41k was made as a
result (2020: £103k). The impairment reflects a fall in value of the underlying AUM acquired. In identifying the
recoverable amount or value in use, a discounted cash flow valuation was undertaken by the Company using
the following key assumptions:

e Net cash flows have been projected over a 5 year period based on past experience.

e Adiscount rate of 8.0% (2020: 8%) has been applied to the net cash flows. The discount rate used in
2021 is based on and similar to historic rates previously determined by ourselves in conjunction with
Quintet (which were based on the Capital Asset rate of Return Method specific to the UK).
Management consider this rate appropriate given the sensitivity analysis conducted around discount
‘rates.

e Income has been calculated at the current run rate for the two businesses acquired.

e It is assumed that AUM will fall by 12% per annum (2020: 12%), determined by historical rates of
decline.

e Sensitivity analysis is performed on the growth rate and discount rates used in the reviews. The
impairment is most sensitive to a fall in AUM and hence income. For example, if the Company were
to assume that AUM fell by 20% p.a. the impairment would be an additional £125k. By contrast, if the
discount rate was 12% rather than 8.0%, the goodwill would only need to be impaired by a further
£75k.

Intangible assets — Customer relationships

considered necessary.

In identifying the recoverable amount or value in use, a discounted cash flow valuation was undertaken by the
Company. The present value of future cash flows was assessed over the useful economic life of the asset.

In assessing the sensitivity of the review to a change in assumptions, the Company applied similar criteria to

those used for goodwill above. Under such assumptions no impairment of the intangible assets would be

Measurement of Expected Credit Losses (“ECLs”)

The Company has adopted ECL modelling which has been developed by and is maintained by its parent
company, Quintet, and the process is summarised below. The Company provides input to the ECL model and
receives the output from Quintet, which is used to compute ECL amounts.

For the most material exposures, being the investment portfolio and the customer loan portfolio, the ECL is
calculated by breaking down the cash flow structure of the exposure and assuming a number of defaults
along its lifeline.

The Company’s definition of ‘default’ is when either or both of the following have taken place:

« the Company considers that the borrower is unlikely to pay its credit obligations to the Company (or
to Quintet), in full and without recourse by the Company to actions such as realising security (for
example, insolvency of the borrower), and/or

« the borrower is past due more than 90 (consecutive) days on any material credit obligation to the
Company (or to Quintet), including the payment of interest or principal and including failure of the
borrower to repay or refinance the loan in full at maturity and which remains expired for a period
exceeding 90 days.

ECLs are calculated based on probability-weighted scenarios to measure the expected cash shortfalls,
discounted at an approximation to the Effective Interest Rate (“EIR”).

ECLs are measured as the probability-weighted present value of expected cash shortfalls over the remaining
expected life of the financial instrument. Three quantities mainly drive this measure of loss:

(i) the instrument's probability of default (“PD”),
(ii) the loss given default (“LGD”), and
(iii) the exposure at default (EAD).

IFRS 9 requires that estimated ECLs reflect an unbiased and probability-weighted amount that is determined
by evaluating a range of possible outcomes.

The Company incorporates three forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios in its ECL calculation process,
namely a base scenario, an upside scenario and a downside scenario. Probability weights are attributed to
each scenario by a committee of experts, under the review of senior management. This assessment is
performed on a quarterly basis.

When relevant, the assessment of multiple scenarios also incorporates how defaulted loans are expected to

Provisions for ECLs relating to undrawn loan commitments are assessed as set out in Note 29. The calculation

The mechanics of the ECL method are summarised below:

Stage 1: The 12 month ECL is calculated as the portion of Lifetime ECLs ("LTECLs’”) that represent the
ECLs that result from default events in a financial instrument that are possible within the 12 months after
the reporting date. The Company calculates the 12 month ECL allowance based on the expectation of a
default occurring in the 12 months following the reporting date. These expected 12-month default
probabilities are applied to a forecast EAD and multiplied by the expected LGD rate and discounted by an
approximation to the original EIR.

- Stage 2: When a loan has shown a significant increase in credit risk since origination, the Group
records an allowance for the LTECLs. The mechanics are similar to those explained above, including the
use of multiple scenarios, but PDs and LGDs are estimated over the lifetime of the instrument. The
expected cash shortfalls are discounted by an approximation to the original EIR.

- Stage 3: For loans considered credit-impaired the Group recognises the lifetime expected credit losses
for these loans. The method is similar to that for Stage 2 assets. The default PD for Stage 3 assets is set
at 100%, although specific impairments for these assets are subject to Group Credit Committee
judgement, based upon ‘dedicated in-depth’ analysis.

- Loan commitments and letters of credit: When estimating LTECLs for undrawn loan commitments, the
Group estimates the expected portion of the loan commitment that will be drawn down over its expected
life. The ECL is then based on the present value of the expected shortfalls in cash flows if the loan is
drawn down, based on a probability-weighting of the three scenarios. The expected cash shortfalls are
discounted at an approximation to the expected EIR on the loan. For credit cards and revolving facilities
that includes both a loan and an undrawn commitment, ECLs are calculated and presented together with
the loan. For loan commitments and letters of credit, the ECL is recognised within Provisions.

Forward looking information incorporated in the ECL models

Both the assessment of credit risk (for non-low risk exposures) and the estimation of the expected credit

computed. On that basis, the credit risk will be assessed and the ECL calculated. The weights will be refreshed

Interest Rate Risk

The Company has a defined strategy for managing interest rate risk and respective mis-match limits have
been established.

In order to manage positions effectively Risk Management produces a daily mismatch report for each
currency. The net or unmatched positions are summarised with the results compared against predefined Gap
limits. These limits are designed to restrict the duration of the Company's interest rate exposure, so that it is
primarily conducted on a short-term basis (i.e. so that the opportunity for rate changes to occur will be limited),
and mitigate the Company’s exposure to ‘basis risk’ (i.e. instances where transactions have different reference
rates, such as base rate and LIBOR). These mis-match or Gap limits are approved by ALCo. Excesses to
these limits are monitored by Risk Management, must be authorised appropriately, and are reported to senior
management.

revised to include a range of interest rate shocks, as per the EBA guidelines (i.e. parallel and non-parallel
