The Third Force

Economic activity based on Co-operative Principles, while initially evolving as a reaction against
some of the less attractive features of British industrial and commercial life some 150 years ago,
is steadily gaining acceptance as a realistic alternative to the “free market” private and the publicly-
owned sectors of industry and trade respectively, The Consumer Co-operative Movement has of
course retained its impact throughout the past century and a half and the Bank has played an im-
portant role in helping to maintain this position. Now, however, an increasing number of groups of
people are investigating the possibilities of working or operating together within institutions which
they themselves have set up and control. In other words they are moving from the traditional
situation where capital employs labour to an opposite position where the labour force as controllers
of an enterprise provide, and, where necessary, recruit capital.

Naturally, the collective impact on the economy of these newer fcrms of co-operation will be minimal
for some time, but with the experience and support of the already long established other areas of
the Co-operative Movement, much can be achieved. This mixing of youthful enthusiasm: with
experience based on solid achiavement has, | am glad to say, been a hopeful sign in the initial
discussions held by the Co-operative Development Agency. The Agency was formally established
in Saptember last, and is now taking on the role of encourag'ng interest in the philosophy and
principles of Co-operation, By providing information and advice and by creating a communications
network wherein, in addition to its own services, i, will be able to bring together groups of existing
or potential co-operators, and those who are prepared to provids the services which are essential
prerequisites to the establishing of successful \entures of whatever kind. The Chairman of the
Agency, Lord Oram, has indicated that he sees in important role for the established Co-operative
institutions, including the Co-operative Bank, ir assisting the effective operations of the Agency,

Banking Initiatives

To be successful in the long term any institution, whether based on Co-operative, Capitalist or any
other principles, must provide the products which its markets demand or need and in trading
conditions which meet the requirements of both buyer and seller, The Co-operative Bank Group
has long been employing this basic rule, continually acljusting the characteristics of its range of
products and services and successfully expanding its total business at a competitive rate, A further
instance of this activity occurred in January this year when the Bank indicated an intention to launch
a national cradit card for Co-op shoppers — "“Handycard” ~ which will be exclusive to Co-op stores
and valid for a wide range of goods, A pilot exercise to prove the efficacy of the arrangement has
been in progress for some time and itis anticipated that the service will be available nationally during
the course of 1980, The extent of interast being shown indicates that the collective demand for
such a product was already considerable, and it is believed that the Bank’s move to provide the
facility will be a source of advantage and satisfaction on all sides. The retailer will sell more goods
through the credit availability and be able to transfer his administratively expensive in-house
financing schemes to the specialists in finance; the consumer will gain the early satisfaction of his
desired purchase. The Bank will gain a valuable new service with which to attract naw customers,

Initiatives have also been taken by the Bank to expand its role in the international Co-operative
banking movement, Business relationships with industry, commerce and foreign banking enter-
prises have been strengthened largely through personal contacts and active collaboration in joint
ventures. Specifically, the Bank has worked in conjunction with Co-operative and “Popular” banks
overseas and Regional and Local Authorities in the UK in organising seminars designed to encourage
small and medium-sized customers of these banks to trade with and invest in the UK. These avents
have been very successful and plans for similar exercises with other Co-operative banks and UK
local authorities are well advanced,

Operating through its membership of the international Confederation of Popular Credit and the
Association of Co-operative Savings and Credit Institutions of the EEC, the Bank has also continued
to keep closely in touch with proposed Community legislation thereby assisting custorners in their
syarch for advice on Community matters, and in the provision of finance from Brussels, as well as
acting in its own interests.

To assist further its determination to provide a service to all sectors of the wider Co-operative
Movement the Bank has also recently become a member of the International Confederation of
Agricultural Credit (CICA) which brings together banks interested in agricultural finance from more
than forty countries,

Economic Dissatisfaction

The good baginning to the year 1978 was not sustained throughout the period. The overall economic
situation deteriorated considerably over the fast quarter, and into the new year. At the time of
writing there is every prospect of a reversion to double figure inflation, whilst output statistics show
that the level of activity has hardly increased since the burst of growth early last summer. There has,
however, been a glimmer of hope in the past few weeks. Another consolation perhaps has been
that the balance of payments has shown unusual strength by not lapsing into consistent deficit
whenever consumer spending power was made available.
Indeed with ihe curzent measure of control over prices, increases in average real earnings over the
past year have been at their highest level for some time. in effect of course, we have been paying
ourselves a bonus from the North Sea oil reserves and which, whilst it has inspired a certain degree
of activity in British industry, has also been used to a large extent to buy in large quantities of foreign
manufactured goods, many of which | believe we should be producing ourselves.

In fact, British industry has in general responded remarkably sluggishly to the rising demand ‘for
both consumption and investment factors over the past two years. The strengthening of sterling
over that period hds made imports more competitive and has not helped the situation, but that is
only one contributory factor; others include the circumstances that in spite of high unemployment
rates there may well have been a shortage of key skilled labour, with employers reluctant to invest
in training because of the uncertain economic climate. One solution to this problem would be for
the Authorities to try to generate longer and steadier growth periods in the economy in order to
inspire that underlying confidence which will lead to an entrepreneur's commitment to invest and
expand his activities. \ndeed a significant step forward towards achieving this has occurred in
tecent weeks in the form of a fairly general realisation and acceptance that to operate an economy
efficiently there must be general agreement on all sides as to what is trying to be achieved. The
West German system of both sides of industry and Government meeting round a table once a year
2 decide on output, wage and price targets for the next year or so has, | feat, a great deal to commend
t.

Financial Control

Over the past year we have seen a steadily increasing degree of impact from measures introduced
by the Authorities. From a relatively low level of interest rates during the early part of the year
~ Co-operative Bank Base Rate stood at 64 per cent in mid-January ~ financial confidence weakened
and with growing concern about the growth of the money supply, the Bank of England's Minimum
Lending Rate was raised to 10 per cent in early June. Towards the end of this period, and as a
further measure of control, the Authorities reintroduced in May a directly administered method of
determining MLR. A month later they reimposed the system of supplementary special deposits
known as the “corset”, as a direct restriction on the expansion of interest bearing liabilities but with
the overall objective of restricting the Jevel of the banks’ lending activities. Although the direct
impact of the “corset” is hard to judge most banks appeared to have little difficulty in keeping
below the monthly ceiling, Bank lending overall continued to rise steadily, with increasing usage of
bill acceptances, a long-standing traditional method of financing which has been given a new lease
of fife. The year ended with unpracedented steep rises in short-term interest rates in the United
States combining with increasing anxiety on the part of our own Government about the funding
of its borrowing requirement. The outcome was a further raising of MLR by one-quarter to 124 per
cent and the eventual successful, some would say over expensive, funding of the needs of the
public sector,

Group Financial Performance

Consolidated group operating profit for the year to 13th January, 1979, totalled £5-4m, an increase
of 33-7% over the last year. After allowing for taxation, retained earnings totalled £3-4m — a
significant increase on the equivalent figure for 1977. Co-operative Bank Limited aud Co-onerative
Commercial Bank Limited combined recorded an increased operating profit of £3-7m compared
with £3:0m the previous year. F. C. Finance Limited, the other major participant in the Group,
increased its operating profit very significantly by over one-half to reach £1 -8m.
The Group's consolidated balance sheet total of £460m at 13th January, 1979, was 11% higher
than at the same time the previous year, but showed a slightly lower rate of expansion than the
13% recorded by the parent bank and Co-operative Commercial Bank combined to reach £410m,
The Price Commission in its Report “Banks — Charges for Money Transmission Services” published
in April last suggested that the Bank was “in a position to become a real force in alternative banking”.

Co-operative Bank Limited

The parent bank of the Group has made considerable progress in developing its market share among
personal customers and specific groups within the corporate sector. At the year end, the Bank had
144% more accounts of all types than a year earlier, which maintained the highly satisfactory rate
of growth of recent years,

In the personal sector, growth of new business has again been rapid, partly as a result of the retention
of the commission-free current account service for those customers operating in credit. A high
proportion of new business continues to be won from competitor institutions. Customer knowledge
of the advantages of using a full range of tlie Bank‘s financial services has increased and, in conse-
quence, for example, the number of general purpose Budget Account facilities in operation has
risen by over one-quarter,

The Bank has continued to extend and improve its service to its traditional groups of customers
within the Co-operative Movement, among Local Authorities and Trade Unions, and towards the
club sector, all these being areas in which the Bank enjoys a major share of the market. Considerable
expansion has taken place in the development of the Bank's links with corporate customers outside
the traditional areas. Now Business sector accounts have grown in line with the overall expansion
in numbers of accounts, with particularly good progress among small businesses where the Bank
has established regional branch offices, The Bank is fast establishing a reputation for a sympathetic
and swift response to requests for facilities from existing and potential customers, thus putting into
practice, to gond effect, the co-operative principle of “caring”.

During the year, a new regional office of the Bank was opened at Hanley; Barnsley Branch was
relocated in new and larger purpose-built premises; Doncaster Branch was refurbished and en-
larged considerably, Progress has also been made in identifying areas for new branch developments
over the next faw years to provide a truly country-wide network in all major centres of population.

Development of the range of banking facilities offered on our behalf by our colleagues in Co-operative
Retail Societies is continuing with increasing success. Well over 700 “Handybanks’ are now in
operation, providing access to all the main Bank services, during shopping hours, while the 4,000
"Gash-a-Cheque" points situated at convenient locations in most Co-operative stores continue to
meet the very clear demand for this basic service.

Plans are now well advanced for completion of the first phase of the transfer of “back-office”
activities to the purpose-built Customer Service Bureau in Skelmersdala New Town. During the
past year many routine operations have been transfarred to the centralised and highly automated
systems at the Bureau and further significant changes will be effected in 1979.

The Bank’s new Head Office in Corporation Street, Manchester, is also nearing completion. The
“topping out” ceremony was held in January this year and by early next year the Bank’s main
Manchester Branch should be operating from the new building.

F, C, Finance Limited

Although the cost of money steadily increased throughout the year, finally reaching near crisis
levels as the year drew to a close, F. C, Finance Limited improved on its performance in the previous
year and achieved a profit befare taxation which is a record since the company was incorporated in
1958, The company’s balance sheet reflects the pragrassive change in the mix of the business, with
the traditional areas of activity in personal and industrial credit, and in leasing, showing growth,
against the steady reduction in the proportion related to property development and construction
finance. Profit is now derived increasingly from the former areas rather than the latter, and the
sounder financial basis on which it moves into 1979 may be regarded as reassuring. During 1978
there was steady growth of new business activity overall with the new branch offices at Perth and
Portsmouth, augmented at the end of the year by a further branch at Stoke-on-Trent, increasing the
coverage and making their contribution. Association with, and advances to, Co-operative Retail
Societies and their customers were maintained during the year with the company competing
actively. Some limitations were experienced in the company’s ability to handle certain classes of
business such as the smaller unit credit facilities sought by many Co-operative Retail Societies for
their customers. The introduction of the “Handycard” scheme by the Bank will do much to resolve
this problem.

Accounting Policies

Accounting Policies which have changed this year

(a) Losses on Advances

The amount charged against operating profit for losses on advances comprises specific provisions against
identifiable losses and a general provision to cover latent but unidentifiable losses in the advances at the year
end. Previously Co-operative Bank Limited had based the charge for losses on advances on the average
experience of the last five years and the current volume of lending; any shortfall or excess in the adequacy of
the provision to meet anticipated losses on advances was charged or credited to profit and loss account.

(b) Sale of Investments

Reallsed profits and losses on sales of investments are taken to profit and loss account in the year in which
they arise, Previously they were transferred to profit and loss account in five equa! annual instalments.

(c) Depreciation

Depreciation of freehold buildings is provided at 23% of the original purchase price. Depreciation of leasehold
Jand and buildings is provided at 24% or in equal annual instalments over the life of the lease if it is less than
40 years, Depreciation of equipment and fittings is provided in equal annual instalments over the estimated
working lives of the assets, Previously, land was depreciated in Co-operative Bank Limited at 12% of the
purchase price and the subsidiary companies did not depreciate freehold buildings.

(d) Comparative figures

The comparative figures for 1977 have all been restated to conform with the revised accounting policies stated
above. Such restatements are not material,

Accounting Policies which have not changed

(e) Basis of consolidation and accounting date

The accounts of the group which are made up for the 52 weeks to 13th January, 1979, incorporate the accounts
of F. C. Finance Limited and its subsidiaries which are made up for the year ended 31st December, 1978.
Since the group accounting date is virtually coterminous with the calendar year 1978 the financial year's
figures are headed 1978 and the correspanding figures for the previous year are headed 1977, The comparative
figures are for a 53-week year.

(i) Investment Income

Income from investments includes amortization of premiums and discounts on dated stocks, credit for which
is taken in equal annual instalments from the date of acquisition to maturity. The premiums and discounts on
investments acquired from CWS are based on the market value of these investments at 10th July, 1971, at
which date the banking activities of CWS were transferred to and vested in Co-operative Bank Limited,

(g) Hire Purchase and instalment credit agreements
