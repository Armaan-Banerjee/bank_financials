Global Economic Environment

The global economy continued to face subdued momentum through 2025, shaped by ongoing trade frictions,
uneven consumer demand, and policy adjustments in major economies. Growth remained moderate in
advanced markets, with emerging economies generally expanding at a faster pace, albeit from diverse starting
points. Inflationary pressures, which peaked in earlier years, eased in many regions as commodity prices
softened and supply-side frictions gradually diminished. Central banks in key jurisdictions adopted cautious
monetary policy stances, balancing inflation objectives with support for growth, although the pace and extent
of policy adjustments varied across regions. Global trade and investment flows continue to be influenced by
geopolitical tensions and shifts in energy markets, reinforcing the importance of resilience and adaptability in
financial institutions.

United Kingdom

The UK economy experienced modest growth in 2025, with real GDP expanding by around 1/1.2 % amid
persistent global uncertainties and domestic structural headwinds. Activity was supported by consumer
spending and services output but remained below longer-term trends. Labour market conditions softened, with
unemployment rising modestly and wage growth decelerating in real terms. Consumer price inflation remained
above pre-pandemic norms, averaging above the Bank of England’s 2% target for much of the year. This
influenced monetary policy decisions, with the Bank of England lowering interest rates cautiously through 2025
as inflation gradually eased, and markets. anticipating further adjustment in 2026 as inflation pressures
moderate. The broader economic backdrop included continued cost pressures on businesses, elevated energy
and food prices relative to prior years, and subdued investment growth, though forecasts suggest inflation could
approach target levels by mid-2026 if current trends persist. Labour market slack and slower hiring intentions
have tempered wage dynamics and consumer confidence toward year-end.

Eurozone and Europe

in the euro area, inflation aligned more closely with the European Central Bank’s target around the end of 2025,
reflecting lower energy costs and subdued domestic price pressures. Growth remained modest, with internal
demand providing some support against external headwinds, including weaker export markets and geopolitical
uncertainty. Interest rates were broadly stable, with policymakers signalling a cautious stance pending clearer
evidence of sustained inflation convergence.

Egypt and Regional Economy

Egypt’s economy has showed resilience, supported by recent reforms and investment inflows. Enhanced fiscal
and monetary policy frameworks, alongside structural investment commitments, contributed to stabilising
foreign currency flows and alleviating external bottlenecks that weighed on performance in prior years. While
geopolitical tensions in the wider Middle East continued to influence sentiment and regional trade patterns,
Egypt's strategic position as a nexus between Africa, the Middle East and Europe support its long-term trade
and investment proposition. At the time of writing this report there is no clear consensus on when the current
conflict will end. Egypt and the UK will be affected like the majority of the world with its impact on the energy
prices and inflation. Rating agencies maintained a positive outlook on Egyptian sovereign credit metrics,
underpinned by ongoing reform and structural diversification efforts. Refer to the Strategic report for more
insight on the current conflict in the West Asia and Middle East.

Climate Risk Awareness

UK regulators recognise climate change as a source of financial risk, arising through physical and transition
channels and potentially affecting credit, market, operational and liquidity risks over time. The Bank adopts a
proportionate and risk-based approach to managing climate-related financial risks, consistent with its size,
business model and risk profile. While direct exposure remains limited, climate considerations are embedded
within the Bank’s existing governance and risk management framework rather than treated as a standalone risk.
During 2025, oversight of climate-related risks was maintained through established Board and management
committees. Relevant considerations are incorporated, where appropriate, into credit assessments, sector
reviews and risk appetite discussions. The Bank continues to monitor regulatory developments and will refine
its approach as supervisory expectations and industry practices evolve.

Strategic Report

The Bank is a wholly owned subsidiary of the National Bank of Egypt, 1187 Corniche El Nil Street, Cairo, Egypt.
The Bank is authorised by the Prudential Regulation Authority (“PRA”) and regulated by the Financial Conduct
Authority (“FCA”) and the PRA.

The Bank provides general banking services in the United Kingdom (“the UK”) to private retail and public sector
customers, particularly to the Egyptian community, and conducts international banking business. The Bank
participates actively in the inter-bank money market, foreign exchange, and syndicated loans markets, the
finance of international trade, and invests in gilt-edged securities and floating rate notes.

Vision
The Bank’s mission is to:
i. Support Egypt and Egyptians abroad via synergies with our parent
ii. Find opportunities in markets of value where our distinctive proposition is a strength
iii. Prioritise sustainable funding for growth and financial disciplines for delivery
iv. To build strategic foundations for growth across people, risk settings, compliance, processes and
technology

The Bank operates a traditional banking model, centred on attracting deposits and extending credit through
various business units, including Personal Banking, Lending, Treasury, and Trade Finance.

Our deposit base comprises a diverse range of customers: governmental agencies; individuals; financial
institutions; deposit aggregators; and corporate businesses. These customers represent a geographic spread
across Egypt, the UK, and other regions.

We leverage these deposits, along with equity and debt capital, to provide financing solutions to corporate
clients and financial institutions. Our lending activities encompass loans, debt securities, trade finance,
unregulated mortgages and money market lending. This diversified approach allows us to effectively serve our
clients while managing risk and optimising returns.

As a wholly owned subsidiary of National Bank of Egypt, the Bank is adequately capitalised (in terms of retained
profit, equity capital and subordinated loans) by the sole shareholder.

Business Model
The Bank operates a number of business lines which are described below:

Personal Banking: The Bank offers personal banking services in the UK to Egyptian nationals, Egyptian embassies
and related offices and Egyptian corporate customers operating primarily outside Egypt. Currently, the key
product offerings are fixed term deposits, plus current account services.

Lending: The Bank offers syndicated loans to banks, corporations, and sovereign entities to meet general funding
requirements. It also provides bilateral and direct loans, including unregulated Buy to Let mortgages, to support
working capital, capital expenditure, and trading activities. In 2025, the Bank experienced strong growth in this
segment and intends to continue expanding it in 2026. Furthermore, the Bank delivers corporate and
institutional banking services through club deals and bilateral lending arrangements.

Treasury: Treasury activity is focused primarily on liquidity management through the Money Markets & the

management of a portfolio of investments to assist with liquidity and enhance income. The Bank’s Treasury
operates a non-trading book. Foreign exchange services in major currencies are also offered through traditional

Financial Overview
As at 31 December 2025, the Bank had total assets of £1,796m. An increase of 30.2% from the previous year’s
total assets of £1,379m.

This increase of £417m is part of the Bank’s growth strategy with a key focus of growing its GBP based customer
loan book especially in respect of Commercial Real Estate (CRE) which increased by £306m over the year. As the
assets have grown, we have increased funding from retail customer deposits and the issue of additional sub
debt, this also enabled us to reduce the wholesale funding which had a positive impact on our interest rate
margin. The growth in the CRE space partly offset lower trade finance volumes. We have found maintaining our
margin expectations in trade finance sector, especially in respect of Egypt challenging. The improvements in the
macro-economic situation for Egypt has led to an increase in competition so putting pressure on margins for
trade finance transactions. We are seeing competitors re-enter the trade finance market as economic conditions
and with the risk profile improving making Egypt far more an attractive offering but the downside being pricing
pressure and decreasing margins. We continue to assess the return for each transaction in line with our
risk/return objectives.

Net interest income remained at a very similar level of £20.9m in 2025 (2024 - £21.Sm). The yield on interest
bearing assets exceeded the increase in cost of funding. Although the mix of the Balance Sheet changed
significantly, the increase in the CRE income has offset the drop in loans and advances to banks income due to
narrowing of spreads.

Fees and commissions income decreased to £2.2m from £3.5m, which represents a fall of 36.1%. This has been
predominantly due to lower Bank trade finance business during the year due to increased competition after
macro-economic conditions improved and more Banks entered the Egyptian space.

The Bank realised a profit on debt securities amounting to £1.5m (2024- £0.2m) mainly from sale of Egyptian
bonds.

Operating expenses rose by 7.2% to £19.4m (2024: £18.1m), primarily reflecting an increase in staff costs as
headcount was expanded to support the heightened scale of operations.

An operating profit of £5.5m for the year ended 31 December 2025 compares with a profit of £7.9m for the
period ended 31 December 2024.

The Bank made a profit after tax of £3.9m compared to a profit of £5.8m the previous year.
During the year, the fixed asset additions included £1.98m related to the capitalisation of core banking Flexcube
projects which went live on the 10th of October 2025. The transition of these costs from capital work-in-progress

to intangible fixed assets reflects the project’s completion and the commencement of its expected economic
useful life.

Review of current conflict in the West Asia and Middle east
Egypt has so far avoided being directly pulled into the conflict, but its position at the heart of the global trade
and energy network makes it susceptible to broader regional! disturbance.

Currency: The Egyptian Pound (EGP) is expected to face continued pressure, a natural outcome for a freely
floating currency as risk-averse investors exit the region. Should the regional conflict persist, the EGP could
weaken below 53 EGP to the US dollar. We expect the current conflict will lead to the Central Bank of Egypt to
pause its planned interest rate cuts to maintain economic stability. Significant "hot money” outflows occurred
in the days following the outbreak of the war. Ultimately, the duration of the conflict will dictate the scale of
these capital flights and the severity of the Egyptian pound's temporary depreciation.

Egypt has a much-improved stock of foreign currency reserves. Confidence in the exchange rate regime and the
flexibility of exit strategies have successfully incentivised investors to return as market optimism rebounds;
furthermore, this shift is underscored by the notable reversal of capital outflows into inflows. We appreciate
this time is different in nature but shows how a spring back may happen with a ceasing of the conflict.

Tourism: Tourism to Egypt has been booming and seemed poised to get yet another boost from the opening
late last year of the Grand Egyptian Museum. Red Sea demand is typically the most sensitive to geopolitical
issues, Past incidents have seen cancellations hit there first, and a small number of tourists are already leaving
Hurghada and Sharm El Sheikh. The issue is that tourists make their decisions based on the image of the region
as a whole, not just the borders of a single state.

Suez Canal: Shipping lines had already been diverting away from the Suez Canal as tensions and rhetoric rose
even before any military action occurred. Hope of luring traffic back to the canal will be pushed back, with
shipping lines often looking for 1-3 months of stable and safe conditions following the end of any hostilities
before sending back their larger vessels. Flights to Egypt from outside the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) are so
far unaffected, with the country considered sufficiently distant from the conflict in terms of distance and lack
US military bases on its territory.

Remittances: Remittances could decline significantly if economic activity in host countries (GCC) is disrupted. It’s
difficult to quantify by how much at this stage.

Inflation and rates: If the conflict gets worse, we could see an imported wave of inflation due to jumps in shipping
and fuel prices, and that could prompt the central bank to keep interest rates higher for longer than planned.

Section 172

In fulfilling their duties to promote the success of the Bank, as outlined in Section 172(1) of the Companies Act
2006, the directors consider a wide range of stakeholders. This approach ensures that decisions are made with
a comprehensive understanding of their potential impact on all parties involved.

We have developed into a successful business, driven by a focus on a clear strategy and efforts to consider our
stakeholders’ interests throughout our decision-making process. In this statement we have detailed our key
stakeholders, their importance to our business and how we have engaged with them throughout the year.

Stakeholder engagement

We recognise and promote the importance of long-term business relationships with our stakeholders across all
of our business lines, and we are committed to engaging with them to ensure we maintain long-term
relationships and add long lasting value to the wider community in which we operate. Effective stakeholder
management is a key part of the development and execution of long-term growth and strategy. In undertaking
its duties, the Board is mindful in all its decisions to balance the interests of all the Banks stakeholders. The
Executive team have a full understanding of the requirements of Section 172 which forms a key part of our
decision-making process and how we balance the expectations of our Parent and the Groups other stakeholders.

Below we give examples of stakeholder engagement:

® Shareholders

transparent. The CFO provides regular financial updates to the Board on future performance and the

along with all correspondences with our Regulators with the Audit and Steering Committee.

e Employees
The Bank aims to be a meritacracy, driven by excellence in all that it does. It places significant emphasis on
the skills and experience of its employees and believes that investing in their development supports the long-
term success of both the Bank and its workforce. The Bank aims to maintain a positive and culturally diverse
working environment that promotes engagement and encourages employees to develop their skills and
perform at their best.

A structured annual appraisal process is in place to assess performance, identify strengths and areas for
development, and support career progression. A summary of appraisal outcomes is reviewed by the
Remuneration Committee. The Bank continues to prioritise training and development initiatives designed to
attract, retain, and develop high-calibre employees. These include mentoring, management and leadership
training, and support for relevant professional qualifications. The Board recognises that employees are
fundamental to the long-term success of the Bank and takes their interests into account in its decision-making
and oversight.

The Board determines the governance framework best suited to the Bank’s size and complexity. In support
of this, it receives regular updates from the CEO, Managing Director and HR on workforce matters, including
employee engagement, training and development initiatives, and appraisal outcomes.

context, accounting for the full range of stakeholders mentioned above, are executed with complete

e Liquidity
The Bank was able to meet all liquidity obligations using its counterbalancing capacity throughout the year.

Operational resilience

The year 2025 marked a key transition in the Bank’s Operational Resilience context, moving from project delivery
into full Business as Usual (BAU) by the end of Q1/2025. Building on prior years and aligned with PRA/FCA
expectations, the Bank continued to strengthen its resilience, governance, and assurance activities throughout
the year.

In April 2025, the Bank implemented a comprehensive Operational Resilience Policy and Procedures, formalising
its approach to safeguarding Important Business Services (“IBS”), managing critical third-parties; and responding
to disruptions. Annual resilience training was delivered to all staff in June, followed by the review and sign-off
of IBS definitions and impact tolerances during June—July 2025.

A major scenario exercise took place in August 2025 alongside the Disaster Recovery (“DR”) test for the new
Flexcube core banking system, validating failover and recovery capabilities and confirming the Bank remained
within IBS impact tolerances.

The annual Operational Resilience Self-Assessment was completed early in Q3, confirming full compliance and
no material vulnerabilities. In Q4/2025, the Bank finalized all resource mapping, Business Impact Analyses
(“BIA’s”), and Business Continuity Plans (“BCP’s”) within the BAU governance framework, supported by ongoing
use of Riskonnect.

Overall, the Bank strengthened its resilience posture in 2025 through embedded procedures, staff training,
refreshed annual reviews, validated scenario testing, and enhanced governance. Work continues into 2026 to
further mature Operational Resilience and maintain alignment with evolving regulatory expectations.

Principal Risks and Uncertainties
The Bank operates within a clearly defined risk management framework which is designed to identify, assess,
monitor and control the principal risks arising from its business activities. The Board has established a Risk
Appetite Framework which sets out the level of risk the Bank is willing to accept in pursuit of its strategic
objectives. Adherence to this framework is monitored through regular management information and reporting
to the Board and its committees.

_ Strategic Risk ; a -

Strategic risk is the risk of financial loss, capital or earnings volatility, or adverse impact on the Bank’s viability
arising from inappropriate strategic decisions, ineffective implementation of strategy, or failure to respond
appropriately to changes in the external environment.

The Bank’s strategic objectives and risk appetite are set by the Board and are supported by a structured strategic
planning process. Strategic initiatives are subject to robust governance, including Board oversight, challenge by
senior management and the Risk function, and assessment of alignment with the Bank’s risk appetite, capital
and liquidity resources.

Strategic risk is monitored through regular management information and performance reporting to the Board,
including assessment of business plan delivery, key assumptions, and emerging external risks such as
macroeconomic conditions, competitive dynamics, regulatory change and technological developments.

The Bank seeks to mitigate strategic risk by maintaining a prudent and sustainable business model, undertaking
periodic stress and scenario analysis, and reviewing its strategy considering changes to its operating
environment, financial performance and risk profile.

The key risks inherent in our business model are:

° Credit Risk
Credit risk is defined as the risk that a customer or counterparty is unable to meet its contractual
financial obligations, resulting in financial loss to the Bank. Credit risk represents the most significant
risk to which the Bank is exposed and includes counterparty risk, settlement risk and concentration risk.

The Bank’s approach to credit risk management is founded on maintaining the independence and
integrity of credit assessment, approval and monitoring processes. This is supported by clearly defined
credit procedures, delegated authorities and limit structures which guide the day-to-day management
of credit exposures.

Credit limits are established for all customers and relevant countries following a detailed assessment of
creditworthiness, incorporating both external information and internal analysis. Credit exposures are
actively monitored against Board-approved risk appetite limits, including limits on single-name, sectoral
and geographical concentrations.

The Bank applies appropriate credit risk mitigation techniques and provisioning methodologies in line
with applicable accounting and regulatory requirements. Credit risk performance and emerging trends
are reported regularly to senior management and the Board.

° Liquidity Risk
Liquidity risk is the risk that the Bank is unable to meet its financial obligations as they fall due, without
incurring unacceptable losses. The Bank manages liquidity risk in accordance with a Board-approved
liquidity risk policy and associated limits.

Liquidity positions across currencies are monitored daily, with regular reporting provided to senior
management, Risk Management, Treasury and Treasury Operations. The Bank maintains a portfolio of
high-quality liquid assets. and. manages. its. liquidity profile to ensure that -regulatory liquidity ~- ~~
requirements and internal risk appetite thresholds are always met.

Oversight of liquidity risk is provided by ALCO, which reviews liquidity metrics, funding concentrations
and the contractual maturity profile on a regular basis.

Liquidity risk is assessed through the Bank’s ILAAP, which identifies key liquidity risk drivers, sets out the
assumptions underpinning stress testing, and assesses the Bank’s ability to withstand severe but
plausible stress scenarios. The ILAAP also incorporates the Bank’s contingency funding plan and reverse
stress testing to identify scenarios that could threaten the Bank’s viability.

° Market Risk and Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book
Market risk is the risk of loss arising from movements in market variables such as interest rates, foreign
exchange rates and security prices. The Bank does not engage in proprietary trading and maintains only
a Banking Book.

Market risk arises primarily from the Bank’s holdings of debt securities and from interest rate and foreign
exchange exposures. Interest rate risk in the Banking Book (IRRBB) is actively monitored with exposures
maintained within Board-approved limits.

The Bank uses interest rate swaps primarily for hedging purposes in relation to asset and liability
positions. Foreign exchange risk arises from daily currency positions and is managed through clearly
defined position limits and escalation procedures.

Market and interest rate risks are monitored regularly and reported to ALCO and the Board.

e Country and Concentration Risk (Credit and Funding)
Concentration risk is the risk of loss arising from a lack of diversification within the Bank’s credit or
funding portfolios. This may result from exposure to individual counterparties, sectors, products or
geographical regions.

The Bank manages concentration risk through the application of Board-approved limits covering single
counterparties, industry sectors and geographic regions. Country risk assessments are informed by
external credit ratings and internal analysis.

Funding concentration risk is monitored as part of the Bank’s liquidity risk management framework.
During the year, the Bank maintained a diversified funding profile comprising wholesale funding and a
broad retail depositor base, including deposits sourced through digital platforms.

« Operational Risk and Operational Resilience
Operational risk is defined as the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes,
people and systems, or from external events. This includes legal risk but excludes strategic and
reputational risk.
Operational risk is managed on a day-to-day basis by business areas, with oversight provided by the Risk
function, Internal Audit and the Board. The Bank maintains a framework of policies, controls and incident
reporting processes designed to identify, assess and mitigate operational risks.

in line with regulatory expectations, the Bank has taken steps to enhance its operational resilience,
including identifying important business services, assessing potential vulnerabilities and strengthening
arrangements for business continuity, outsourcing and third-party risk management.

For regulatory capital purposes, the Bank applies the Basic Indicator Approach to operational risk, under
which capital is held equal to a fixed percentage of average positive gross income over the previous
three years.

e Cyber Risk
Cyber risk is the risk of financial loss, operational disruption, or reputational damage arising from the
failure or compromise of information systems, data, or technology infrastructure, due to cyber-attacks,
system failures or third-party incidents.

The Bank manages cyber risk as an integral component of its operational risk and operational resilience
frameworks. Responsibility for cyber risk management is shared between business areas and specialist
technology functions, with oversight provided by the Risk function, senior management and the Board.

The Bank maintains a range of preventive and detective controls designed to protect the confidentiality,
integrity and availability of its systems and data. These include information security policies, access
controls, monitoring, incident response arrangements and staff awareness training. Cyber risks arising
from outsourcing and third-party arrangements are managed through due diligence, contractual
protections and ongoing monitoring.

In line with regulatory expectations, the Bank considers cyber risk within its operational resilience
programme, including assessing the potential impact of severe but plausible cyber incidents on
important business services, payment processing and access to financial market infrastructures. Cyber-
related disruption scenarios are incorporated into business continuity and incident management
planning, with escalation and recovery arrangements subject to regular review.

° Climate-Related Financial Risk
Climate-related financial risk represents the risk of financial loss arising from physical risks associated
with climate change, such as extreme weather events, and transition risks arising from the move to a
lower-carbon economy.

The Bank has assessed climate-related financial risk across its business activities and considers the
current level of exposure to be limited, having regard to the nature, scale and geographic distribution of
its lending and investment portfolios.

Oversight of climate-related financial risk is provided by the Board, with management responsible for
monitoring developments in regulatory expectations and assessing the potential impact on the Bank’s
strategy and risk profile. The Bank continues to enhance its understanding of climate-related risks in a
manner proportionate to its size, complexity and risk profile.

e Regulatory, Compliance and Financial Crime Risk
Regulatory and compliance risk is the risk of financial loss, regulatory sanction or reputational damage
arising from a failure to comply with applicable laws, regulations and supervisory expectations.

Governance of regulatory and compliance risk begins at Board level and is supported by the Compliance
function, Risk Management and Internal Audit. The Bank maintains policies, procedures and training
programmes designed to promote compliance with regulatory requirements and ethical standards.

Financial crime risk, including money laundering, terrorist financing, sanctions breaches and fraud, is
managed through a risk-based framework incorporating customer due diligence, transaction
monitoring, staff training and ongoing monitoring. The Bank continues to invest in systems and
resources to ensure that financial crime risks are effectively managed.

The Bank is committed to high standards of conduct and to delivering fair outcomes for customers, in
line with applicable conduct and consumer protection requirements.

e Reputational Risk
Reputational risk is the risk of damage to the Bank’s reputation arising from adverse perceptions among
customers, counterparties, regulators or other stakeholders. Reputational risk may arise from
operational failures, regulatory breaches, financial crime events or adverse external developments.

The Bank seeks to mitigate reputational risk through robust governance arrangements, effective risk
management, and adherence to applicable legal and regulatory standards.

(b) Income Recognition

Interest income and interest expense for all interest-bearing financial instruments along with fee income and expense are
recognised in the income statement using the Effective Interest Rate (“EIR”) method. The EIR is the rate that discounts the
expected future cash flows, over the expected life of the financial instrument, to the net carrying value of the financial asset
or liability. The EIR calculation includes all fees paid or received between parties to a contract that are an integral part of
the interest rate and are shown as net interest income. Interest on derivative financial instruments forming part of a
qualifying hedging relationship, is recognised on a net basis under interest income.

Fair value of financial assets and financial liabilities are determined as follows:

The fair values of financial assets and liabilities with standard terms and conditions and traded on active liquid markets are
determined with reference to quoted market prices. Other non-derivative financial assets and liabilities are determined in
accordance with generally accepted pricing models based on discounted cash flow analysis using prices from observable
current market transactions based on quoted prices for debt securities and dealer quotes for similar instruments. There
has been no transfer of levels during the year.

The following table provides an analysis of financial instruments that are measured subsequent to initial recognition at fair
value, grouped into Levels 1 to 3 based on the degree to which the fair value is observable:

* Level 1 — The best evidence of fair value is a quoted price for an identical asset in an active market. This category
comprises of debt securities and floating rate agreements as observable prices are available in the market.

¢ Level 2 — When quoted prices are unavailable, the price of a recent transaction for an identical asset provides evidence
~ of fair value as long as there has not been a significant change in economic circumstances or a significant lapse of time
since the transaction took place. If the entity can demonstrate that the last transaction price is not a good estimate of
fair value (e.g. because it reflects the amount that an entity would receive or pay in a forced transaction, involuntary
liquidation or distressed sale), that price is adjusted. This category comprises of foreign exchange contracts, interest rate
swaps, valued using data such as yield curves and exchange rates, requiring little management judgement.

« Level 3 — If the market for the asset is not active and recent transactions of an identical asset on their own are not a
good estimate of fair value, an entity estimates the fair value by using a valuation technique. The objective of using a
valuation technique is to estimate what the transaction price would have been on the measurement date in an arm's
length exchange motivated by normal business considerations.

exists for securities that are affected by market fluctuations. The interest rate swaps are packaged within asset swaps and
effectively hedged. Therefore, the market risk associated with asset swaps is limited to the interest rate spreads embedded
in the transactions only.

(i) Foreign currency risk

The Bank is exposed through daily currency open positions, but this is mitigated by the restrictions placed on the maximum
position allowed on each currency and enforced stop-loss positions. The Bank's net open foreign exchange positions are
monitored on a daily basis and managed by the Treasury front office. As most of the transactions are back-to-back therefore
the Bank is not exposed to material foreign currency risk.

Climate change

The Board recognised that the Bank can be exposed to risks arising from climate change. As such, a policy was developed
and approved by the 8oard for climate-related financial risk. This risk is closely monitored, and a quarterly update of our
impacted exposure is reported to ExCo, the Steering Committee, Risk and Compliance Committee and the Board. Climate
change financial risk is also embedded within the (CAAP. The consideration related to climate risk and monitoring of
financial impact of climate change is also presented on page 6 of the Annual Report. As at 31 December 2025, there is no
direct financial impact arising from climate related risk recognised in the financial statements.

Capital management

The Directors apply Common Equity Tier 1 Capital and Tier 2 Capital for capital management purposes. The principal
objectives in managing capital are to ensure it is sufficient to participate in lines of business and to meet capital adequacy
requirements of the Prudential Regulation Authority. Common Equity Tier 1 is measured as share capital and reserves less
regulatory deductions. Tier 2 capital consists of long-term subordinated debt from the parent Bank as detailed in note 19.
All the regulatory capital adequacy requirements were met during the year.
