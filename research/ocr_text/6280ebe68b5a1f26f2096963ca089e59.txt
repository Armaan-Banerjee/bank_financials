Chairman’s Statement

This is my fourth annual report as Chairman of RC] Bank UK Limited (the “Company’). The Company and its subsidiary RC! Financial Services
Limited ("RCI FS”) (together “RCI UK") continue to offer transparent and simple savings accounts to customers and to provide retail and wholesale
vehicle financing for the Renault, Dacia, Alpine and Nissan customers and dealerships.

RCI UK has emerged from the Covid 19 crisis with unwavering commitment to our customers, staff and our regulatory duties. RCI UK has delivered
a very strong commercial and financial performance for 2023 along with a strengthened balance sheet, continuing our commitment to our
customers, our staff and fulfilling our regulatory duties. However, macro-economic risks exacerbated by the war in Ukraine and the ongoing Israel-
Hamas conflict have created uncertainty and volatility in global markets, from commodities to currencies to interest rates and bond prices.

Interest rates have continued to rise in 2023 as Central Banks have acted to contain inflation and are now considered to be at their peak. With
inflation falling, there are expectations of rate cuts in 2024 as economic growth slows and fear of recession becomes the main focus.

In 2023, the motor industry aiso remained vulnerable to ongoing logistical pressures and rising geopolitical tensions. Supply chains, particularly
for new vehicles, have improved although some models may still experience delivery times in excess of pre-Covid times.

The motor industry is experiencing a structural change in the ownership and use of vehicles, which will develop over the next few years with the
surge of more flexible mobility options such as subscription services and lease offers. RC] Financial Services rebranded to Mobilize Financial
Services in 2022, demonstrating its close relationship to the new mobility brand of Renault Group, Mobilize. Mobilize Financial Services ("MFS’)
is the brand reference for Renault Group customers, including Renault, Dacia and Alpine, who are looking for a complete range of services related
to the usage of their cars.

In 2023, RCI UK acquired a stake in Select Car Leasing and a new leasing brand was launched. Select Lease by Mobilize operated by Mobilize
Lease & Co UK Limited ("MLC,” a subsidiary of RCI UK) will offer a full range of multi-brand car and van leasing solutions for private and business
customers in the UK fleet market. This partnership contributes to the global strategy of MFS to accelerate the deployment of operating lease
offers and meeting the new mobility needs of customers. Nissan customers will continue to benefit from the same quality of services and products,
through the Nissan Financial Services brand. MFS will offer more innovative services and digital experiences which will allow customers to reduce
their usage cost while accessing a greener mobility.

Climate change and global warming remain a key focus of governments globally. Full battery electric vehicles continue to rise in popularity, and
they have now overtaken diesel to become the second most common powertrain. RCI UK makes it easier to buy and drive new and used vehicles
and to enable Alliance brands fo develop sustainable mobility and to make electric vehicles accessible for all.

RCI UK maintains a focus on its customer service standards, and | am pleased to report that the UK group has been awarded a Net Promoter

Strategic Report

The Directors of RCI Bank UK Limited (the Company’) present their Strategic Report for the year ended 31 December 2023.

Business Model

Organisational structure

RCI UK is authorised by the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) and regulated by the PRA and the Financial Conduct Authority ("FCA’). The
Company is wholly owned by RCi Banque SA, a credit institution incorporated in France and supervised by the European Central Bank. RCI
Banque SA is a subsidiary of Renault SA and trades globally as Mobilize Financial Services since May 2022.

RCI Bank UK Limited is the parent company and sole shareholder of RCI Financial Services Limited ("RCI FS”), a company authorised and
regulated by the FCA to undertake consumer credit activities. RC! FS operates now as Mobilize Financial Services since October 2022.

RCI UK is also the parent company and 85% shareholder of Mobilize Lease & Co UK Limited ("MLC"), a new leasing company incorporated in
2023.

In July 2023, RC! Bank UK announced the acquisition of 36.6% in Select Vehicle Group Holdings Limited (“SVGH’), which is the holding of the
broker trading as Select Car Leasing. The deal became effective in November 2023 following regulatory approval.

This Annual Report presents the consolidated statements of RCI UK, its stake in SVGH and the controlled undertaking Special Purpose Vehicles
("SPV") Cars Alliance 2015 Limited and Cars Alliance UK Master pic ("RCI” or the "Group’).

Principal activities
Sales Financing

RCI UK supports the Renault Group and its key partner Nissan in the distribution of their new vehicles in the UK by providing financing options to
retail and business customers for the purchase of Renault, Dacia, Alpine and Nissan vehicles, mainly through their dealer networks. Following
announcements made in February and July 2023, a new Alliance Agreement came into force on 8 November 2023 with new partnership initiatives,
that expressed the strategic importance of Mobilize Financial Services for the Alliance in Europe.

The retail product range includes hire purchase, personal contract purchase (“PCP”), contract hire as well as other car and loan-related services.
RCI also supports the Alliance brand dealers by financing inventories of new vehicles, used vehicles and spare parts.

Savings Activity (RCI Bank’)

The current range comprises an easy-access savings account (‘Freedom Savings’), a green 14 Day Notice Account (‘E-Voive Saver’), a 95 Day
Notice Account and fixed-term accounts with 1 to 5 years initial maturity. The accounts are managed entirely online through the website
www.rcibank.co.uk and the mobile app for Apple and Google Android devices. Retail deposits represent a key element in the diversification of
funding for the bank.

Business Performance and position

Commercial performance

The commercial performance is generated by the sales financing activity of RCI FS. The RCI Bank Savings activity is part of our funding plan.
Commercially we rely on our lending business as a key driver which impacts the amount of funding we may require.

In 2023 new vehicle registrations in the UK, the total volume across the industry (“TIV’), reached 2.2 million vehicles, +19% compared to 2022. It
was the best industry performance since the pandemic. The brands financed by RCI UK achieved a total market share of 8.73% in 2023 compared
to 8.18% in the previous year. Nissan's market share was 4.31% (2022: 4.26%), whilst Renault captured 3.13% (2022: 2.47%) and Dacia 1.29%
(2022: 1.45%). The level of new vehicles registred by those brands totalled 196k units in 2023 compared to 154k in 2022.

In total, RCI UK financed 124k new and used vehicle contracts (2022:126k) corresponding to new financings of £2,229k (up 4%) thanks to an
increase in the average financed amount. The New vehicle financing penetration rate (i.e. proportion of new registrations that were financed by
RCI UK) was 36% in 2023, compared to 46.7% in 2022, a decrease that reflects the higher proportion of OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer)
sales to fleet channels. The 2023 level of used car finance reached 50k contracts, 4% below 2022 levels.

Future developments

The geo-political environment remains volatile with the conflicts in Ukraine and in Gaza, leading to volatility in commodity prices and potential
impacts to supply chains. The global inflation outlook has reduced significantly throughout 2023 but fears of recession remain high for the 2024
outlook. The interest rate outlook changed dramatically in Q4 2023 from ‘higher for longer’ to expectations of rate cuts throughout 2024 for the
major western economies. With inflation currently remaining above the Bank of England target of 2%, coupled with the current high level of interest
rates, this has the potential to negatively impact the credit risk of our portfolio although no material change in payment behaviour has been
observed to date.

The automotive industry has remained vulnerable to the global semiconductor shortage over the last two years although the situation has now
improved. The automotive market saw a material volatility in residual values notably in the first semester of 2023 affecting some electric vehicles.
Given the growing share of those vehicles in our business, this will remain a main attention point.

The regulatory landscape and management of conduct risk continues to be a keen area of attention as the Financial Conduct Authority (“FCA”)
enters into its third year of its 3-year strategy which focuses on achieving better outcomes for consumers and markets. Key regulatory change
initiatives and outcomes of consultations are expected in 2024 and the first reporting on delivery of good customer outcames in line with the
Consumer Duty (which came into effect on 31 July 2023) is required by July. Focus remains on the delivery of good outcomes for customers,
including those with vulnerable characteristics, as well as ensuring measures are in place to avoid causing foreseeable harm continues to be
implemented and embedded in RCI UK to ensure ongoing compliance with the Consumer Duty requirements.

The Financial Conduct Authority announced on 11 January 2024 that they are to undertake work in the motor finance market. In 2021 the FCA
banned discretionary commission arrangements for Dealers and brokers in relation to motor finance. The Financial Ombudsman Service has
considered some complaints rejected by firms in relation to commission arrangements. It recently found in favour of two complainants. This may
lead to an increase in complaints from consumers to firms and the Financial Ombudsman Service. Therefore the FCA announced it would review
historical motor finance discretionary commission arrangements and sales across several firms. As the FCA’s review of discretionary commission
arrangements for the motor finance market has just started, we are unable to determine whether the FCA review is likely to have a material
adverse impact on our financial statements.

RCI UK will continue to monitor closely the evolution in the regulatory framework applicable to its activities, especially that relating to the
implementation expected in July 2025 of remaining Basel prudential reforms also referred to as Basel 3.1 in the United Kingdom.

Clear business focus for 2024 and beyond will be to offer products tailored to customers looking for flexible offers, including car subscription, and
supporting the electrification strategy of the Alliance brands. Sales of electric vehicles are expected to further increase due to change in customers
demand and following the climate change initiatives by the UK Government including the phase-out date for the sale of new petrol and diesel cars
and vans in 2035.

Credit risk

Credit risk represents a primary risk faced by RCI UK. Credit risk will be retail or wholesale (dealer finance). It is defined as the possibility of a
loss resulting from a borrower's failure to repay a loan or meet contractual obligations. The Board has a low appetite for credit risk - oversight is
maintained through Executive and Board committee structures as noted below.

As well as customer default, this includes the residual value ("RV’) risk that the vehicle being financed is not worth the amount predicted when
returned to RCI UK (either following default or as a voluntary termination ("VT") or return of the vehicle at the end of a PCP contract).

Credit Risk is considered a Tier 1 primary risk to the business. It includes the following Tier 2 risks:

Retail Risk
Wholesale Risk
Counterparty Risk
Concentration Risk
Large Exposure Risk
Residual Value Risk

RCI UK faces credit risk as an inherent component of its lending activities. Its primary credit risk is the loss from a retail customer's or dealer's
failure to make payments according to contract terms. It can result from adverse changes in the credit quality of its obligors, a general deterioration
in UK economic conditions or adverse changes arising from systemic risks in the UK. Credit risk is segmented into three categories: retail (financing
of individual and corporate customers), wholesale (dealer funding) and counterparty (banks and financial institutions). The Credit Risk function is
responsible for the management of this risk -for both retail and wholesale lending- with oversight being provided by the Risk function as second
line of defence.

Retail credit risk is managed via credit scorecards developed over many years by RCI FS using external credit bureau data for auto-decisioning
and by manual underwriting for marginal cases. Additionally, RC! UK retains legal ownership of a financed vehicle until the financial obligation is
repaid. After initial loan approval, either via scorecard or manual underwriting, the ongoing default statistics are monitored and reviewed monthly
to ensure that scorecards continue to operate effectively. Should a customer default, a dedicated collections team will then attempt to recover the
funds.

Wholesale lending primarily includes financing to Renault and Nissan dealers to fund inventories of new, demo and used vehicles and, to a much
lesser extent, spare parts and working capital loans. This activity is subject to manual underwriting to assess the dealers’ financial situation, level
of financial guarantee received and numerous other financial and qualitative factors. The majority is stocking finance, with the vehicles as collateral
where RCI UK retains ownership until the vehicle is sold. Asset verification processes are in place and include physical audits of vehicle stocks
with increased audit frequency for higher risk dealers.

RCI UK takes only limited counterparty credit risk which arises both where it places deposits with other financial institutions, all of which are prime
UK banks, the Bank of England or its parent company, and when it invests in UK Government bonds which are considered low risk.

Residual Value (RV) risk

This is the risk that at the end of the contract period, a vehicle being financed is not worth the amount predicted. It potentially impacts credit
contracts (hire purchase and PCP) and leases (contract hire).

Changes in RV may happen at any time due to external factors and may cause a loss if the actual proceeds realised upon sale of a returned
vehicle are lower than the forecast at the beginning of the contract. Additionally, under the Consumer Credit Act, a vehicle may be returned at any
time once more than 50% of the repayments have been made, without liability (Voluntary Termination).

The forecast end-contract values of the vehicles financed are currently provided by an external provider. The data is used to calculate the current
RV position under a contract against the updated market value. For PCP contracts that represent most agreements where RCI UK bears the
residual value risk, the guaranteed future value (“GFV’) - used to determine the balloon payment at the end of the contract - is typically set below
expected market value to generate equity for the customer and to protect RCI UK against residual value risk. This risk is managed by the Finance
function that includes RV specialists and a Remarketing department which manages the resale of returning cars through different channels.
Oversight is provided by the Risk function. RCI UK keeps a provision for RV and VT which sits at £74m as of December 2023 (2022: £96m).

Liquidity and Funding risk

Liquidity and Funding risk is the risk that RCI UK is unable to make available sufficient resources to meet its current or future financial obligations
as they fall due or is only able to do so at a premium cost. RCI UK relies on its access to diverse sources of funding to finance its lending business
and for working capital requirements. If access to funding, specifically for retail deposits, became restricted or are impacted by change in customer
behaviour, either through an idiosyncratic event, through market movements, or through regulatory or Government action, this could result in the
scaling back or cessation of new lending.

Through its treasury operations and aversight by the Assets and Liabilities Committee (“ALCO”), RCI UK seeks to mitigate this risk by actively
managing its liquidity reserve and seeking diverse sources of funding. The current funding mix consists of retail natice accounts, sight and term
deposits raised in the UK, a private/public securitisation program, borrowing from the Bank of England through the TFSME (Term Funding Scheme
for Small and Medium Sized Enterprises) and bank loans from the parent company in France. Since 2021, RCI UK has access to the Discount
Window Facility with the Bank of England improving further the available funding sources in a stress situation and strengthening the liquidity
management framework.

Operational Resilience

Operational resilience is the ability of the business to prevent, adapt and respond to, recover and learn from operational disruption. It is the early
and effective recovery from an operational disruption (not just IT-related) event, that causes harm to customers, other market participants and or
the reputation/viability of RCI UK. It is essential to ensure the continuity of business services or economic functions, and their sustained
availability to customers. These services and functions therefore need to be resilient, robust and capable of being provided in periods of stress
by building operational resilience.

RC! UK has a detailed framework and operational plan to prevent, adapt, respond, recover and learn from the risk of operational disruption. RC|
UK adopted a path of continuous improvements and carries on with ongoing strengthening the processes.

The RCI UK Executive Committee as well as the Board regularly reviews the key risks affecting its operations together with a horizon scan of
potential emerging risks.

a) Physical risks

Climate-related physical risks are the potential of large financial losses due to changes in climate and weather-related events
(heatwaves, droughts, floods, storms and sea level rise).

RCI UK is exposed to the automotive industry through its provision of credit to manufacturers, firms in the supply chain, dealerships
and directly to consumers — a key physical risk is damage to stock held at dealerships where legal ownership lies with RCI UK.

b) Transition risks

Climate-related transition risks can arise from the process of adjustment towards a low-carbon economy. Changes in policy,
technology and sentiment could create future credit risk for RCI UK as costs and opportunities become apparent.

Over the next ten years there will be a significant transition in the car market from internal combustion engine (ICE) cars to alternative
fuel vehicles (AFVs) — the pace of transition could lead to significant uncertainty which poses risks to the sustainability of businesses
in the automotive supply chain.

The automotive industry is one of the sectors most impacted by transition risks due to policy changes aiming to reduce the sector's
contribution to global emissions - transport accounts for 26% of greenhouse gas emissions in the UK, the largest single source of
emissions.

Compliance risk
The sectors in which RCI UK operates are subject to extensive regulatory and legislative requirements.

Compliance Risk is defined as ‘the risk of legal or regulatory sanctions, material financial loss, or loss to reputation a bank may suffer as a result
of its failure to comply with laws, regulations, rules, related self-regulatory organisation standards and codes of conduct applicable to its banking
activities.’

On an ongoing basis it is necessary for RCI UK to ensure adherence to regulatory standards (which includes conduct) to reduce the inherent risk
of penalties and material loss from failure to act in accordance with industry laws and regulations, internal policies and procedures, and/or
prescribed practices.

The Compliance Function within RC! UK promotes a compliance culture as a key part of the overall regulatory control framework as outlined in
the Risk Management Framework, with compliance activities focused upon the clear identification of new regulatory requirements and the impact
assessment on RCI UK operations, providing compliance expertise by way of advice to support risk management and mitigation, and the efficient
execution of compliance monitoring and assurance to assess the adequacy and effectiveness of the measures and procedures put in place to
ensure compliance with the requirements of the requlatory system and delivery of good customer outcomes.

This includes (but not limited to) the review of application of relevant and applicable policies and procedures, reporting and interrogation of data
and how this information is used to assess risks and/or emerging risks and drive improvements, first line testing and controls to assure regulatory
adherence and reasonable avoidance of causing foreseeable customer harm, and risk governance structures to ensure sufficient Senior
Management oversight. :

Conduct risk

This is the risk that customers experience detriment from the way in which RCI UK conducts its activities, which applies equally to borrowing
customers of RCI FS and savings customers making deposits with RCI Bank.

The conduct risk management framework which forms part of the Risk Management Framework ensures that RCI UK treats its customers fairly
and delivers good customer outcomes. It is outcome focused and covers some of the following key areas:

- Culture - established through ensuring there is investment in training, robust governance and oversight throughout the three lines of
defence, an effective risk assessment framework, and positive staff performance management and compliant reward and incentive
schemes.

- Customer needs and targeting — by ensuring there are adequate measures in place to review and assess that products and services
offer fair value, meet the demands and needs of customers, and deliver benefits as expected.

- Customer information and communication — by ensuring customers understand RCI UK products to be able to make timely and informed
decisions.

- Product performance and customer service — by ensuring products and services are innovative and continually improved that in turn, it
helps customers to achieve their financial objectives and reasonably avoids causing foreseeable customer harm through unnecessary
barriers and sludge practices and/or leads to complaints.

Conduct Risk is overseen by the Compliance Department and monitored via Key Risk indicators assessing the conduct risk position on a monthly
basis. Individual conduct risk appetite breaches are recorded and reported via relevant governance together with the associated actions for
remediation.

Focus on managing conduct risk and delivery of good outcomes for customers, including those with vulnerable characteristics, as well as ensuring

measures are in place to avoid causing foreseeable harm continues to be implemented and embedded in RCI UK to ensure ongoing compliance
with the Consumer Duty requirements which came into effect on 31 July 2023.

Actions Taken

Energy Efficiency: The building in which we operate already has a range of energy efficiency features, including an air source heat pump and
LED (light emitting diode) lighting. Our Sustainability Team, working closely with our CEO, is constantly looking to improve the environmental
and energy impact of our business. An electricity usage review we began in 2022 was extended during 2023. This has provided valuable
additional insights into our energy usage. We installed the first phase of electricity monitoring equipment, and the associated software has
enabled real time electricity consumption measurement and analysis. Starting in August 2023, a series of floor closure tests were initiated at the
RCI UK building which led to tangible energy reductions. Since September, floor closures have been implemented on Fridays as part of our
energy saving plans.

Employee: We have undertaken a number of campus-based activities to support our employee's education and transition to more sustainable
practices. To help employees’ transition to EV or Hybrid models, we offer free charging at our offices, a discount on home EV charging
infrastructure and offer incentivised rates for electrified vehicles on our company car scheme. During 2022 we commissioned 24 new EV
charging points at our offices. We continued to encourage the uptake of BEV's (battery electric vehicles) and full hybrid vehicles by employees
offering them an extended choice of vehicles. in November 2023 following the creation of the Climate Steering Group, ‘Green incentive’
Battery EV options were added to every banding of the ECOP and loan plan lists for a trial period. Since this initiative has started 48 employees
have signed up, increasing our BEV fleet to 88 units. 99% of all ECOP/loan plan vehicles are BEV or full hybrid.

In 2022 we rolled out Carbon Literacy Training, we are now a Bronze Certified Carbon literate organisation. In addition, in October 2023 a new
climate change awareness training ‘Climate Fresk was rolled out centrally, with 2 facilitators now trained. In 2023, we held our first World
Environment Day event with speakers from both our manufacturer partners, Renault, and Nissan. We also offered employees the opportunity to
offset their personal footprint via our benefits system and continue to give employees the choice of which projects we offset our company
footprint with.

Suppliers: Environmental and climate considerations have been made in the supplier tender process, where ESG credentials are assessed and
required in RFP's.

Customer: Along with the extensive list of actions we have achieved internally, we continue to develop products that will help customers
transition to greener mobility solutions: Through our partnership with Mobilize, we now offer customers the ability to fund EV charging
infrastructure along with their finance agreement when they purchase an EV vehicle with us. We also offer Mobilize Charge Pass, a charging
card that is both the key and wallet to access over 28,000 charging points in the UK. As well as this, in 2022 our subscription platform Bipi was
launched which makes EVs more accessible to customers. Finally, our award-winning E-Volve Savings account deposits, fund pure EVs and
charging facilities with 1.6k customers and £79.2m of deposits (December 2023).

Other Actions: We have a comprehensive defleet action plan and are engaging with our key suppliers to support this transition. Internal
paperless sign-off and HR processes, as well as paperless options for customers via the portal and across our defleet activity, has significantly
reduced our printing and postage footprint. We also consider an organisation's Climate credentials during the tender process to ensure they are
aligned with our priorities.

Results

In 2023, our greenhouse gas emissions (as defined under the SECR regulations) rose by 7% vs 2022. This was mainly due to business mileage
increasing as we moved beyond pre- Covid-19 levels of normality. Despite this, when comparing against our reported greenhouse gas
emissions for 2019 they have decreased by 49% driven by actions we have taken to be more carbon efficient as a business.

(ii) Classification and measurement
(i) Financial assets

There are three principal classification categories for financial assets: measured at amortised cost, fair value through other
comprehensive income (FVOCI) and fair value through profit or loss (FVTPL).

To classify financial assets the Group performs two assessments to evaluate the business model in which financial assets are
managed and their cash flow characteristics. The business mode! assessment is typically performed first, as the financial assets
managed on a fair value basis are classified as FVTPL and are not subject to cash flow characteristics test.

Business model assessment:

The ‘business model assessment determines whether the Group's objective is to generate cash flows from collecting contractual
cash flows, or by both collecting contractual cash flows and selling financial assets. The assessment is performed at a portfolio level
as this best reflects the way business is managed and how information is provided to Management. The assessment is based on
Management's expectations. If cash flows are realised in a manner that is different from the original expectation, the classification of
the remaining assets in that portfolio is not changed but such information is used when assessing new financial assets going
forward.

Cash flow characteristics test:

The assessment of cash flow characteristics determines whether the contractual cash flows of the financial asset are solely
payments of principal and interest (SPPI) and is referred to as the 'SPPI test’. For the purposes of the SPPI test, principal is defined
as the fair value of the financial asset at initial recognition. Interest is defined as consideration for the time value of money and credit
risk associated with the principal amount outstanding and for other basic lending risks and costs (e.g. liquidity risk and
administrative costs), as well as a reasonable profit margin. The SPPI test is performed at an instrument level based on the
contractual terms of the instrument at initial recognition.

Based on the two assessments, financial assets are classified as follows:

Amortised cost: A financial asset is classified as measured at amortised cost if it is held within a business model whose objective is
to hold the assets to collect contractual cashflows; and its contractual terms give rise on specified dates to cash flows that are SPPI.

FVOCI: A financial asset is classified as FVOC! if it is held within a business model whose objective is achieved by both collecting
contractual cash flows and selling financial assets; and its contractual terms give rise on specified dates to cash flows that are SPPI.

FVTPL: Financial assets that are managed on a fair value.basis, or not classified as measured at amortised cost or FVOCI as per
above, are classified as FVTPL. This includes all derivative financial assets.

On initial recognition, the Group may irrevocably designate a financial asset that otherwise meets the requirements to be classified
as measured at amortised cost or FVOCI as FVTPL if doing so eliminates or significantly reduces an accounting mismatch that
would otherwise arise.

e Financial assets at FVTPL: subsequently measured at fair value. Net gains and losses, including any interest or dividend
income, are recognised in the Statement of Profit or Loss.

e Financial assets at amortised cost: subsequently measured at amortised cost using the effective interest rate method.

Measurement of Expected Credit Losses (ECLs)

Impairment of financial assets is calculated using a forward looking ECL model. The Group records an allowance for ECLs for all
financial assets that are not held at FVTPL, together with an allowance for ECLs for loan commitments. ECLs are an unbiased
probability-weighted estimate of credit losses determined by evaluating a range of possible outcomes. They are measured in a manner
that reflects the time value of money and uses reasonable and supportable information that is available without undue cost or effort
at the reporting date about past events, current conditions and forecasts of future economic conditions.

Measurement of ECLs is based on the ‘stage’ of the financial asset, based on changes in credit risk occurring since the assets initial
recagnition as explained below:

e Stage 1: when a financial asset is first recognised, it is assigned to Stage 1. If there is no significant increase in credit risk
from initial recognition the financial asset remains in Stage 1. Stage 1 also includes financial assets where the credit risk
has improved, and the financial asset has been reclassified back from Stage 2. For financial assets in Stage 1, a 12-month
ECL is recognised. The expected credit loss is calculated by multiplying the default probability (PD) by the outstanding
amount that will be exposed to the default using the contractual schedule (EAD), the loss ratio in case of default (LGD) and
a discounting factor.

e Stage 2: when a financial asset shows a significant increase in credit risk from initial recognition it is moved to Stage 2.
Stage 2 also includes financial assets where the credit risk has improved, and the financial asset has been reclassified back
from Stage 3. A similar formula as in Stage 1 is used but a lifetime ECL is recognised for Stage 2 financial assets.

e — Stage 3: when there is objective evidence of impairment and the financial asset is considered to be in default, or otherwise
credit-impaired, it is moved to Stage 3. For financial assets in Stage 3, a lifetime ECL is recognised and is calculated by the
outstanding amaunt at reporting date (EAD) by the LGD.

e Purchased or originated credit-impaired (POCI): POCI assets are financial assets that are credit-impaired on initial
recognition. On initial recognition they are recorded at fair value. ECLs are only recognised or released to the extent that
there is a subsequent change in the ECLs. Their ECL is measured on a lifetime basis. The Group had no POC! assets as
at 31 December 2023 (2022: Nil).

In relation to the above:

e Lifetime ECL: defined as ECLs that result from all possible default events over the expected life of a financial instrument.
e  12-month ECL: defined as the portion of lifetime ECL that will result if a default occurs in the 12 months after the reporting
date, weighted by the probability of that default occurring.

For loan commitments, where the loan commitment relates to the undrawn component of a facility, itis assigned to the same stage
as the drawn component of the facility. For the loans underwritten but not yet originated, the loan commitment is assigned to Stage 1.

Asummary of ECL measurement is as follows:

e Financial assets that are not credit-impaired at the reporting date: is the present value of all cash shortfalls. Cash shortfalls
are the difference between the contractual cash flows due to the Group and the cash flows that the Group expects to receive.

e _ Financial assets that are credit-impaired at the reporting date: is the difference between the gross carrying amount and the
present value of estimated future cash flows.

e Loan commitments: is the present value of the difference between the contractual cash flows that are due to the Group if
the commitment is drawn down and the cash flows the Group expects to receive. A credit conversion factor (CCF) to a
balance sheet item is included in the calculation.

Credit-impaired is defined by the Group as a financial asset in Stage 3 as detailed above.

Credit risk is defined as the risk of default on a financial asset including finance lease receivables that may arise from a borrower

When determining whether the risk of default on loans and advances to customers has increased significantly since initial recognition,

(ii) Calculation of expected credit losses:

ECLs are the discounted product of the probability of default (PD), exposure at default (EAD) and loss given default (LGD), detailed
below. ECLs are determined by projecting the PD, EAD and LGD for each future month for each exposure. The three components
are multiplied together and adjusted to reflect forward looking information. This calculates an ECL for each future month, which is then
discounted back to the reporting date and summed. The discount rate used in the ECL calculation is the original effective interest rate
or an approximation thereof.

Probability of default ("PD”) is an estimate subject to a degree of uncertainty of the likelihood of default over a given time horizon. A
default may only happen at a certain time over the assessed period, if the facility has not been previously derecognised and is still in
the portfolio.

The current risk grading framework for Retail customers consists of 10 grades for the sound portfolio and one for default. Corporate

Exposure at default ("EAD") is an estimate of the exposure at a future default date, taking into account expected changes in the
exposure after the reporting date, including repayments of principal and interest, whether scheduled by contract or otherwise, expected
drawdowns on committed facilities, and accrued interest from missed payments.

EAD is designed to address increases in utilisation of committed limits and unpaid interest and fees that the Group would ordinarily
expect to observe to the point of default, or through to the point of realisation of the collateral.

Loss given default ("LGD") is an estimate subject to a degree of uncertainty of the loss arising in the case where a default occurs at a
given time. It is based on the difference between the contractual cash flows due and those that the Group would expect to receive,
including from the realisation of any collateral. It is expressed as a percentage of the EAD.

Economic losses are estimated using discounted recovery flows for Retail customers and corporates, or debt write-offs for the car
dealers, on the basis of historical data generally going back 5 years. Recovery costs are factored in according to the management
phases involved. After analysis, transactions have been grouped into segments representing homogeneous joss levels. The
quantifying of these losses per segment results from a statistical model, the main vectors of which are analysis of recoveries per
generation and the speed of collection.

(iii) | Forward-looking information

IFRS 9 introduces into the credit risk-related expected loss (ECL) calculation the notion of forward looking. Through this notion, new
requirements in terms of monitoring and measuring credit risk are introduced with the use of forward-looking data, in particular of
macroeconomic type.

The incorporation of forward-looking data is not intended to determine a prudential margin on the amount of provisions. It mainly
concerns taking account of the fact that past observations do not necessarily reflect future expectations and consequently adjustments
are necessary to the amount of the provision determined on the basis of parameters calibrated exclusively on a historic basis. Such
adjustments of the amount of the provision can be made either upwards or downwards and must be duly documented.

Leases

The group as lessor

Certain leases for which the Group is a lessor are classified operating leases. Whenever the terms of the lease transfer substantially
all the risks and rewards of ownership to the lessee, the contract is classified as a finance lease. All other leases are classified as
operating leases.

There are three types of leases classified as operating lease: Contract Hire Vehicles, Lease Hire Vehicles, and Batteries.

Contract Hire assets are typically for a term between 24 and 60 months where the customer will pay a fixed rental monthly until the
end of the agreement, at which point the vehicle will be returned to the Group, classified as inventory and subsequently re-
marketed. These assets are classified as operating leases since most of the risks and rewards are not transferred to a third party
outside the Group, and the lease term is far shorter than the economic life of the vehicles, which is put by the Groupe Renault at an
estimated seven or eight years, depending on the type of vehicle.

Lease Hire assets are typically for a term between 24 and 60 months where the customer will pay a fixed rental monthly until the
end of the agreement, at which point the customer will act as the Group's agent and sell the vehicle to a third party, typically a
dealer. These assets are classified as Lease hire assets and will typically have a useful economic life estimated at seven or eight
years, depending on the type of vehicle.

Battery lease assets have an expected life between 7 and 10 years. The customer will pay a fixed rental monthly for as long as they
are in possession of the vehicle. If the custamer sells the vehicle to a third party, that party has the choice of either continuing with
the fixed rental monthly or purchasing the battery outright from the Group. When a battery reaches the end of its useful life, it's
returned to the Group for recycling.

The classification of battery leases for electric vehicles as operating leases is justified by the fact that the Group retains the risks
and rewards incidental to ownership throughout the automobile life of the batteries, which is put at between seven and ten years,
and so is much longer than the lease agreements.

When assets are held subject to operating leases, they are initially measured at fair value being the cost and are subsequently held
at cost less accumulated depreciation.

The assets are depreciated down to their estimated residual values on a straight-line basis over the lease term. Lease rental income
is recognised on a straight-line basis over the lease term and is recognised in the Statement of Profit or Loss in “Operating Lease
Rentals’.

As per IFRS 16, the Group records an adjustment in depreciation over the remaining lease term when there is an indication that the
residual value at the end of the term might be below the projected net book value. This adjustment is subject to uncertainty and is
calculated based on forecasts provided by an external data provider which vary regularly and whose projected value might be
different than the actual result and proceeds generated by the sale of the vehicles.

The Group as lessee

The Group assesses whether a contract is or contains a lease, at inception of the contract. The Group applies lessee accounting
under IFRS 16 to its property leases and recognises a right-of-use asset and a corresponding lease liability with respect to these
lease arrangements in which it is the lessee.

The lease liability is initially measured at the present value of the lease payments that are not paid at the commencement date,
discounted by using the rate implicit in the lease. If this rate cannot be readily determined, the Group uses its incremental borrowing
rate.

Lease payments included in the measurement of the lease liability comprise fixed lease payments (including in-substance fixed
payments), less any lease incentives receivable.

The lease liability is presented as part of “Other Liabilities” in the consolidated balance sheet.

The lease liability is subsequently measured by increasing the carrying amount to reflect interest on the lease liability (using the
effective interest method) and by reducing the carrying amount to reflect the lease payments made.

The Group remeasures the lease liability (and makes a corresponding adjustment to the related right-of-use asset) whenever a
lease contract is modified and the lease modification is not accounted for as a separate lease, in which case the lease liability is
remeasured based on the lease term of the modified lease by discounting the revised lease payments using a revised discount rate
at the effective date of the modification.

The Group did not make any such adjustments during the periods presented.

The right-of-use assets comprise the initial measurement of the corresponding lease liability, lease payments made at or before the
commencement day, less any lease incentives received and any initial direct costs. They are subsequently measured at cost less

Borrowings including debt are recognised initially at fair value, net of transaction costs incurred. Borrowings are subsequently

Q)

Pension

For defined benefit pension schemes, the amounts charged to operating profit are the current service costs and gains and losses on
settlements and curtailments. They are included as part of staff costs. Past service costs are recognised immediately in the Statement
of Profit or Loss if the benefits have vested. If the benefits have not vested immediately, the costs are recognised over the period until
vesting occurs. The interest cost and the expected return on assets are shown as a net amount within operating expenses.

Actuarial gains and losses are recognised in full in the Statement of Other Comprehensive Income in the periods in which they arise.

Defined benefit pension schemes are funded, with the assets of the scheme held independently from those of the Company, in
separate trustee-administered funds. Pension scheme assets are measured at fair value and liabilities are measured on an actuarial
basis using the projected unit method and discounted at a rate equivalent to the current rate of return on a high-quality corporate bond
of equivalent currency and term to the scheme liabilities. The actuarial valuations are obtained at least triennially and are updated at
each balance sheet date. The resulting defined benefit asset or liability is presented separately after other current assets or other
current liabilities on the face of the balance sheet.

For defined contribution schemes, the amount charged to the Statement of Profit or Loss in respect of pension costs and other post-
retirement benefits is the contributions payable in the year. Differences between contributions payable in the year and contributions
actually paid are shown as either accruals or prepayments in the balance sheet.

Income tax on the profit or loss for the year comprises current and deferred tax. Income tax is recognised in the Statement of Profit

recognised in the Statement of Other Comprehensive Income or in the Statement of Changes in Equity.

Deferred tax is provided using the balance sheet liability method, providing for temporary differences between the carrying amounts
of assets and liabilities for financial reporting purposes and the amounts used for taxation purposes. The amount of deferred tax
provided is based on the expected manner of realisation or settlement of the carrying amount of assets and liabilities.

Deferred tax assets and liabilities are measured at the tax rates that are expected to apply in the year when the asset is realised or
the liability is settled, based on tax rates (and tax laws) that have been enacted or substantively enacted at the reporting date.

Deferred tax assets have been recognised to the extent that it is probable that future taxable profit will be available against which
they can be utilised. Deferred tax assets are reviewed at each reporting date and are reduced to the extent that it is no longer
probable that the related tax benefit will be realised.

31 ‘Risk and risk management

In the following review of risk and risk management any reference to the Group should be deemed to apply equally to the Compa ny unless
otherwise stated. Tables show the Company and the Group position separately wherever appropriate.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the risk of suffering financial loss should borrowers or counterparties default on their contractual obligations to the Group due to lack
of willingness to pay and/or lack of ability to pay. These risks are overseen by the Board Risk Committee, Risk and Compliance Committee and
by the Asset and Liability Committee (ALCO). This risk has two main components:

« Customer credit risk (from core lending activity to retail and wholesale borrowers); and
e Treasury credit risk (from Treasury activity).

The Group's treasury credit risk exposure is limited to:

e Overnight and short-term deposits with correspondent banks, including Bank of England and the Group's parent, RCI Banque SA
e Treasury assets managed as part of the Group's liquidity reserve.

As at 31 December 2023, these comprised Bank of England reserve and deposits with UK clearing banks.

Credit risk approval process

Credit risk and associated approval processes are outlined in the Group's Lending Policy. Management of credit risk is well-established and
governed directly by written procedures. In all cases credit risk will be approved only when it is within the Group's risk appetite as set out by the
UK Board in the Risk Appetite Statement.

For customer credit risk, separate approval processes exist for wholesale and retail lending and are managed by the Credit and Lending
department in line with specific credit policy and procedures.

The majority of retail lending is auto approved via a bespoke score card linked to UK Credit Bureaux. Should an application fail then it is forwarded
for manual underwriting. All Wholesale (dealer) lending is manually underwritten.

For treasury credit risk, the Lending Policy and Risk Appetite Statement provide guidance on approvals and on lending limits. Limits are agreed
by the Board and put in place for non-UK government exposure.

Credit monitoring

Credit risk is monitored through review by both Risk and Compliance and Board Risk committees, monthly by commercial review in partnership
with RCI Banque SA, and through monthly management information linked to financial performance (including accounting cost of risk). Credit
risks identified by Risk Appetite metrics and KRIs are also circulated monthly to the Group's Board. Large credit exposures are monitored on a
daily basis and must at all times be within regulatory and Board-approved limits.

Credit risk including levels of default and arrears is also monitored through the Group's regulatory reporting to the PRA.

Expected credit losses (ECLs)

Impairment of financial assets is calculated using a forward-looking expected credit loss (ECL) model. The Group records an allowance for
ECLs for all financial assets not held at FVTPL, together with an allowance for ECLs for loan commitments where applicable. The Group's
accounting policy is detailed in Note 1 J) of the financial statements.

Liquidity risk

Liquidity risk is the risk that the Group is unable to meet its current and future financial obligations as they fall due, or is only able to do so at
excessive cost.

The Group has, therefore, developed comprehensive funding and liquidity policies to ensure that liquidity risk management is appropriate and
fits to the size and complexity of the business. Key source of liquidity risk is the maturity mismatch arising from the ultimate business strategy,
i.e. the Group partially funds fixed term retail auto loans by contractually short-term retail deposits. Although sight deposits are considered
behaviourally long-term, stable funding, liquidity risk might arise if outflows accelerate in a liquidity stress situation. The Group's strategy is to
maintain a prudent funding profile, to monitor the evolution of the liquidity position on daily basis including early warning indicators and to hold
sufficient amount and quality of liquidity buffer to cover outflows that might occur in a severe liquidity stress situation.

The Group's Treasury and ALM function is responsible for the day-to-day management of the Group's liquidity and funding with the delegated
authority from Assets and Liabilities Committee (ALCO). The Board sets thresholds and limits over the adequate level of liquidity reserve and
against further funding and liquidity indicators, reviewing these at least annually. Compliance with these limits is monitored by Finance and
independently reviewed by Risk.

For regulatory purposes, the Group reports its liquidity position against its liquidity coverage ratio (LCR), net stable funding ratio (NSFR) and
other regulatory returns.

Stress testing is a major component of the liquidity risk management, and the Group has developed a set of scenarios covering a range of market
wide and firm specific stress factors. A comprehensive stress testing exercise is conducted at least annually, and the methodology is incorporated
into the Group's ILAAP model. Stress testing results are updated and reviewed monthly. The output of stress testing is reported to the Board,
Board Risk Committee and to the ALCO.

The Group also maintains a Liquidity Contingency Plan which provides an action plan including a set of available management responses to be
implemented in case of a liquidity stress situation.

Market risk

Market risk is the risk that the value of, or income arising from, the Group's assets and liabilities change as a result of changes in market prices,
the principal element being interest rate risk.

The Group is not exposed to traded market risk as it does not have a trading book.
Treasury security positions consists of short-term treasury bills only and therefore associated interest rate risk is considered not material.

The Group's Treasury function is responsible for managing the Group's exposure to all aspects of market risk within the operational limits set
out in the Group's risk appetite defined by the Board Risk Committee.

Basis risk
Basis risk arises where, although repricing structure of assets and liabilities are matched, they might be linked to a different underlying reference

rate. The Group has minimal exposure to basis risk due to the high correlation between short-term reference rates. The Group is exposed to
basis risk in case of significant uncorrelation of Bank of England and SONIA rates which are monitored by ALCO on a regular basis.

Foreign exchange risk

All Group assets and liabilities are denominated in GBP — no foreign exchange risk arises other than a limited number of non-GBP invoices
received from suppliers.

Residual value risk on Operating Lease Assets

The end of contract residual value (RV) of an operating !ease vehicle is important in determining the profitability of the contract relating to that
vehicle. RVs are set by reference to the vehicle type and by using predictive and historical data furnished by an independent external
benchmark (CAP) and internal historical performance data. However, the future is unknown, and the Group is exposed to the risk that future
RVs could fall, leading to a reduction in profitability.

In order to manage this risk, the Group regularly reviews its RV estimates. The Group does this by using its own data obtained from vehicle
disposals through auction sales and other sales routes, and by using industry-wide data obtained from CAP Monitor. The Group is then able to
react quickly to any falls in RVs. This ensures that the Group maintains its profitability for this revenue stream.

Residual value risk PCP and Hire Purchase Contracts

The Group bears indirect residual value risk on its PCP and Hire Purchase products. For the PCP products, guaranteed future values (GFV) are
set providing customers with the option to return the vehicle at the end of the contract at this GFV.

For both the PCP and Hire Purchase products, customers may exercise their right under the Consumer Credit Act (1974) to return the vehicle to
RCI and terminate the contract. The Group is exposed to residual value risk on these retumed vehicles.

Where applicable, GFVs are set and managed using the same methodology for setting RVs as described in the Residual Value Risk on Operating
Lease Assets section above. As part of the management of this risk the Group estimates end of contract return rates and voluntary termination
rates which impact the level of provisions held for residual value risk.
