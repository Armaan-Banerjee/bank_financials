Strategic report

The directors are pleased to present their Strategic Report of JN Bank UK Ltd (“the Bank”) For the
year ended 31 March 2025.

Key highlights

It has been a challenging year for the Bank and its customers. Our customers have had to contend
with the aftermath of the cost-of-living squeeze on their Finances and a continued economic
uncertainty From _a change in government. The Bank reacted to the changing conditions by
restructuring the balance sheet.

Change in Majority Ownership

In the year new investors were sought to supplement the existing investment by The Jamaica
National Group. The result of which were that, after the receipt of regulatory change in control
permissions, the Bank issued in the year, £9.1m of ordinary shares to Step One Money UK Limited
(“SOMU”") along with £15.9m of share premium. At the same time SOMU acquired additional
ordinary shares in the Bank from JN Financial Group Limited (“JNFG”). As a result, the immediate
controlling company changed to SOMU on 30 September 2024. This transaction enables the Bank
to continue its journey to profitability and access additional growth capital From a wider investor
base in future periods.

Portfolio Transactions and Product Mix

This new investment enabled the Bank to expand its operations and take on Further deposits which
in turn, allowed the Bank to grow its loan book from £83.1m to £277.3m, an increase of 234%. The
Bank decided to de-risk its product mix during the year through the purchase and sale of portfolios.
This was a strategic decision made by management to strengthen the loan book and to align with
business objectives.

In June 2024, the Bank purchased a secured loan portfolio of £129.1m from the Step One Finance
Limited. (“SOFL”). The existing Forward flow agreement with SOFL resulted in a further €£59.4m of
new secured lending (2024: €20.6m). These agreements helped bring the total year-end secured
loan balance of £226.7m (2024: £78.9m).

In June 2024, the Bank also sold a defaulted unsecured portfolio with a carrying value of £15.7m for
£3.0m to an external third party. Unsecured retail loans were issued for £34.9m during the year
(2024: £2.3m). In December 2024, the Bank purchased a portfolio From JNFG For a price of £20.7m.
The year-end unsecured loan balance was £50.6m (2024: £4.5m).

In 2025, the Bank's net interest income was £8.4m (2024: £5.3m) and an improving loss of £9.6m For
the year (2024: £11.0m). The losses have continued as the loan book remains below critical mass to
cover costs.

New Leadership and People Development

The Bank appointed Christopher Waring as Chief Executive Officer during the year as part of its
accelerating growth strategy. Chris is an experienced UK banking leader with substantial expertise
in leading specialist Financial service businesses. He joined JN Bank UK in early 2023 as Chief
Commercial Officer, bringing with him a wealth of experience in financial services. With his
extensive background, Chris is now poised to lead JN Bank UK into its next stage of growth, Focused
on delivering exceptional value and fostering long-term relationships with customers.

Business model

The business model of the Bank is to provide unsecured personal lending and acquire First and
second charge secured mortgages, mainly in the retail market. The Bank services segments which
may be typically underserved by the larger banks. Our unsecured lending products are
differentiated by straightforward products, that offer us a competitive advantage. The Bank
recognises the importance of a high level of data analysis and risk management expertise and
continues to invest in building these capabilities. Lending is originated through partners and is
Funded by offering simple, competitively priced online savings products to personal customers.

Our model is underpinned by a commitment to the disciplines of risk management, regulatory
compliance and governance. Risk appetite and culture are set by the Board to provide the
appropriate tone From the top. ,

Our values

We exist to work For our customers and their communities. We understand the importance of
treating everyone with compassion and respect, that everyone has a story, and that is why helping
our customers achieve their goals is at our heart.

Our approach is simple: we provide financial products that are fair, transparent, and easy to
understand. With a history and continued association with The Jamaica National Group, we have
inherited a rich customer service culture shaped by the values of a mutual organisation. We embrace
the diversity of our customers and are committed to building a bank Founded on inclusion, respect,
and genuine care. We are deeply invested in the well-being of our customers, their unique needs,
and the environment we share.

Principal risks and uncertainties

Financial institutions operate in a competitive and highly regulated environment and are subject to
economic and other uncertainties. The management of risk is therefore central to the continuing
success of the Bank.

The Board is responsible for ensuring that the Bank’s risk management framework and system of
internal controls is appropriate and are designed to enable the Bank to achieve its objectives within
its agreed risk appetite: To do this, the Bank bases its risk management Framework on the ‘three
lines of defence’ model. The ‘first line’ of defence is the checks and controls utilised by operational
staff directly engaged in the management of the risks in their area of activity. The ‘second line’ is
the assurance provided by the Bank’s Risk Management and Compliance function. The ‘third line’ is
the independent internal audit Function which undertakes reviews - These reports are regularly
provided to management and the Board Audit and Board Risk Committees.

d.1 Measurement of ECLs

ECLs are an unbiased probability-weighted estimate of the present value of credit losses, taking
account of Forward-looking information. ECLs are measured as the difference between contractual
cash flows and expected cash Flows arising From an asset, discounted at the asset’s effective
interest rate,

When measuring ECLs, the Bank assesses the probability of default (“PD”), the expected exposure
at the time of default, exposure at default (EAD), and the loss that is expected to arise in the event
of default, loss given default (LGD). The maximum period considered is the maximum contractual
period over which the Bank is exposed to credit risk. The Bank typically assesses ECLs on an
individual asset basis.

h) interest

h.1  — Effective interest rate

Interest income and expense are recognised in profit or loss using the effective interest method.
The ‘effective interest rate’ is the rate that exactly discounts estimated Future cash payments or
receipts through the expected life of the financial asset or financial liability to the gross carrying
amount of the financial asset or Financial liability.

When calculating the effective interest rate for financial instruments the Bank estimates Future
cash flows considering all contractual terms of the Financial instrument, but not ECLs.

The calculation of the effective interest rate includes transaction costs and fees earned that are an
integral part of the effective interest rate. Transaction costs include incremental costs that are
directly attributable to the acquisition or issue of a financial asset or financial liability.

A key source of uncertainty is the rate of early settlement of loans (prepayments). A higher rate of
prepayments can change the estimated life of the loan and hence the EIR. Prepayments are
currently not easy to predict and there is insufficient observable data to apply a portfolio treatment
for prepayments. The Bank therefore apportions costs over the contractual life of customer loans
adjusted For behavioural activity. Any EIR balance resulting from capitalised costs is written off on
settlement of loan ahead of the contractual date.

The Bank has capitalised £7.9m (2024: €3.3m) of transaction costs which are incremental and
directly attributable to the issue of customer loans, such as fees and commissions paid to
aggregators over the expected life of the customer loan. The Bank expensed £6.2m (2024: £3.8m)
of transaction costs attributable to the effective interest rate treatment during the year ended 31
March 2025.

h.2.  Amortised cost and gross carrying amount

The ‘amortised cost’ of a Financial asset or financial liability is the amount at which the financial asset
or Financial liability is measured on initial recognition minus the principal repayments, plus or minus
the cumulative amortisation using the effective interest method of any difference between that
initial amount and the maturity amount and, For financial assets, adjusted For any expected credit
loss allowance. The ‘gross carrying amount of a Financial asset’ is the amortised cost of a Financial
asset before adjusting for any expected credit loss allowance.

IFRS9 requires interest earned from loans to be measured under the EIR method. The effective
interest rate (EIR) is the rate that exactly discounts estimated Future cashflows to the carrying value
of the loans. The EIR method uses a discounted cashflow model to spread the interest and costs
over the estimated life of the asset.

In calculating interest income, the effective interest rate is applied to the gross carrying amount of
the asset (when the asset is not credit-impaired).

n) Taxation

Tax on the profit or loss for the year comprises current and deferred tax. Tax is recognised in the
income statement to the extent that it relates to items recognised directly in the statement of
profit and loss. Income tax relating to items recognised directly in equity or other comprehensive
income is recognised in equity or other comprehensive income respectively and not in the
Statement of Profit or Loss

Current tax is the expected tax payable or receivable on the taxable income or loss For the year,
using tax rates enacted or substantively enacted at the reporting date, and any adjustment to tax
payable in respect of previous years.

Deferred tax is provided on temporary differences between the carrying amounts of assets and
liabilities for financial reporting purposes and the amounts used for taxation purposes. The amount
of deferred tax provided is based on the expected manner of realisation or settlement of the
carrying amount of assets and liabilities, using tax rates enacted or substantively enacted at the
reporting date.

The recognition of a deferred tax asset requires management to make assumptions concerning
Future taxable profits against which deferred tax assets can be recovered.

Deferred tax assets are recognised only when it is probable that taxable profits will be available
against which the deferred tax asset can be utilised. The Bank has considered the recoverability of
deferred tax assets taking into account the Following:

the availability of sufficient taxable temporary differences.

the probability that the entity will have sufficient taxable profits in the foreseeable future,
in the same period as the reversal of the deductible temporary difference or in the periods
into which a'tax loss can be carried back or forward; and

the availability of tax planning opportunities that allow the recovery of deferred tax assets.

Valuation techniques ,

Fair values are determined using the following Fair value hierarchy that reflects the significance of
the inputs in measuring Fair value:

Level 1: Quoted prices for identical instruments
Level 2: Directly observable market inputs other than Level 1 inputs
Level 3: Inputs not based on observable market data

POCI Loans — Inputs not based on observable market data. However, on initial recognition, these
were credit impaired. The lifetime ECL’s are incorporated as part of the loan balance.

(i) Cash and cash equivalents

The fair values of cash.and cash equivalents, which are carried at amortised cost are not considered
to be materially different From their carrying values. This is considered is reached due to their short-
term nature and the use of highly rated counterparties. As these are cash balances, they are
considered to be directly observable and considered to be level 2 measurements.

(ii) Loans to customers

To assess the likely fair value of the Bank's loan assets in the absence of a liquid market, the
directors have considered the estimated cash Flows expected to arise From the Bank's investments
in its loans to customers based on a mixture of market-based inputs, such as rates and pricing and
non-market based inputs such as redemption rates. Given the mixture of observable and non-
observable inputs these are considered to be level 3 measurements.

(iii) Investment Securities

The fair value of investment securities is disclosed by reference to market prices. Investment
securities, being government debt securities, are financial instruments issued by sovereign
governments and include both long-term bonds and short-term bills with fixed or Floating rate
interest payments. These instruments are generally highly liquid and traded in active markets
resulting in a Level 1 classification. When active market prices are not available, the Bank uses
discounted cash Flow models with observable market inputs of similar instruments and bond prices
to estimate future index levels and extrapolating yields outside the range of active market trading,
in which instances the Bank classifies those securities as Level 2. The Bank does not have Level 2 or
Level 3 government securities where valuation inputs would be unobservable.

(iv) Customer deposits

To assess the likely fair value of the Bank's retail deposit liabilities, the directors have considered
the estimated cash Flows expected to arise based on a mixture of market-based inputs, such as rates
and pricing and non-market based inputs such as withdrawal rates. Given the mixture of observable
and non-observable inputs, these are considered to be level 3 measurements.

a) = Credit risk

Credit risk is the risk of Financial loss to the Bank if a customer or counterparty to a financial
instrument fails to meet its contractual obligations. The Bank recognises impairment loss
allowances for ECLs calculated as per IFRS 9 requirements at the statement of financial position
date.

a.1 Exposure to credit risk

Total credit risk exposure is the amount of loss that the Bank would suffer if all counterparties to
which the Bank is exposed were to default at once. This is represented substantially by the gross
carrying amount of financial assets, without taking account of the value of any collateral held.

The Bank was contractually committed to make Future loans and advances of £16.2m (2024: £1.5m)
to customers. The maximum exposure to credit risk is represented by these Financial assets was
estimated to be £12k.

a.2 Credit risk measurement
Loans and advances

The estimation of credit exposure for risk management purposes is complex and requires the use
of models, as the exposure varies with changes in market conditions, expected cash Flows and the
passage of time. The assessment of credit risk of a portfolio of assets involves estimations as to the
likelihood of defaults occurring, of the associated loss ratios and of default correlations between
counterparties. The Bank measures credit risk using Probability of Default (PD), Exposure at Default
(EAD) and Loss Given Default (LGD).

To calculate Forward looking provisions, the Bank sources data From its own internal information,
the Bank of England Treasury Forecast and the Office of Budget Responsibilities (“OBR”).
Management exercises judgement in estimating the future economic conditions which are
incorporated into provisions through the modelling of multiple scenarios. For the Bank’s provision
calculation, Four different projected economic scenarios are considered to cover a range of possible
outcomes, reflecting upside, downside and severe downside scenarios relative to the baseline
forecast economic conditions. The economic scenarios are generated to capture a range of possible
economic outcomes to Facilitate the calculation of unbiased and expected credit losses. The Bank’s
economic scenarios, and the probability weightings assigned to each scenario, are produced by the
Credit Function and reviewed and challenged by the Bank’s Credit Committee and approved by the
Board Risk committee. The Bank’s scenarios are weighted 50.1% (2024: 60.22%) base case, 23.7%
(2024: 34.91%) upside, 20.2% (2024: 0%) downside and 6.0% severe downside (2024: 4.86%). The

e) Capital risk and management

The Bank has operated in line with its capital risk appetite as set by the Board and above its
regulatory requirements throughout the period ended 31 March 2025.

Capital is held to protect depositors, by ensuring that the Bank has sufficient capital resources to
repay liabilities even in the Face of unexpected losses. Day-to-day capital management is delegated
to the Chief Financial Officer and overseen by the risk Function, ALCO, Board Risk Committee and
ultimately the Board. The Bank's objective in managing its capital is to maintain appropriate levels
of capital to support its business strategy and to meet regulatory requirements at all times. The
Bank manages its capital in accordance with Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) and Capital
Requirements Directive (together referred to as CRD IV). The Framework is enforced by the PRA.
The PRA sets and monitors the Bank's capital requirements.

The PRA's approach to the measurement of capital adequacy is primarily based on monitoring the
relationship of the capital resources requirement to available capital resources. The regulator sets
Total Capital Requirement (TCR) for each bank in excess of the minimum resources requirement of
8% of risk weighted assets. A key input to the TCR setting process is the Bank's Internal Capital
Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP).

The Bank's policy is to maintain a strong capital base to maintain stakeholder and market confidence
and to sustain the Future development of the business. The Bank monitors current and Forecast
levels of capital against the capital risk appetite approved by the Board and reports the capital
position to ALCO, Board Risk Committee and the Board on a regular basis. The capital Forecast forms
an integral component of the annual budgeting process and is updated in line with changes to the
Bank's business plan.

f) Operational risk

Operational risk is the risk of loss arising from inadequate or failed internal processes, people and
systems or from external events. These could arise from information technology, information
security, project outsourcing, tax, legal, fraud and compliance risks.

The Bank has established an Operational Risk management framework, underpinned by clear
_ policies which are geared to ensuring the Bank operates with a low-risk appetite For operational risk .
Failures. .

The First line of defence is responsible for the management of operational risk in accordance with
stated policies, governance, agreed processes and controls. The second line of defence provides
oversight and challenge supplemented by a programme of regular assurance testing. The third line
of defence (via internal audit) provides an independent review of key operational risk controls and
Frameworks via an agreed programme of audits.

The responsibility of monitoring and reporting of the overall operational risk profile is undertaken
through the Risk Management & Conduct Committee, the Executive Committee and Board Risk
Committee.
