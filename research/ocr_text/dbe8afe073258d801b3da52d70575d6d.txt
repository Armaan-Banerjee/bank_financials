Business Model

Redwood Bank (the “Bank’) has, in its third full year of trading, continued to pursue its purpose of meeting the
funding needs of both small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs) and individual property investors, mainly in its
Heartlands (Hertfordshire,- Bedfordshire, Buckinghamshire, Cambridgeshire, Norfolk and Suffolk), and
Warrington and the North West. The business model is based on the development of tailored business
relationships, through a broker network, with customers, and manually underwriting all tending. Lending takes
the form of commercial, residential and refurbishment secured mortgages, funded through Best Buy savings
notice accounts and savings bonds, offered to businesses, charities, clubs, and associations, both directly and
through cash management services, with varying notice or maturity periods between 35 days to 1 year.

The Bank has identified four core values that guide the Bank's behaviours, being simplicity, integrity, certainty
and tirelessness. It prides itself on offering simple, transparent and time conscious business solutions to its
customers, that have begun to deliver brand loyalty and trust amongst customers. Lending products are targeted
to be underwritten within 48 hours, and savings products can be accessed almost instantly through the Bank's
online infrastructure, demonstrating the Bank's understanding of the value of customers’ time. The Bank’s entire
infrastructure is cloud-based, allowing it to be nimble in response to change, and resilient, as staff can service
customers from any location. .

COVID-19

The COVID-19 pandemic and the unprecedented lockdowns and restrictions imposed by the government to
prevent the spread of the virus had a significant impact on the Bank’s operations, performance and risk appetite.
Redwood offered forbearance to impacted customers in line with the FCA guidance provided, and increased its
impairment provisions to accommodate an increased probability of default and loss given default caused by the
lockdowns’ impact on customers’ businesses. The Bank reduced its appetite for new lending in severely
impacted sectors, however continued to proactively lend in less impacted sectors. Operationally, all the Bank's °
staff have worked from home since March 2020 with minimal disruption due to the Bank’s cloud-based IT
infrastructure and its existing policy, which was in place pre the pandemic, which allowed all employees to work ,
from home at least one day per week.

Performance review

In a year significantly impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic the Bank has increased its total lending book by
£154m during the period, mainly as a result of new lending of £179m during the year, including a small lending
book acquisition of £16m. Total new organic lending was £61m higher than in 2019 (£102m) representing an
increase of 60% year on year. Total deposits held by the Bank increased by £149m during the year, with the
Bank demonstrating an ability to generate liquidity quickly and when necessary. The use of cash management
services as an alternate source of deposits continued to be an ancillary part of the Bank’s funding strategy. It
held £112m in liquid assets at the year end, comprising cash at bank, deposits at the Bank of England, UK
Treasury Bills and UK Gilts. Liquidity being held was sufficient for the Bank's immediate lending needs.

Redwood delivered a loss before tax of £1.7m, which was higher than the 2019 loss before tax of £1.2m. The
loss was driven mainly a higher impairment charge being recognised in the year as a result of the uncertainty
created by the COVID-19 pandemic. The Bank took the opportunity to bolster its second line of defence and
strengthen the underwriting process and as part of this has reviewed its lending portfolio extensively during 2021,
including stressing the value of securities and reassessing probability of default in light of COVID-19. The result
was an increase in impairment loss provisions from £0.4m to £3.1m, representing 0.96% of total lending at the
31 December 2020.

The Bank invested in building and strengthening its teams, especially in Risk & Compliance, Portfolio
Management, Treasury, and Business Development, supporting the increase in fending and improving the
management of risks through the COVID-19 pandemic. The total staff complement grew to 99 (78: 2019) by the
year end.

The Bank continued to develop the use of its Customer Relationship Management (CRM) platform, which
allowed a better tracking of customer information and more robust MI on lending performance. It also completed
the implementation of a number of new software modules, including a regulatory reporting module, Single
Customer View (SCV) module and a treasury management module. Further modules planned for
implementation in 2021 include Assets Liability Management (ALM), financial planning, Effective Interest Rate
(EIR), and Hedge Accounting.

The Bank continued to measure its customer satisfaction and advocacy, as well as staff satisfaction. It obtained
average customer satisfaction scores of 98% for deposits and 96% for lending customers during the year. It
temporarily closed its two offices in Letchworth and Warrington, in line with the Government directive to work at
home if possible, and all staff were successfully working from home since March 2020. The Bank actively
monitored staff engagement and well-being throughout this period. It once again and for the third year running,
took part in the Banking Standards Board Employee survey, in which it ranked first, in seven out of the nine of
the characteristics being surveyed, out of 29 banks and other firms, for employee’s perception of Respect,
Openness, Accountability, Reliability, Resilience, Responsiveness and Shared Purpose. As for the last two
years, the Bank considers these results to be extremely positive, but particularly given the unprecedented
operational circumstances resulting from COVID-19.

The Bank was also the recipient of six Banking industry awards during the year and was a finalist for a further
four awards.

Redwood’s shareholders have continued to show support for the Bank’s business model by investing a further
£3.2m (2019: £12.0m) of Tier 1 capital in the Bank during the year, increasing the total Tier 1 capital invested to
£38.0m. The Bank also received an investment in the form of Tier 2 eligible subordinated debt of £9.0m during
2020 (2019: Enil). Following the year end, in March 2021 the shareholders invested a further £5.9m of Tier 1
capital in the Bank.

The Bank also accessed the governments TFSME scheme for contingent liquidity, against which it drew £19.2m.

Key Performance Indicators

Redwood made a loss before tax for the year ended 31 December 2020 of £1.7m (2019: £1.2m). The average
lending interest rate achieved during the year was 5.60% (2019: 6.46%%), the decline mainly attributable to the
reduction in Bank Base Rate to 10bps in March 2020, and a planned increase in the proportion of secured
residential lending to landlords. The average interest rate paid for the year on deposits was 1.43% (2019:
1.58%). The Bank adopted a conservative liquidity policy during the pandemic, and as a result delayed passing
on any of the impact of the Bank Base Rate reductions to its savings customers until the impact of COVID-19
was more certain.

Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital, as at the 31 December 2020, was £27.4m (2019: £25.8m) and this
exceeded the regulatory minimum required.

The Bank's liquidity coverage ratio of 485% as at 31 December 2020 (2019: 557%) exceeded the regulatory
minimum set by the PRA. The Bank held a total value of high-quality liquid assets of £112m (2019: £87m).

The balance sheet includes loans of £324m (2019: £170m) and deposits of £380m (2019: £231m), reflecting
significant balance sheet growth in the year.

Other key performance indicators

All staff have completed the required conduct training and have each passed the requisite tests. Customer
feedback has been monitored since the Bank's launch in August 2017. There were ten regulatory complaints,
which were eligible for referral to the Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS), received in 2020 (2019:5), all of
which have been amicably resolved.

Strategic Plan

The Bank is continually assessing its strategy to ensure it remains effective and capable of delivering value to all
stakeholders. The Bank expects the core of its lending to continue to be property backed mortgages to SMEs,
including professional landlords who are commercial and residential property owners. Assessments of the risks
to the strategy are completed regularly, including in respect of net interest margin (NIM), impairments and
external market influences such as the continuing COVID-19 pandemic. The Bank continues to assess its capital
and liquidity plans to ensure regulatory compliance. See Key Risks for further assessments of risks regarding
capital and liquidity.

The Bank identifies five main risk categories:

Capital: There are a number of risks which could threaten the Bank's capital adequacy, including credit risk
losses from business loans and liquidity counterparties, operational risk losses from service interruptions, error
or fraud, and mismanagement of the business resulting in either inadequate margins or capital loss. The Bank -
ensures that enough capital is held to remain solvent, even if such risks materialise.

The directors have prepared base case and sensitised forecasts, which consider both capital requirements and
liquidity, for a period of at least 12 months from the date of approval of these financial statements. The base
case and sensitised forecasts do not assume any further capital injections from shareholders. The Bank, and the
directors, expect the Bank to require additional funding from its Investors to fulfil its full potential, meet future
business plans of increased growth, and achieve improved, sustainable, profitability.

The Board considers that sufficient provisions have been made for COVID-19 impaired lending. With its track
record of continuous growth, impending profitability, and a stable infrastructure, the Bank is well positioned for
the future.

Credit Risk: Lending and investment in liquid assets give rise to credit risk. Poor underwriting could lead to
arrears and defaults. As a consequence of the impacts of COVID-19 on economic activity in 2020, the Bank
significantly increased its assessment of its lending portfolio’s Probability of Default (PD) and Loss Given Default

The Bank fully supported its customers by offering repayment holiday periods in line with FCA COVID-19

The Bank also revised elements of its credit risk appetite during the year, as a consequence of the general

Operational Risk: The Bank faces a range of operational risks which are continuously managed within its risk
governance framework, with actions undertaken as required. Key risks are identified in a risk register and graded
according to a frequency and impact methodology. The Bank looks to mitigate risks by ensuring that adequate
controls and procedures are in place and are identified and monitored as part of risk and control self-assessment
exercises, as well as by frequent reviews by the Second Line and the Internal Audit function. It holds adequate
levels of regulatory capital to absorb any potential losses that may arise from operational risk, and it reassesses
the level of capital holdings required to offset potential operational risk losses as part of its ICAAP, It continued
to incur a very low level of operational risk losses in the year and was able to adjust to a scenario where its entire
staff worked remotely since March 2020 without experiencing any operational disruption and without increasing
operational risk losses. :

Business Risk: In addition to the risks above, and in particular to the risk of failure of future capital injections
being completed, the Bank is also exposed to the failure to meet its loan origination targets, which would
negatively impact the Bank's long-term profitability and capital position. These origination targets are closely
monitored, and if necessary, actions identified, in weekly Trading Meetings held. The Bank could also be
exposed to operating at an inadequate net interest margin, which could result from a range of potential issues,
particularly in times of stress, including under-estimating the cost of liquidity, deposit funding or incorrect pricing
of credit risk. It also considers the factors that might lead to an existential risk, such as fundamental regulatory or
political change. This is monitored on an ongoing basis and the appropriate actions taken if necessary.

Economic and political environment

The UK's decision to withdraw from the European Union took effect on 31 January 2020, and although the Bank
has not experienced any direct impact, it recognises that its customers could potentially have adverse impacts
on their ability to service their debts, and therefore result in increased credit losses for the Bank. However, as
part of previous iterations of the Strategic Plan, ICAAP and ILAAP, the Bank has considered several stress
scenarios that could be triggered by Brexit and has evaluated the impact on the Bank’s capital and liquidity
positions. The scenarios consider the possible impact on the Bank of changes to base rate, house price inflation,
property interest rates and cost inflation.

Whilst recognising a potential negative impact on the Bank’s performance and capital position, the Bank is
sufficiently capitalised to continue in business, even under severely stressed economic conditions. Although the
full impact of Brexit is yet to be seen, the Board is confident that the risks have been appropriately captured
within the Bank’s Strategic Plan.

Following a year of lockdowns and business interruptions resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic, the Bank will
grow cautiously and moderately, in what is generally expected to be a recovering economy. The Bank has
considered increased inflation, changes to base rates and the impacts of GDP on its forecasts and has
assessed that it is suitably capitalised and has sufficient liquidity to cope with head winds in the upcoming year.
The Board will continue to review developments regarding the pandemic and assess their impact on potential
risks to the Bank.

Future developments and prospects

The Bank will continue to build on the strong foundations that have been put in place, by continuing to build a
strong Business Development function, supported by robust and experienced operations, compliance, credit,
finance, risk, and management teams.

The Bank is committed to deliver simple and transparent financial products in a timely fashion to British
businesses, and any new products launched will be designed to meet the financial needs of customers.

Through continued investment in our strategy and people we will continue to deliver the highest levels of
customer service for local and national businesses and their owners.

Going concern

Notwithstanding a loss before tax for the year ended 31 December 2020 of £1.7m, the financial statements have
been prepared on a going concern basis, which the directors consider to be appropriate. The Bank recognised
elevated impairment provisions of £2.7m in the year, which negatively impacted profitability, as a result of the
COVID-19 pandemic. The Bank believes the impairment provisions are adequate for the current level of risk in .
the lending book.

The effects of the COVID-19 Pandemic have been severely felt throughout the world and the future impact on
the results of the Bank are difficult to predict. The directors are optimistic about the impact the continued vaccine
roll out will have and the lifting of social restrictions.

On 2 March 2021, the Bank raised £5.9m through the issuance of Tier 1 capital to existing investors, which will
allow the Bank to continue to lend and support its loan book in the near term and weather any further headwinds
that may occur. ,

The directors have prepared base case and sensitised forecasts, which consider both capital requirements and
liquidity, for a period of at least 12 months from the date of approval of these financial statements. The base
case and sensitised forecasts do not assume any further capital injections from shareholders. In preparing those
forecasts, the directors have considered the current economic uncertainty, in particular the effects of the COVID-
19 outbreak. In addition, the directors have considered the impact of an orderly exit from the board of the
incumbent non-executive directors and the transition to new non-executive directors, as the Bank reaches its
fourth full year of operations.

The directors have considered severe but plausible downside scenarios, including stress tests aligned to its
Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP). These stress scenarios are aligned to the Bank of
England's prescribed stress tests. These forecasts demonstrate that the Bank would continue to operate as a
going concern, in what are considered to be severe but plausible downside scenarios.

As a result of the above, the directors are confident that the company will maintain adequate capital and liquidity
for at least 12 months from the date of approval of the financial statements and therefore have prepared the
financial statements on a going concern basis.

The Bank, and the directors, expect the Bank to require additional funding from its Investors to fulfil its full
potential, meet future business plans of increased growth, and achieve improved, sustainable, profitability.

However, as noted above, the forecasts used in the going concern assessment do not assume any further
capital injections from shareholders.

2.2. Going concern

Notwithstanding a loss before tax for the year ended 31 December 2020 of £1.7m, the financial
statements have been prepared on a going concern basis, which the directors consider to be appropriate.
The Bank recognised elevated impairment provisions of £2.7m in the year, which negatively impacted
profitability, as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic. The Bank believes the impairment provisions are
adequate for the current level of risk in the lending book.

The effects of the COVID-19 Pandemic have been severely felt throughout the world and the future impact
on the results of the Bank are difficult to predict. The directors are optimistic about the impact the
continued vaccine roll out will have and the lifting of social restrictions.

On 2 March 2021, the Bank raised £5.9m through the issuance of Tier 1 capital to existing investors,
* which will allow the Bank to continue to lend and support its loan book in the near term and weather any
further headwinds that may occur.

The directors have prepared base case and sensitised forecasts, which consider both capital
requirements and liquidity, for a period of at least 12 months from the date of approval of these financial
statements. The base case and sensitised forecasts do not assume any further capital injections from
shareholders. In preparing those forecasts, the directors have considered the current economic
uncertainty, in particular the effects of the COVID-19 outbreak. In addition, the directors have considered
the impact of an orderly exit from the board of the incumbent non-executive directors and the transition to
new non-executive directors, as the Bank reaches its fourth full year of operations.

The directors have considered severe but plausible downside scenarios, including stress tests aligned to
its Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP). These stress scenarios are aligned to the
Bank of England’s prescribed stress tests. These forecasts demonstrate that the Bank would continue to
operate as a going concern, in what are considered to be severe but plausible downside scenarios.

As a result of the above, the directors are confident that the company will maintain adequate capital and
liquidity for at least 12 months from the date of approval of the financial statements and therefore have
prepared the financial statements on a going concern basis.

The Bank, and the directors, expect the Bank to require additional funding from its Investors to fulfil its full
potential, meet future business plans of increased growth, and achieve improved, sustainable, profitability.
However, as noted above, the forecasts used in the going concern assessment do not assume any further
capital injections from shareholders.

2.3 ‘Interest income and interest expense

Interest income and expense are recognised in comprehensive income using the effective interest rate
method. The ‘effective interest rate’ is the rate that exactly discounts the estimated future cash payments
and receipts through the expected life of the financial asset or financial liability (or, where appropriate, a
shorter period) to the carrying amount of the financial asset or financial liability. When calculating the
effective interest rate, the Bank estimates future cash flows considering all contractual terms of the
financial instrument, but not future credit losses.

The calculation of the effective interest rate includes those transaction costs and fees paid or received,
which are deemed to be an integral part of the effective interest rate. Transaction costs include
incremental costs that are directly attributable to the acquisition or issue of a financial asset or financial
liability.
Interest income and expense presented in comprehensive income include:
- interest on financial assets and financial liabilities measured at amortised cost calculated on an
effective interest basis; and .

- interest on available-for-sale investment securities calculated on an effective interest rate basis.

2.6 Financial instruments

Recognition .
The Bank initially recognises loans and advances, deposits, and debt securities on the date on which they
are originated. A financial asset or financial liability is measured initially at fair value plus, for an item not at
fair value through profit or loss, transaction costs that are directly attributable to its acquisition or issue.

Classification

Financial assets

The Bank classifies its financial assets into one of the following categories:
Loans and advances to customers

‘Loans and advances to customers’ are non-derivative financial assets with fixed or determinable
payments that are not quoted in an active market and that the Bank does not intend to sell immediately or
in the near term.

a

Loans and advances are initially measured at fair value plus incremental direct transaction costs, and
subsequently measured at their amortised cost using the effective interest rate method (see Note 3).

The Bank acquired a portfolio of commercial loans which were initially measured at fair value plus
incremental direct transaction costs, less any discount and subsequently measured at their amortised cost
using the effective interest rate method

Treasury bills and gilts

Treasury bill and gilts intended to be held on a continuing basis, other than those designated at fair value,
are classified as available for sale or held to maturity. They are recognised on the trade date the Bank
enters into contractual arrangements to purchase those instruments, and are normally derecognised when
either the securities are sold or when the Bank no longer has contractual right to the cashflows associated
with the treasury bill or gilt.

‘Available-for-sale financial assets' are initially measured at fair value plus direct and incremental
transaction costs. They are subsequently remeasured at fair value, and changes therein are recognised in
other comprehensive income until the assets are either sold or become impaired. When available-for-sale
financial assets are sold, cumulative gains or losses previously recognised in other comprehensive
income are recognised in the income statement as interest income.

'Held-to-maturity’ investments are non-derivative financial assets with fixed or determinable payments and
fixed maturities that the Bank positively intends, and is able, to hold to maturity. Held-to-maturity
investments are initially recorded at fair value plus any directly attributable transaction costs, and are
subsequently measured at amortised cost; less any impairment losses. Interest is recognised at the
effective interest rate to the maturity of the asset. ;

Interest income is recognised at the effective interest rate over a debt security's expected life. Premiums
and/or discounts arising on the purchase of dated debt securities are included in the interest recognised.

Other fair value changes, other than impairment losses, are recognised in 'Other Comprehensive Income'
and presented in the available for sale reserve within equity. When the investment is sold, the gain or loss
accumulated in equity is reclassified to profit and loss.

Financial Liabilities

‘Trade creditors’ are initially measured at transaction price plus attributable transaction costs, and are
subsequently measured at amortised cost, using the effective interest rate method.

‘Subordinated debt' is initially measured at transaction price plus attributable transaction costs, and are

Measurement
Amortised cost measurement

The amortised cost of a financial asset or financial liability is the amount at which the financial asset or
financial liability is measured at initial recognition, minus principal repayments, plus or minus the
cumulative amortisation using the effective interest method of any difference between the initial amount
recognised and the maturity amount, minus any reduction for impairment.

Fair value measurement

Fair value is the amount for which an asset could be exchanged, a liability settled, or an equity instrument
granted could be exchanged, between knowledgeable, willing parties in an arm’s length transaction.

When available, the Bank measures the fair value of an instrument using the quoted price in an active
market for that instrument. A market is regarded as active if transactions for the asset or liability take
place with sufficient frequency and volume to provide pricing information on an ongoing basis.

if there is no quoted price in an active market, then the Bank uses valuation techniques that maximise the
use of relevant observable inputs and minimise the use of unobservable inputs. The chosen valuation
technique incorporates all of the factors that market participants would take into account in pricing a
transaction.

The best evidence of the fair value of a financial instrument at initial recognition is normally the transaction
price — i.e. the fair value of the consideration given or received. If the Bank determines that the fair value
at initial recognition differs from the transaction price and the fair value is evidenced neither by a quoted
price in an active market for an identical asset or liability or based on a valuation technique that uses only
data from observable markets, then the financial instrument is initially measured at fair value, adjusted to
defer the difference between the fair value at initial recognition and the transaction price. Subsequently,
that difference is recognised in profit or loss on an appropriate basis over the life of the instrument, but no
later than when the valuation is wholly supported by observable market data or the transaction is closed
out.

Identification and measurement of impairment

At each reporting date, the Bank assesses whether there is objective evidence that financial assets not
carried at fair value through profit or loss are impaired. A financial asset or a group of financial assets is
‘impaired’ when objective evidence demonstrates that a loss event has occurred after the initial
recognition of the asset(s) and that the loss event has an impact on the future cash flows of the asset(s)
that can be estimated reliably.

Objective evidence that financial assets are impaired includes:

- significant financial difficulty of the borrower or issuer;

2.10 Share capital and equity

In accordance with FRS 102.22, financial instruments issued by the Bank are treated as equity only tothe -
extent that they meet the following two conditions:

e they include no contractual obligations upon the Bank to deliver cash or other financial assets or to
exchange financial assets or liabilities with another party under conditions that are potentially
unfavourable to the Bank; and

e where the instrument will or may be settled in the Bank's own equity instruments, it is either a non-
derivative that includes no obligation to deliver a variable number of the Bank's own equity instruments
or is a derivative that will be settled by the Bank's exchanging of a fixed amount of cash or other
financial assets for a fixed number of its own equity instruments.

To the extent that this definition is not met, the proceeds of issue are classified as a financial liability.
Where the instrument so classified takes the legal form of the Bank's own shares, the amounts that are
presented in these financial statements for called up share capital and share premium account exclude
amounts in relation to those shares.

2.11 Operating leases: the Bank as lessee

Leases are classified as finance leases whenever the terms of the lease transfer substantially all the risks
and rewards of ownership to the lessee. All other leases are classified as operating leases.

Payments made under operating leases are recognised in the profit and loss account on a straight-line
basis over the term of the lease, unless the payments to the lessor are structured to increase in line with
expected general inflation; in which case the payments related to the structured increases are recognised
as incurred. Lease incentives received are recognised in comprehensive income over the term of the
lease as an integral part of the total lease expense.

2.12 Pensions

The Bank operates a defined contribution plan for its employees. A defined contribution plan is a pension
plan under which the Bank pays fixed contributions into a separate entity. Once the contributions have
been paid the Bank has no further payment obligations.

Amounts not paid are shown in accruals as a liability in the Statement of Financial Position. The assets of
the plan are held separately from the Bank in independently administered funds.

Contributions to defined contribution pension schemes are charged to the statement of comprehensive
income in the year to which they relate.

2.13 Tax

Tax comprises current tax and deferred tax. Tax is recognised in the statement of profit and loss except to
the extent that it relates to items recognised directly in equity or other comprehensive income.

Current tax

Current tax comprises the expected tax payable or receivable on the taxable income or loss for the year
and any adjustment to the tax payable or receivable in respect of previous years. It is measured using tax
rates enacted or substantively enacted at the reporting date.

Deferred tax

Deferred tax is provided in full using the liability method on temporary differences between the carrying
amounts of assets and liabilities for financial reporting purposes and the amounts used for tax purposes.
The amount of deferred tax provided is based on the expected manner of realisation or settlement of the
* carrying amount of assets and liabilities, using tax rates enacted or substantively enacted at the reporting
date.

A deferred tax asset is recognised in the statement of financial position for unused tax losses, unused tax
credits and deductible temporary differences to the extent that it is probable that future taxable profits will
be available against which they can be utilised. Deferred tax assets are reviewed at each reporting date
and are reduced to the extent that it is no longer probable that the related tax benefit will be realised.

Accounting estimates and judgements

The preparation of financial statements in conformity with FRS 102 requires management, from time to
time, to make judgements and estimates that affect the application of policies and reported amounts of
assets, liabilities, income and expenses.

These estimates are based on experience and various other factors that are believed to be reasonable
under the circumstances. Actual results may differ from these estimates. The estimates and underlying
assumptions are reviewed on an ongoing basis. Revisions to accounting estimates are recognised in the
period in which the estimates are revised and in any future periods affected.

Effective interest rate

FRS 102 requires interest earned from loans and advances to be measured under the Effective Interest
Rate Method (EIRM). Management must therefore use judgement to estimate the expected life of each
instrument and hence the expected cash flows relating te it.

The key assumptions applied by management in the EIR methodology are regarding the behavioural life
of the assets and the quantum of future fees and costs directly related to the instrument. The expected life
behaviours are subjected to changes in internal and external factors and may result in adjustments to the
carrying value of loans which must be recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss and Other
Comprehensive Income.

Management has limited historical experience of the Bank's customer behaviours due to the relative
immaturity of the portfolios and therefore models expected behaviour based on market trends and
experience. The actual behaviour of the portfolios are compared to the modelled behaviour on a quarterly
basis and the modelled behaviours are adjusted if the modelled behaviour materially deviates from actual
behaviour, with adjustments recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss and Other Comprehensive
income.

A 10% decrease in expected life of loans result in an increase in income of £52k (2019: £23k) and an
increase in expected life of 10% would result in a decrease in income of £43k (2019: £19k).

Liquidity risk

Liquidity risk is the risk that the Bank is unable to meet its current and future financial obligations as they
fall due, or is only able to do so at excessive cost. The Bank's liquidity is managed on a daily basis by the
Asset and Liability Committee, with oversight from the Board Risk Committee.

The Bank has developed comprehensive funding and liquidity policies to ensure that it maintains
sufficient liquid assets to be able to meet all of its financial obligations and maintain public confidence.

Liquidity stress testing is conducted under a variety of scenarios, covering both normal and more severe

market conditions.

Interest rate risk is the risk of loss arising from adverse movements in market interest rates. Interest rate

procedures. In the future interest rate swaps will be introduced to manage interest rate risk.

Basis risk

similar, but not identical characteristics (for example, SONIA and the Bank of England base rate). This is
monitored closely and regularly reported to the Assets and Liabilities Committee (ALCO). This risk is

. Managed by matching, and where appropriate and necessary, with established risk limits and other —
control procedures.

The Bank's forecasts and plans take account of the risk of interest rate changes and are prepared and
stressed accordingly, in line with PRA guidance. .

Regulators and central banks have set the goal of improving the robustness of financial benchmarks,

benchmarks, the London Interbank Offered Rate (“LIBOR’) is uncertain. In the UK, the FCA has

Operational risk

Operational risk is the risk of direct or indirect loss arising from a wide variety of causes associated with
the Bank’s processes, personnel, technology and infrastructure, and from external factors other than
credit, market and liquidity risks, such as those arising from legal and regulatory requirements and
generally accepted standards of corporate behaviour. Operational risk arises from all of the Bank’s
operations.

The Bank's objective is to manage operational risk so as to balance the avoidance of financial losses and.
damage to the Bank's reputation with overall cost effectiveness and innovation. Bank policy requires
compliance with all applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

The Board of directors has delegated responsibility for operational risk to its Risk Committee, which is
responsible for the development and management of controls to address operational risk.

Compliance with standards is supported by a programme of periodic reviews undertaken by Internal
Audit.

The results of Internal Audit reviews are discussed in the Audit & Compliance Committee.
Capital risk management

The Bank's objective in managing its capital is to maintain appropriate levels of capital to support its
business strategy and meet regulatory requirements, including holding capital for undrawn credit lines.

The Bank’s capital resources are comprised of a minimum of 75% CET1 and a maximum of 25% Tier 2
regulatory capital (any Tier 2 in excess of 25% of total capital is considered ineligible for capital
purposes). The total capital ratio sits at 17.8% (unaudited) (2019: 20.3%) and the Bank's regulatory

capital requirements for CET1 and total capital are met. The Bank also holds capital for undrawn credit
lines.

The Bank manages its capital under Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) and Capital Requirements
Directive (together referred to as CRDIV). The framework is enforced by the Prudential Regulatory
Authority (PRA). The PRA sets and monitors the Bank's capital requirements.

The PRA's approach to the measurement of capital adequacy is primarily based on monitoring the
relationship of the capital resources requirement to available capital resources. The regulator sets the
total capital requirement (TCR) for each bank in excess of the minimum resources requirement of 8%. A
key input to the TCR setting process is the Bank's internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process
(ICAAP). \

The Bank's policy is to maintain a strong capital base to maintain investor and market confi dence and to
sustain the future development of the business.

The Bank has elected to use the standardised approach for credit risk. Under CRDIV the Bank must set
aside 8% of it’s total risk weighted assets to cover its ‘Pillar 1° capital requirements. The Bank must also
set aside additional ‘Pillar 2' capital to provide for additional risks. This is calculated by multiplying the
Pillar 1 capital by the TCR ratio. The TCR ratio is based on the various risks which the Bank faces and is
agreed by the PRA. The Bank's capital base was in excess of the minimum required under the TCR
throughout the year.
