The Bank is an authorised institution under the United Kingdom Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 and carries on

RESULTS AND PERFORMANCE

Despite the ongoing general market and economic uncertainty during 2023, the Bank reported a profit after tax of $47.0m.
The Bank continued to focus on its core lending businesses of residential mortgages, commercial property, margin lending
and providing a global fund management service to institutional and private clients.

Net Interest Income

The Bank achieved a net interest income result of $84.6m for the year (2022: $56.3m). During the year key benchmark
interest rates increased significantly as central banks looked to reduce inflation and market uncertainty. During 2023, the
Bank of England base rate, being the key reference rate for the Bank’s loan book, increased from 3.5% to 5.25%, the
highest since April 2008.

As aresult, the impact of increases in interest rates was reflected in the repricing of the balance sheet, where by net interest
margin increased to an average of 3.06% for 2023, compared to 2.05% in 2022.

Fees and Other Operating Income

Fees and other income for the year were $18.1m (2022 : $23.0m) and includes the following:

« Net fees and commissions of $8.1m (2022 : $11.1m), which reflects a reduction in Institutional and REFM fund fees
as a result of funds maturing, increased fee expenses in respect of Deposit Aggregator platform, and a reduction in
redemption fees in 2023 when compared to 2022;

= Investment income of $4.5m (2022 : $6.2m), includes net realised gains from derecognition of financial assets during
the year;

« Other operating income of $5.4m (2022: $5.6m) which remained stable during the year, compared to 2022, which
emanates from distributions from Private Equity investments.

Credit Loss Recoveries
The expected loss provision charge during the year was $4.7m, which is due to a net increase in stage 3 provision
requirements of $4.5m, and stage ] and 2 provision requirements increased of $0.2m.

Operating Expenses
The increase in operating expenses of $36.8m (2022: $28.3m) is due to increased staff costs, in line with market
compensation, premises cost and business costs, including advisory support costs.

PRINCIPAL RISKS

The principal business risks faced by the Bank in relation to the financial instruments recognised within the balance sheet

(detailed within notes 29 - 32) are summarised as follows:

" Credit Risk - The risk arising from a counterpart's failure to discharge on time and in full as contracted and cause
the other party to incur a financial loss;

* Liquidity Risk - The risk that the Bank does not have sufficient financial resources to meet its obligations as they
fall due, or will have to do so at an excessive cost. This risk arises from mismatches in the timing of cash flows.
Funding risk arises when the necessary liquidity to fund illiquid asset positions cannot be obtained at the expected
terms and when required;

"Market Risk - The risk of potential financial loss that may arise from adverse changes in the value of financial
instruments, or portfolio of financial instruments, due to movements in interest rates, foreign exchange rates or other
prices and volatilities. The hedging framework applied to manage specific market risks is outlined in Note 26.

» Concentration Risk ~ The risk arising from the Bank having deposits with customers that are a material percentage
of total deposits.

For each of the aforementioned identified risks, the Bank has in place a management framework and policies to identify,
control and provide management oversight for such risks.

In recent years, the respective regulatory authorities have placed a high focus on additional risks that have arisen due to
the impact of climate change and resulting new legislation, and the increased outsourcing of key support functions and
the recent changes in working patterns initially implemented as a result of the Covid-19 pandemic. These are detailed as
follows:

CLIMATE CHANGE RISK

The risk that the Bank experiences losses and/or reputational damage because of climate change, either directly or through
its customers. These losses may be realised from physical events, the required adaptation in transitioning to a lower
carbon economy or because of the responses to managing these changes.

The scale of the potential impact of climate related risks and opportunities and the time horizons over which these will
manifest will vary across the Bank’s business operations. The variability of impacts and the time horizons will be
dependent on several different factors, some of which are in the control of the organisation and some of which are not.

Climate risk manifests through, and has the potential to impact, the Bank’s existing primary financial and non-financial
risks. For example, physical and transition risk, the impairment of asset values, may impact the creditworthiness of the
Bank’s customers, and result in currently profitable business deteriorating over the term of agreed facilities.

The Bank considers climate-related risks resulting from the following drivers:

# Physical risks — changes in climate or weather patterns which are acute, event driven (e.g. flood impacts on the
underlying real estate collateral in relation to the Bank’s loan portfolio), or chronic, longer-term shifts (e.g. rising sea
levels).

* Transition risks — associated with the move towards a lower carbon economy, e.g. changes to policy, legislation and
regulation, technology, market, reputational and changes to customer preferences.

CLIMATE CHANGE RISK (continued)

The ability to identify, measure and manage the risks associated with climate change is integral to our Enterprise Risk
Management Framework (ERMF). This is consistent with other key risks that we manage. However, the time horizon
in which climate risk will present itself may be a significantly longer time horizon than we have previously experienced
compared to other risks considered by the Bank.

Climate risk includes the risk of financial loss associated with a changing climate and the related legislative and societal
shifts that are taking place in response to that change. We are committed to understanding and assessing the risks
associated with climate change and the impact on the Bank’s financial results. In preparing the financial statements, we
have considered the impact of climate-related risks on our financial position and performance, and while the effects of
climate change represent a source of uncertainty, there has not been a material impact on our financial judgements and
estimates due to the physical and transition climate-related risks in the short to medium term.

The Bank follows the Task Force on Climate change-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) guidelines. Essentially the

Governance

The Bank’s governance structure provides clear oversight and ownership of the Bank’s management of climate risk at
Board and Executive levels. The Board has overall oversight of all Climate change/Environmental, Social and Corporate
Governance (ESG) matters and is engaged on a regular basis in our climate risk agenda, receiving ongoing training to
develop an understanding of climate risk.

The Bank’s Risk Committee is responsible for the oversight of climate-related risks, including alignment with regulatory
expectations, embedding into the Bank’s Risk Management Framework and implications for risk appetite.

The Risk Committee supports the Bank’s Board in fulfilling its risk governance and oversight roles and responsibilities.
It is responsible for oversight of the risks the business faces from climate change, and receives standalone updates to
monitor progress and assess the evolving risk management capabilities and approach.

Climate risk is considered on a monthly basis in the Bank’s risk reporting with qualitative updates provided, while regular
updates are presented quarterly to the Board Committee regarding the Bank’s climate risk management activities and key
developments.

These more detailed updates are intended to ensure Board oversight of:

* Overall climate risk programme management across the Bank, including climate scenario analysis and stress testing;
«  Climate-related risk appetite development and approval at both a Business and individual sector level;

" Metrics associated with the climate risk programme and risk appetite.

Risk Management
Climate risk is embedded into the Bank’s activities and the Enterprise Risk Management Framework, through
consideration of climate risk as its own principal risk, and integration into other principal risks materially impacted.

Risk Management has integrated climate-related risk considerations into all risk-management processes, including capital
allocations (ICAAP Pillar 2a assessment), funding/liquidity TLAAP assessment). IFRS 9 charge (changes to Expected
Credit Losses), credit approvals (credit risk appetite and credit applications), portfolio monitoring and MI reporting. An
overall Climate Risk Policy is in place to provide an overarching framework for the management of climate risks and
opportunities across the Business.

As part of the Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) the Bank includes all material exposures relating
to the financial risks from climate change.

Strategy
Challenges facing each sector vary considerably, and each sector is at a differing scale of maturity in its transition journey.

Where applicable, the Bank has been looking at opportunities to reshape its approach to the types of investments and

business informed decisions. As a mortgage lender, improving energy efficiency of homes is one of our key priorities and
to incentivise customers to take action the business is considering the launch of a Green Home Purchase Plan product.

OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE RISK

The risk that the Bank fails to design resilience into business operations, underlying infrastructure, and controls (people,
process, technical) to withstand external or internal events that could impact the continuity of operations or alternatively
the failure to respond to events in a way which meets stakeholder expectations and needs when the continuity of operations
is compromised.

Effective operational resilience ensures the Bank designs resilience into its systems, is able to withstand and/or recover
from a significant unexpected event occurring and can continue to provide services to its customers. A significant outage
could result in customers being unable to access accounts or conduct transactions, which as well as presenting significant
reputational risk for the Bank would negatively impact the Bank’s purpose. Operational resilience is also an area of
continued regulatory and industry focus, similar in importance to financial resilience.

Failure to manage operational resilience effectively could impact the following other risk categories:

* Regulatory compliance: non-compliance with new/existing operational resilience regulations, for example, through
failure to identify emerging regulation or not embedding regulatory requirements within the Bank’s policies,
processes and procedures or identify further future emerging regulation;

« Operational risk: being unable to safely provide customers with business services;

« Conduct risk: an operational resilience failure may render the Bank liable to fines from the FCA for poor conduct.

Key mitigating actions

The annual self-assessment which outlines the Bank’s approach to meeting the regulatory operational resilience
requirements, which includes the identification of important business services and its respective impact tolerances will be
presented to the Board for approval in Q1] 2024.

Mitigating controls are in place to manage the risk of severe critical business process disruption and the Bank
acknowledges the risks associated with the failure to maintain appropriately skilled and available staff.

The Bank remains highly focused on the operational resilience of its services and continues to evolve its approach to
design and enhance resilience into business operations to ensure it remains appropriate. The activities in plan will increase
the resilience of key services to the Bank’s customers.

FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

On the 2 October 2022 Kuwait Finance House (KFH) completed its acquisition of Ahli United Bank B.S.C., creating one
of the largest Islamic banks in the Gulf region. The strategy for conversion and integration to Sharia compliance for the
Bank was initiated during 2023 and will be implemented in 2024.

All aspects of the Bank’s conversion and integration program will be managed by the AUBUK Management team, with
coordination and implementation support from AUB Group, and Sharia permissions (Fatwas) provided by the Bank’s
Sharia Supervisory Board.

The Bank’s core business activities continue to focus on its core lending businesses of residential mortgages, commercial
property, treasury services, and providing a global fund management service to institutional and private clients. The
conversion to Sharia compliance provides the Bank with the opportunity to offer comparable products to clients, and in
addition introducing a menu of products and services to enhance the core offering.

In respect of its core lending businesses, the Bank will continue to lend at rates that are competitive to allow for growth.
Lending is based on the Bank's risk appetite ensuring that the loan business is robust and able to withstand volatile
economic or market changes. The Bank will continue to ensure any business growth complies with regulatory
requirements and is in accordance with the PRA capital and liquidity guidelines.

As part of the wider KFH Group the Bank will have access to a larger client base and internal infrastructure, which will
support the Bank’s short term strategy to enhance its Sharia compliant wealth management offering and become the
‘gateway’ to the UK for the Group’s KFH clients.

The AUB Group’s transformation project provided technical solutions to enhance the customer’s banking experience,
and the Bank will fully integrate during 2024.

The Directors of Ahli United Bank (UK) PLC are pleased to submit the Annual Report and Financial Statements for the

known as the 'Group'). The Directors consider that the information contained within the Annual Report and Financial

RESULTS AND DIVIDENDS

The profit after tax of the Group for the year amounted to $47.0 million (2022: $39.5 million). A final dividend for 2022
of $20.0 million (5 cents per share) was paid during the year. A final dividend of $30.0 million (10 cents per share) is
proposed for 2023.

GOING CONCERN

The future plan for the Bank, in addition to the Bank's policies and reporting for managing the credit, liquidity, depositor
concentration and market risks associated with its financial instruments, together with risks arising from climate change
and operational resilience, provides a basis whereby the Directors believe that the Bank is well placed to manage its
business risks successfully. Based on this review, the Directors have a reasonable expectation that the Bank has adequate
resources to continue in operational existence for the period to 31 March 2025, and therefore the financial statements
have been prepared on a going concern basis. Furthermore, the Directors are not aware of any material uncertainties that
may cast significant doubt upon the Group’s ability to continue as a going concern.

The going concern assessment, as at 31 December 2023, considers the core business model of the Bank, funding plans,
asset quality and provisioning, reverse stress testing, and key financial and regulatory metrics. Such regulatory metrics
include regulatory capital and liquidity metrics covering both short and long-term time horizons.

There are no events or conditions that require mitigating plans to be implemented in addition to the Bank’s existing
business model. Judgements made as part of the going concern assessment are also in accordance with the Bank’s business
model.

In addition, the Bank undertakes regular stress scenarios considered within the Bank's ICAAP and ILAAP. The modelled
output from these scenarios continue to demonstrate first and second order effects that are less severe upon the Bank’s
capital and liquidity resources than those stemming from the chosen scenarios that inform compliance with the Bank’s
risk appetite approved by the Board of Directors.

Our application of materiality
We apply the concept of materiality in planning and performing the audit, in evaluating the effect of
identified misstatements on the audit and in forming our audit opinion.
Materiality

' The magnitude of an omission or misstatement that, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably
be expected to influence the economic decisions of the users of the financial statements. Materiality
provides a basis for determining the nature and extent of our audit procedures.

We determined materiality for the Group to be $3.1m (2022: $2.5m), which is 5% of profit before tax.
We believe that profit before tax provides us with the most appropriate measure that reflects the
performance of the Group. This measure has not changed from the prior year.

During the course of our audit, we reassessed initial materiality and recatculated it based on year-end
profit before tax, which was higher than forecast at the date of our planning.

Performance materiality

The application of materiality at the individual account or balance level. It is set at an amount to reduce
to an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected
misstatements exceeds materiality.

On the basis of our risk assessments, together with our assessment of the Group's overall control
environment, our judgement was that performance materiality was 75% (2022: 50%) of our planning
materiality, namely $2.3m (2022: $1.3m). We have set performance materiality at this percentage due
to our past experience with auditing this entity, including the fact that no significant audit adjustments
were identified in our prior year audit.

Reporting threshold
An amount below which identified misstatements are considered as being clearly trivial.

We agreed with the Audit Committee that we would report to them all uncorrected audit differences in
excess of $0.2m (2022: $0.1m), which is set at 5% of planning materiality, as well as differences below
that threshold that, in our view, warranted reporting on qualitative grounds.

1 ACCOUNTING POLICIES

1.1 BASIS OF CONSOLIDATION

The consolidated financial statements comprise the financial statements of the Bank and its subsidiaries (together known
as the 'Group’) as-at and for the years ended 31 December 2023 and 2022. The financial statements of material subsidiaries
are prepared for the same reporting year as the Bank (unless stated otherwise within note 16), using consistent accounting
policies. Adjustments are made to bring into line any dissimilar accounting policies that may exist.

All material intra-group balances, transactions, income and expenses and profits and losses resulting from intra-group
transactions are eliminated in full on consolidation. Subsidiaries are fully consolidated from the date on which control is
transferred to the Group. Control is achieved when the Bank is exposed, or has rights, to variable returns from its
involvement with the investee and has the ability to affect those returns through its power over the investee. The results
of subsidiaries acquired or disposed of during the year are included in the consolidated income statement from the date
of acquisition or up to the date of disposal, as appropriate. No income statement is presented for the Bank as permitted
by section 408 of the Companies Act 2006. The principal subsidiaries are set out in note 16 for which the Bank holds
100% of the share capital issued.

1.2 BASIS OF PREPARATION

The consolidated financial statements have been prepared on a historical cost basis, except derivatives financial
instruments and financial assets designated at fair value through either P&L (FVTPL) or OCI (FVOCD, all of which have
been measured at fair value. The consolidated financial statements have been prepared in accordance with UK adopted
International Accounting Standards in conformity with the requirements of the Companies Act 2006. The consolidated
financial statements are presented in US Dollars which is the Group’s functional currency, owing to this being the primary
currency that is used: (i) to fund the business, through share capital and deposits; (ii) for dividend distributions; and (iii)
the ultimate parent’s presentational and functional currency. All values are rounded to the nearest thousand (US Dollar
thousand) except where otherwise indicated. The US Dollar / Sterling foreign exchange rate applicable as at the reporting
date was 1.2731 (2022: 1.2068).

In preparing the financial statements, the Group has considered the impact of the physical and transition risks of climate
change and identified this as an emerging risk as set out on pages 3 - 5 but have concluded that it does not have a material
impact on the carrying value of investments and the recognition and measurement of assets and liabilities in the financial
statements as at 31 December 2023.

1.3 GOING CONCERN

The future plan for the Bank, in addition to the Bank's policies for managing the credit, liquidity and market risks
associated with its financial instruments, together with risks arising from climate change and operational resilience,
provides a basis whereby the Directors believe that the Bank is well placed to manage its business risks successfully.
Based on this review, the Directors have a reasonable expectation that the Bank has adequate resources to continue in
operational existence for the period to 31 March 2025, and therefore the financial statements have been prepared on a
going concern basis. Furthermore, the Directors are not aware of any material uncertainties that may cast significant
doubt upon the Group’s ability to continue as a going concern.

The going concern assessment, as at 31 December 2023, considers the core business model of the Bank, funding plans,
asset quality and provisioning, reverse stress testing, and key financial and regulatory metrics. Such regulatory metrics
include regulatory capital and liquidity metrics covering both short and long-term time horizons.

There are no events or conditions that require mitigating plans to be implemented in addition to the Bank’s existing
business model. Judgements made as part of the going concern assessment are also in accordance with the Bank’s
business model.

In addition, the Bank undertakes regular stress scenarios considered within the Bank's ICAAP and ILAAP. The modelled
output from these scenarios continue to demonstrate first and second order effects that are less severe upon the Bank’s
capital and liquidity resources than those stemming from the chosen scenarios that inform compliance with the Bank’s
risk appetite approved by the Board of Directors.

1.4 STATEMENT OF COMPLIANCE

the European Union at that date were brought into UK law and became UK-adopted IAS, with future changes being
subject to endorsement by the UK Endorsement Board.

1.45 NEW AND AMENDED STANDARDS AND INTERPRETATIONS
Effective for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2023, the following new accounting standards
become effective:

Amendments to IAS 8 : Definition of Accounting Estimates

The amendments to IAS 8 clarify the distinction between changes in accounting estimates and changes in accounting
policies and the correction of errors. Also, the changes clarify how entities use measurement techniques and inputs to
develop accounting estimates.

The amendments had no impact on the Bank’s consolidated financial statements.

Amendments to [AS 1 : Disclosure of Accounting Policies

The amendments to IAS 1 and IFRS Practice Statement 2 - Making Materiality Judgements, provide guidance and
examples to help entities apply materiality judgements to accounting policy disclosures. The amendments aim to help
entities provide accounting policy disclosures that are more useful by replacing the requirement for entities to disclose
their ‘significant’ accounting policies with a requirement to disclose their ‘material’ accounting policies and adding
guidance on how entities apply the concept of materiality in making decisions about accounting policy disclosures.

The amendments have not resulted in any amendment of the accounting policies disclosed and additionally not on the
measurement, recognition or presentation of any items in the Bank’s consolidated financial statements.

International Tax Reform — Pillar Two Model Rules — Amendments to IAS 12

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)/G20 Inclusive Framework on Base Erosion and
Profit Shifting published the Pillar Two model rules designed to address the tax challenges arising from the digitalisation
of the global economy. It is unclear if the Pillar Two model rules create additional temporary differences, whether to
remeasure deferred taxes for the Pillar Two model rules and which tax rate to use to measure deferred taxes. In response
to this uncertainty, on 23 May 2023 and 27 June 2023, respectively, the IASB and AASB issued amendments to IAS 12
*Thcome taxes’ ititYoducing 4 mandatory temporary exception to the requirements ot LAS [2 under which a company does
not recognise or disclose information about deferred tax assets and liabilities related to the proposed OECD/G20 BEPS
Pillar Two model rules. The Bank applied the temporary exception as at 31 December 2023.

Pillar Two legislation has been enacted or substantively enacted in certain jurisdictions in which the Group operates. The
legislation will be effective for the Bank’s financial year beginning | January 2024. The Bank has performed an
assessment of the Bank’s potential exposure to Pillar Two income taxes. This assessment is based on the most recent
information available regarding the financial performance of the constituent entities in the wider Group. Based on the
assessment performed, and considering relevant tax legislation that has been enacted or substantively enacted, the Bank
has assessed that the effective tax rates in relevant jurisdictions are above 15%. The Bank continues to monitor

i) Overview of the ECL impairment principles

IFRS 9 requires a forward-looking expected credit loss ("ECL") approach, whereby the Bank records an allowance for

The ECL allowance is based on the credit losses expected to arise over the life of the asset (the lifetime expected credit

been a significant increase in credit risk are set out in Note 29.

The 12mECL is the portion of LTECLs that represent the ECLs that result from default events on AC assets that are
possible within the 12 months after the reporting date.

The Bank has established a Credit and Investment policy to perform an assessment, at the end of each reporting period,

of whether an AC asset’s credit risk has increased significantly since initial recognition, by considering the change in the

risk of default occurring over the remaining life of the financial instrument, which is further explained in Note 29. For

the calculation of ECL the Bank groups its AC assets into Stage 1, Stage 2 and Stage 3, as described below:

«Stage 1: When an AC asset is first recognised, the Bank recognises an allowance based on 12mECLs;

« Stage 2: When an AC asset has shown a significant increase in credit risk since original recognition the Bank records
an allowance for the LTECLs; and

"Stage 3: When AC assets are considered credit-impaired, the Bank records a specific allowance for the LTECLs,
taking into consideration the reasonable expectation of recovering, for which the gross carrying amount of the AC

it) ECL calculation

The Bank calculates ECLs based on three probability-weighted scenarios to measure the expected cash shortfalls,

discounted at an approximation to the original effective interest rate (EIR). A cash shortfall is the difference between the

cash flows that are due to the Bank in accordance with the contract and the cash flows that the Bank expects to receive.

The mechanics of the ECL calculations are outlined below and the key elements are as follows:

«EAD - The Exposure at Default is an estimate of the exposure at a future default date, taking into account expected
changes in the exposure after the reporting date, including repayments of principal and interest, whether scheduled
by contract or otherwise, expected drawdowns on committed facilities, and accrued interest from missed payments;

* PD - The Probability of Default is an estimate of the likelihood of default over a given time horizon. A default may
only happen at a certain time over the assessed period, if the facility has not been previously derecognised and is still
in the portfolio; and

* LGD - The Loss Given Default, expressed as a percentage, is an estimate of the loss arising in the case where a
default occurs at a given time. It is based on the difference between the contractual cash flows due and those that the
lender would expect to receive, including from the realisation of any collateral. The recovery rate is defined as the
value of a security when it emerges from default or bankruptcy.

For the Bank's impairment model, the PD and LGD calibration tables are sourced from either:

= A recognised rating agency (S&P and Moody’s) where comparable asset data is available. Debt instruments and
deposits with Banks are included in this category; and

«" The Bank's historical default data for loans and advances, taking into consideration the underlying collateral
available.

When estimating the ECLs, the Bank considers three scenarios (a base case, an upside {‘good'} and a downside {‘bad’}).

Each of these is associated with different PDs and LGDs.

The ECL calculation is as follows : ECL = [EAD x PD X LGD x discounted by an approximation to the original EIR].

« Stage 1 : The ECL calculation is referenced to the appropriate rates and forecast for the 12 month period following
the reporting date;

= Stage 2: The ECL equivalent calculation is referenced to the lifetime of the underlying AC asset;

« Stage 3 : A specific provision is calculated for those exposures where the discounted recovery values (including
holding costs) of the underlying collateral are lower than the Bank's gross exposure; and

= The current base/good/bad scenario split for the Bank's loan exposure remains at 20%/5%/75% split, which the Bank
considers reflects the current macroeconomic environment.

For the AC assets where collateral has been pledged, the associated collateral is discounted and thereon applied to the
initial ECL to arrive at a final reported ECL. In accordance with the Bank's credit and investment policy (see note 29)
the Bank monitors the market value of collateral and may request additional collateral when necessary in accordance with
the underlying contractual agreement.

iii) Forward looking information

In its ECL models, the Bank relies on a broad range of forward looking information as the inputs and models for PDs and
LGDs used for calculating the ECL may not always capture all characteristics of the market at the reporting date. As
additional adjustment to the model includes economic market data in relation to:

* House price indices;

= Unemployment rates;

= Interest rates.

The Bank’s Risk Management department monitor, on an ongoing basis, market and economic factors when considering
applicable amendments to model parameters that are considered to directly impact the ECL provision.

To reflect general uncertainties, an adjustment to the model is applied to the ECL calculation, whereby a LGD floor of
1% is incorporated into the ECL model.

L) HEDGE ACCOUNTING

The Bank enters into derivative instruments including foreign exchange forwards, swaps and fx options to manage
exposures to interest rates and foreign currency risks, including exposures arising from forecast transactions. In order to
manage particular risks, the Bank applies hedge accounting for transactions which meet the specified criteria. Derivatives
held for hedging purposes are stated at fair value.

At inception of the hedge relationship, the Bank formally documents the relationship between the hedged item and the
hedging instrument, including the nature of the risk, management objectives and strategy for undertaking the hedge. The
Bank performs statistical regression analysis to assess the effectiveness of the hedging relationship, which forms part of
the Bank's documentation. The Bank has adopted IFRS 9 for applying the requirements of hedge accounting.

For the purposes of hedge accounting, hedges are classified into two categories: (i) fair value hedges which hedge the
exposure to changes in the fair value of a recognised asset or liability; and (ii) cash flow hedges which hedge exposure to
variability in cash flows that is attributable to a particular risk associated with a recognised asset or liability, or a profit
transaction.

1) Fair value hedges

For fair value hedges which meet the conditions for hedge accounting, any gain or loss from re-measuring the hedging
instrument at fair value is recognised immediately in the consolidated statement of income. The hedged item is adjusted
for fair value changes relating to the risk being hedged and the difference is recognised in the consolidated income
statement.

If the hedging instrument expires or is sold, terminated or exercised, or where the hedge no longer meets the criteria for
hedge accounting, the hedge relationship is terminated. For hedged items recorded at amortised cost, the difference
between the carrying value of the hedged item on termination and the value at which it would have been carried without
being hedged is amortised over the remaining term of the original hedge. If the hedged item is derecogniséd, thé
unamortised fair value adjustment is recognised immediately in the consolidated income statement.

In accordance with its hedging strategy, the Bank matches the principal of the hedging instruments to the principal of the
hedged items, including prepayment expectations. The Bank uses pay fixed/receive floating interest rate swaps to hedge
its fixed rate debt instruments and loans and pay floating/receive fixed interest rate swaps to hedge its fixed rate liabilities.

Hedge ineffectiveness can arise from:

" Differences in timing of cash flows of hedged items and hedging instruments;

« Different interest rate curves applied to discount the hedged items and hedging instruments;

« Derivatives used as hedging instruments having a non-nil fair value at the time of designation; and

"The effect of changes in counterparties’ credit risk on the fair values of hedging instruments or hedged items.

ii) Cash flow hedges

For cash flow hedges which meet the conditions for hedge accounting, the portion of the gain or loss on the hedging
instrument which is determined to be an effective hedge is recognised initially in equity. The ineffective portion of the
gain or loss, if any, on the hedging instrument is recognised immediately in the consolidated statement of income within
investment income.

The gains or losses on effective cash flow hedges recognised initially in equity are transferred to the consolidated
statement of income in the period in which the hedged transaction impacts the consolidated statement of income.

The possible sources of ineffectiveness for cash flow hedges are generally the same as those for fair value hedges,

described above. However, for cash flow hedges, prepayment risk is less relevant, and the causes of hedging
ineffectiveness arise from the changes in the timing and the amount of forecast future cash flows.

V) FINANCIAL GUARANTEES AND LOAN COMMITMENTS

In the ordinary course of business, the Group gives financial guarantees, consisting of letters of credit, guarantees and
acceptances. Financial guarantees are contracts that require the Group to make specified payments to reimburse the
holders for a loss that is incurred because a specified holder fails to make payments when due in accordance with the
terms of a debt instrument. Loan commitments are firm commitments to provide credit under pre-specified terms and
conditions. Financial guarantees are initially recognised in the consolidated financial statements at fair value. Subsequent
to initial recognition, the contract will be measured at the higher of the amount of the loss allowance or the amount initially
recognised less, when appropriate and relevant, the cumulative amount of income recognised.

W) WRITE OFFS

The Bank monitors impaired assets within the Risk Committee. Where there is an objective evidence that there is no
realistic prospect of future recovery and all collateral has been realised, any residual net carrying value of the asset is then
written off. Such events are managed by the Risk Management department and notified to the Board in its next meeting.
If a write-off is later recovered, the recovery is credited to the income statement within credit provision (loss)/recovery.

X) LEASES

i) The Bank as a lessee

The Bank assesses at contract inception whether a contract is, or contains, a lease. That is, if the contract conveys the
right to control the use of an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for consideration. The Bank's leasing
activities are primarily in relation to agreements to rent office space and office equipment for carrying out its business
activities.

The Bank applies a single recognition and measurement approach for all leases, whereby the Bank recognises lease
liabilities to make lease payments and right-of-use assets representing the right to use the underlying assets.

- Right-of-Use Assets

The Bank recognises right-of-use assets at the commencement date of the lease. Right-of-use assets are measured at cost
which is the aggregate of the amount of lease liabilities recognised, less any lease incentives received, initial direct costs
incurred and an estimation of costs to be incurred to restore the underlying asset to the condition required by the terms of
the lease. Right-of-use assets are depreciated on a straight-line basis over the lease term, as follows:

* 35 Portman Square lease contract - until December 2026.

Right-of-use assets are subsequently measured at cost, less depreciation, and tested annually for impairment in accordance
with IAS 36. Due to the nature of the underlying lease agreements, the Bank considers the probability of an impairment
event to be unlikely. ‘

- Lease Liabilities

At the commencement date of the lease, the Bank recognises individual lease liabilities measured as the present value of
lease payments (excluding VAT) to be made over the lease term. Payments in this regard comprise solely lease payments
and do not include any non-lease components included within the contractual arrangements.

Where the lease contains a break or extension option clause, the Bank assesses whether it is reasonably certain that the
option would be exercised by the Bank, considering all relevant facts and economic conditions including market rates,
period remaining of the contract terms and any associated costs that would be incurred when triggering the option. A key
determination that an option would be triggered by the Bank would be a formal decision by either the Board or
Management Committee to trigger an option.

Where an interest rate is not readily determinable in the lease contract, the Bank is required to estimate an incremental

borrowing rate (IBR) at the lease commencement date to calculate the present value of lease payments. The IBR should

represent what the Bank would have to pay to borrow the aggregate sum of undiscounted lease payments over a similar
duration and in the same currency. The Bank determines the IBR as follows:

« Where the aggregate lease payments are less than $200,000, the IBR is based on the Bank's internal funding model
referencing the internal rate applied for equity reserves at the date of the contract commencement. The difference
between an IBR based on borrowing costs and one based on equity reserves is considered immaterial for such
contracts;

« For all other leases, the IBR is based on debt issuances by UK financial institutions with an equivalent external rating
as the Bank, for the same duration as the lease contract, and in the same currency.

After the commencement date, the amount of lease liabilities is increased to reflect the accretion of interest and reduced
for the lease payments made. ;

it) The Bank as a lessor

Leasing activities are not considered a core part of the Bank's business and the Bank does not actively engage in similar

products. For sub-lease contracts the Bank, as an intermediate lessor, is required to assess the contract to determine

whether a finance or operating lease exists. The Bank considers the following key criteria in this respect:

« Whether the lease transfers ownership of the asset to the sub-lessee at the end of the lease term;

« Whether the lease contains an option to purchase the asset at a price lower than fair value;

* Whether the lease term of the contract is for the major part of the economic life of the asset available to the Bank,
based on the non-cancellable period of the sub-lease. In this regard, the Bank assesses whether the option to cancel
will be triggered, or not, considering all relevant facts and economic conditions including market rates; and

« The economic substance of the lease agreement, by comparing rent receipts with indicative market rates.

26 DERIVATIVES

In the ordinary course of business the Bank enters into various types of transactions that involve derivative financial
instruments. A derivative financial instrument is a financial contract between two parties where its value changes in
response to the change in a specified interest rate, foreign exchange rate or other variable, there is little or no initial net
investment, and settlement is based in the future.

Derivatives include financial options, futures and forwards, interest rate swaps and currency swaps, which create rights and
obligations that have the effect of transferring between the parties of the instrument one or more of the financial risks
inherent in an underlying primary financial instrument. On inception, a derivative financial instrument gives one party a
contractual right to exchange financial assets or financial liabilities with another party under conditions that are potentially
favourable, or a contractual obligation to exchange financial assets or financial liabilities with another party under
conditions that are potentially unfavourable. However, they generally do not result in a transfer of the underlying primary
financial instrument on inception of the contract, nor does such a transfer necessarily take place on maturity of the contract.

Some instruments embody both a right and an obligation to make an exchange. Because the terms of the exchange are
determined on inception of the derivative instruments, as prices in financial markets change those terms may become either
favourable or unfavourable.

The Bank uses forward foreign exchange and swap contracts as part of its asset and liability management process to hedge
underlying on-balance sheet positions. The use of derivatives is controlled through the Bank's Risk Management
department which is an independent area of the Bank. The Risk Management department is responsible for ensuring that
all interest and exchange rate risk is undertaken within the policy guidelines set by the Board and the Asset and Liability
Committee.

Interest rate risk

At 31 December 2023, the Bank had interest rate swap contracts in place whereby the Bank pays a fixed rate against underlying assets
(receives for underlying deposits) and thereon receives a variable rate of return (pays for deposits). The swaps are being used to hedge the
exposure to changes in the fair value of its fixed rate bonds, loans and customer deposits.
Repricing risk arises from timing differences in the maturity of fixed rate assets and liabilities in comparison to the repricing floating rate
during the same period. Such repricing mismatches expose interest results to abnormal fluctuations where there are unforeseen interest rates
movements. The Bank therefore manages such interest rate risk by entering into interest rate swap contracts on a micro basis, where on an
individual transaction level the critical terms of both the hedge item and hedging instrument match, including notional amount, maturity date,
fixed rate and coupon frequency.
The Bank's Risk management department monitor such risk within the policy framework of ‘interest rate risk in the banking book’ and the
results are reported to the Bank's Asset and Liability Committee (ALCO) on a monthly basis. In addition, any hedge ineffectiveness, both
cumulative life to date and current year, are also reported on a monthly basis to the Bank's ALCO. On the basis of the Bank's micro hedging
strategy, ineffectiveness is not considered to materially impact the hedging relationship, and as at 31 December 2023 represented 0.02% of
gross interest rate swap notional.

29 CREDIT RISK

Credit Risk is the risk arising from a counterpart's failure to discharge on time and in full as contracted and cause the other party to
incur a financial loss. Credit Risk arises principally from lending but also from other transactions involving on and off balance sheet
instruments. The Risk Committee in conjunction with the Risk Management department have the responsibility for developing and
implementing policies to ensure that all exposures are properly pre-approved, measured and controlled, With the exception of certain
small retail loans, all credits are independently pre-approved including, for credits above a set limit, by the Parent Undertaking's
Executive Committee. Credit proposals are put through a comprehensive risk assessment which examines the customer’s financial
condition and trading performance, nature of the business, quality of management and market position. In addition, an internal risk
rating model scores these quantitative and qualitative factors. The credit approval decision is then made and terms and conditions set.

Exposure limits are based on the aggregate exposure to the counterparty. All credit exposures are reviewed at least annually.
Measurement of credits includes geographic, product, market and individual counterparty concentration.

All credit exposures are checked daily against approved limits, independently of each business unit, and are reported to senior
management.

The Bank's first priority when making real estate loans is to establish the borrower’s capacity to repay and not rely principally on
security/collateral. Loans are analysed into the following categories:

- Retail : principally individual customers' consumer loans, residential mortgages and overdrafts.

- Corporate : principally loans and other credit facilities for corporate and institutional customers. Also included are credit facilities to
high net worth individuals.

The majority of loans are secured on assets, for which the main geographical credit exposure is to the UK market. Acceptable forms of
collateral are defined within the credit risk framework and conservative valuation parameters are also pre-set and regularly reviewed to
reflect any changes in market conditions. Security structures and legal covenants are also subject to regular review to ensure that they
continue to fulfil their intended purpose and remain in line with market practice. Guidelines are in place regarding the acceptability of
types of collateral and valuation parameters.
The principal collateral types are as follows:

- mortgages over residential properties;

- charges over business assets such as premises, plant, stock and receivables;

- charges over commercial properties being financed; and

- charges over financial instruments, such as debt securities and equities.

The Bank monitors the market value of collateral, periodically by type of asset using a variety of techniques including indexation.
Where appropriate, and in accordance with the underlying agreement, the Bank can request additional collateral.
In relation to loan exposures, nil possession orders were obtained and enforced during 2023 (2022: nil).

It is the Bank's policy to maintain consistent internal risk ratings across the credit portfolio. The credit quality of the portfolio of loans
and advances, debt instruments at amortised cost and deposits with banks, that are neither past due nor impaired can be assessed by
reference to the AUB Group's internal credit rating system. This facilitates focused portfolio management of the inherent risk across all
lines of business.

The four credit quality internal risk ratings given can be equated to the following

High Standard Risk Rating | to 4 Undoubted through to Good Credit Risk
Standard Risk Rating 5 to 6 Satisfactory through to Adequate Credit Risk
Watch list Risk Rating 7 Watch List Credit Risk

Downgrades to risk rating 8 occur where a bankruptcy petition has been filed or similar action taken, irrespective of whether payments
on the obligation are being continued.

31 MARKET RISK

Market risk is the risk of potential financial loss that may arise from adverse changes in the value of financial

instruments or portfolios of financial instruments due to movements in interest rates, foreign exchange rates or other
prices and volatilities. This risk arises from asset-liability mismatches, changes that occur in the yield curve, foreign

exchange rates and changes in volatilities/implied volatilities in the market value of derivatives. The Bank classifies
exposures to market risk into either trading or non-trading portfolios. Given the Bank's low risk strategy, aggregate

market risk levels are considered low. The Bank utilises Value-at-Risk (VaR) models to assist in estimating potential
losses that may arise from adverse market movements in addition to non-quantitative risk management techniques. The
market risk for the trading portfolio is managed and monitored on a VaR methodology which reflects the inter-
dependency between risk variables. Non-trading portfolios are managed and monitored using stop loss limits and other
sensitivity analyses.

VaR is a technique which estimates the potential losses that could occur on risk positions taken due to movements in
market rates and prices over a specified time horizon and to a given level of confidence. VaR is calculated on an
historical simulation basis, using one-day movements in market rates and prices, a 99% confidence level and taking
into account the actual correlations observed historically between different markets and rates. The one-day movement
in market prices is calculated by reference to market data from a 260 trading day history. VaR should be viewed in the
context of the limitations of the methodology used.

~ The use of a one-day holding period assumes that all positions can be liquidated or hedged in one day; this does not
fully capture the market risk arising from times of illiquidity, when one-day liquidation or hedging may not be possible,
- The use of a 99% confidence measure does not take account of any losses that might occur beyond this level of
confidence.

- The use of historical data as a proxy for estimating future events may not include all potential events, particularly
those that aré éxtréine in nature.

- Correlations in the future may change from those correlations observed in the past.

~ VaR is calculated at the close of business with intra-day exposures not being subject to intra-day VaR calculations.

32 LIQUIDITY RISK

Liquidity risk is the risk that the Bank does not have sufficient financial resources to meet its obligations as they fall
due, or will have to do so at an excessive cost. This risk arises from mismatches in the timing of cash flows. Funding
risk arises when the necessary liquidity to fund illiquid asset positions cannot be obtained at the expected terms and
when required.

The Bank maintains a stable funding base comprising core retail and corporate customer deposits and institutional
balances, augmented by wholesale funding and portfolios of highly liquid assets which are diversified by currency and
maturity, in order to enable the Bank to respond quickly to any unforeseen liquidity requirements.

Treasury limits are set by the Asset and Liability Committee. Specifically the Asset and Liability Committee and the
Bank Treasurer are responsible for:

- projecting cash flows by major currency under various stress scenarios and considering the level of liquid
assets necessary in relation thereto;

- monitoring balance sheet liquidity ratios against internal and regulatory requirements;

- maintaining a diverse range of funding sources with adequate back-up facilities;

- managing the concentration and profile of debt maturities;

- managing contingent liquidity commitment exposures within predetermined caps;

- monitoring depositor concentration in order to avoid undue reliance on large individual depositors and ensure
a satisfactory overall funding mix; and ,

- maintaining liquidity and funding contingency plans. These plans must identify early indicators of stress
conditions and describe actions to be taken in the event of difficulties arising from-systemic or other crises
while minimising adverse long-term implications for the business.

Market disruptions or a credit downgrade, for example, may reduce the availability of certain sources of funding. To
guard against this risk, management has diversified funding sources and assets are managed with liquidity in mind,
maintaining a healthy balance of cash, cash equivalents and readily marketable securities.

36 EMPLOYEE BENEFITS
Within the UK, the Bank operates a defined contribution pension scheme (“the Scheme”) which is open to current employees. Contributions to
the Scheme are charged directly to the statement of income.

The Bank also operates a funded defined benefit scheme (“the Fund”) for its employees who joined prior to 1 March 2001, The Fund was a final
salary scheme which became closed to future accrual with effect from 31 March 2010. The weighted average duration of the expected benefit
payments from the Fund is around 13 years.

The Fund is registered with HMRC for tax purposes, and is operated separately from the Bank and managed by a set of Trustees. The Trustees are
responsible for the payment of benefits and the management of the scheme’s assets which are held in a separate Trustee administered fund. The
Fund is subject to UK regulations, which require the Bank and the Trustees to agree a funding strategy and contributions schedule.

The funding plan for the Fund is to hold assets equal to the value of the benefits earned by members of the Fund, based on a set of assumptions
used for funding the Fund. The funding assumptions differ from the assumptions used to calculate the figures in these accounts, and therefore
produce different results. If there is a shortfall against the funding plan, then the Bank and Trustees agree on deficit contributions to make good
the deficit over a period of time.

During the year, the Trustees of the Fund changed the Fund’s investment strategy and invested the Fund’s assets in a buy-in insurance policy ~ an
insurance policy which pays an income to the Fund which matches the pensions to be paid to members. While the Bank remains legally
responsible for supporting the Fund, investing in this policy has removed the Bank’s exposure to investment and funding risks in the Fund.

Contributions are set based on funding valuations carried out every three years. The latest completed triennial actuarial funding valuation was
carried out with an effective date of 31 December 2020, with the next valuation due as at 31 December 2023. The 2020 valuation indicated that on
a funding basis, there was a deficit of $30.4 million (£22.3 million translated at 2020 year-end FX rate of 1.3644). As a result, deficit
contributions of $5.3 million (£3.9 million) per annum payable monthly until 31 October 2023 were agreed. Because the insurance policy fully
covers benefits payable from the Fund and the premium paid could be met entirely from the Fund’s existing assets, the Bank and the Trustees
agreed that the Bank could cease paying deficit contributions with effect from 15 June 2023.

As a result, the amount of total employer contributions paid into the Fund during 2023 is $3 million (£2.4 million) (2022 actual: $4.8 million /
£3.9 million). No further deficit contributions are anticipated to be paid in 2024.

Financial assumtions - summary
The key assumptions at 31 December 2023 have been derived consistently with those adopted at 31 Décember 2022. The changes in
financial assumptions therefore reflect only changes in underlying financial conditions.

Key financial assumptions:

Assumptions are set by reference to yield curves at the relevant accounting dates and reflect the Fund's projected benefit payment
profile. The above assumptions are the single equivalent assumptions that give the same value of the IAS 19 liabilities as if the
figures were calculated using the yield curves described below.

Discount rate:

IAS 19 requires that the discount rate is set based on yields of high quality corporate bonds at the balance sheet date.

We have constructed a yield curve from high quality (AA rated) corporate bonds. To derive the discount rate, the yield curve is
weighted according to scheme cash flows, which have an average duration of about 13 years (31 December 2022: duration of 14
years). The approach used to set the discount rate is in line with the “LCP Treasury Model.

The yield curve used to set the discount rate has been constructed as follows:

¢ Data on corporate bond yields is obtained as at the accounting date.

« Bonds are included in the data set if they are classified as “AA” and “Corporate”, in line with the requirements of IAS19.

* Bonds are classified as AA if they are rated AA by the index provider.

* Reasonableness checks are carried out on the data, in particular to ensure that we use bonds only where the prices supplied and
yields are consistent.

* A yield curve is fitted to the data using least squares optimisation techniques, such that the forward rate at year 30 and above is
equal to the average forward rate over the 15 to 30 year period.

Inflation and pension increase assumptions
IAS 19 requires that assumptions for inflation are best estimate, based on market expectations at the balance sheet date.

Benefits from the scheme depend on both the RPI and CPI price indices, which are constructed differently. The approach to setting
the RPI and CPI inflation assumptions is unchanged from last year. The assumption for future RPI inflation is derived from the
breakeven measure implied by the difference between nominal yields on fixed interest gilts and real yields on RPI linked gilts. The
single equivalent breakeven inflation rate, weighted by scheme cash flows, is 3.3% pa.

RPI inflation: The best estimate, market consistent assumption for RPI is derived by deducting an inflation risk premium from
breakeven inflation. Consistent with last year, we have allowed for an inflation risk premium of 0.2% pa. Overall, this results in a
single weighted average RPI assumption of 3.1% pa (reflecting breakeven inflation of 3.3% pa less an average inflation risk premium
of 0.2% pa).

Inflation and pension increase assumptions (continued)

CPI inflation: The assumption for CPI inflation is set by deducting a “wedge” from the RPI inflation assumption, to reflect structural
differences between the indices. The CPI assumption is derived as follows:

* Pre 2030: a wedge of 1.0% is deducted.

* 2030 onwards: a wedge of 0.1%. This reflects the small expected differences between the CPIH index, which will be used to
calculate RPI from 2030, and the CPI index. We assume that CPIH inflation will be higher than CPI inflation because CPIH includes
an allowance for owner occupied housing costs, which tend to increase at a higher rate than the general price level.

On 1 December 2023 the ONS announced changes to the RPI, CPI and CPIH indices. Historical data released alongside this suggests
there may be a slightly larger gap in the future between CPIH and CPI than was previously expected. In our view the existing
assumption of 0.1% remains appropriate.

Overall, this results in a single weighted average CPI assumption of 2.2% pa, reflecting RPI inflation of 3.1% pa less an average
wedge of 0.9% pa.

Pension increases: In setting the assumed level of future pension increases, we have adjusted the relevant inflation assumption to
allow for the volatility of future inflation and the maximum and minimum increases. For this purpose, we have used assumed
volatility of 1.5% pa for both RPI and CPI inflation. This is the same approach as was used at the previous year-end.

Mortality ,

The assumption for post-retirement life expectancy is important and uncertain, particularly when considering the projection of future
changes in mortality rates. The widely used CMI model requires a number of judgements and parameters to be set. With the
exception of the mortality projection assumptions, all demographic assumptions are the same as used last year end. The effect of the
change in life expectancy assumptions over the year is to reduce the defined benefit obligation (and the value placed on the insurance
policies) by around 1.5%, or £1.5m.

Transfer values

The Bank has made no explicit assumption for how many members will transfer out of the Fund in future. This is because there is
little relevant historical data on past transfers on which to base a robust assumption and whether past experience is a reliable guide to
the future. The figures implicitly assume that future transfer values would be cost neutral on the accounting basis.

Commutation
It is assumed that members commute 20% of pension at retirement based on current factors. This is consistent with the approach

taken last year and the assumed percentage commuted is consistent with the membership experience observed by the Trustees of the
Fund over the three years to 31 December 2020 (the date of the last actuarial valuation).

We have updated the terms under which members are assumed to commute pension to the member option terms to match those
offered by Aviva, to ensure that there is no mismatch between benefit payments from the Fund and Aviva’s payments to the Fund. As
well as affecting the value of the defined benefit obligation, changing the commutation factors changes the asset value by a similar
amount).

Virgin Media v NTL Pension Trustees [1 Limited (and others) - High Court judgment.

On 16 June 2023, the High Court passed judgment in relation to Virgin Media v NTL Pension Trustees II Limited (and others). This
judgment confirmed that certain rules of a contracted-out scheme cannot be altered without the statutory actuarial confirmation
having been obtained and that non-compliant alterations would retrospectively become void. For the Bank’s pension scheme, this
covers the period 6 April 1997 to 31 March 2010.

During this period there were 9 deed amendments to the pension scheme rules, of which 16 included amendments that could
potentially affect benefits accrued after 6 April 1997. At this point in time the Trustees, and legal advisors to the pension scheme, are
not in a position to determine whether the required documentation (“section 37” certificate) was provided and thereon assess whether
the impact could be material or not. Notwithstanding, the Bank is of the opinion that the Trustees, the pension actuary and the
scheme lawyers along with the Bank would have taken all the necessary steps to ensure that changes would have been validly made
at the time.

The appeal date for the ruling has been set for 25-26 June 2024. The Trustees of the pension scheme have been advised to await the
outcome of that appeal before investigating what documentation was in place at that time when the deed amendment occurred. Until
then, the Bank is unable to determine the impact, if any, or a reliable estimate of any impact on the financial statements.
