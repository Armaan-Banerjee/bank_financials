TOWARDS FURTHER CO-OPERATION

While much of the economic news over the past
year has been gloomy in the extreme there have
been brighter aspects, not the least of which has
been the steady, if not spectacular, progress in the
further development of a meaningful Co-operative
sector within the United Kingdom economy. Closely
involved in these activities has been the Co-operative
Bank Limited. The Bank has made considerable
progress in its aim of providing a comprehensive
financial service not only for existing Co-operative
retail and wholesale societies and their members,
who ultimately own the Bank, but also in other
developing areas where those involved are attracted
towards the concept of Co-operation. At the same
time, through the continued adoption of the most
advanced technology available, the Bank is ensuring
that the advances of the 1970's can be maintained
by methods of operation which enable it to advance into the eighties as a strongly competitive
financial institution. In the development and implementation stage this activity is unavoidably
expensive in both manpower and capital investment terms. However, by stressing, in line with
Co-operative principles, the real advantages of price and convenience over other institutions and
ploughing back accrued surplus into the organisation to the direct benefit of customers in order to
maintain these advantages, the Bank Group has continued to expand its custorner base ata signifi-
cant rate, Group operating profit of £6-2 million, 12 per cent higher than for 1978, is deemed
satisfactory particularly as high interest rates prevalent throughout the year, although favourable to
the Bank's trading operations, did not always operate in favour of all constituent companies of the
roup,

Economic and Financial Problems

There were clear signs during the year that official responses to the severe problams facing the
domestic economy would be radically different to the approach of recant years. Few could cavil
at the new Government's sincere desire to curb the rate of increase in retail prices, but the present
policies with a heavy emphasis on money supply control have, paradoxically, so far resulted in an
increased rate of inflation, The level of interest rates has also now ceased to be a policy aim in
itself, becoming instead more a residual factor "1 the money supply equation, to be adjusted to
bring other elements into balance.

High rates of interest might be tolerated more easily if they could be seen to be effective in their
designated purpose but with demand remaining buoyant and seemingly insensitive to price, this
policy was a clear failure fas. year, The clamour for credit represents good business for the banks,
but it is important to view this in the context of the penalties imposed by the authorities on the
expansion of bank activities. Throughout 1979 the Grtish banking system has been subjected
to the Supplementary Special Deposits scheme — the ‘corset’ ~ which imposes penalties on banks
whose rate of growth of interest-bearing eligible liatilities (IBELS) exceeds a fixed amount each
month. Thus banks faced the dilemma of either choosing to meet continuing demand for funds and
thus attracting a penalty, or curbing their lending and offending their customers. Because of the
nature of the overdraft system, this problem is very largely answered for the banks; their commit-
ments to iend far exceed the permitted expansion, and consequently some banks have been forced
to incur penalties under the scheme. To avoid the more severe penalty areas relating to deposits
growth the Co-operative Bank has reduced its investment portfolio.

Group Financial Performance

Consolidated Group operating profit for the year reached a new record of £6-2 million, an increase
of 12:0 per cent over 1978. The trading profit of tha parent Bank was well in line with competitor
banks’ domestic operations but the impact of contheing high interest rates created problems for
the other major Group company — F, C. Finance Limited - where profits were well down on ex-
pectations, However, after allowing for taxation the Group was able to supplement its capital base
by £5-9 million of retained earnings, thus increasing significantly the amount of shareholders’ funds.

The Group's consolidated balance sheet total of £598 million at the year end showed a sharp
increase of 30 per cent over the equivalent figure of the previous year.

Co-operative Bank Limited

At the year end the parent Bank had 15 per cent more customer accounts than a year earlier thus
maintaining the rate of expansion of recent years, although operating from a steadily increasing
base. The number of personal borrowing accounts increased by more than one-third during the

year.

Corporate custamers bringing their business to the Bank have also increased sharply in numbers.
This has been particularly apparent in towns where the Bank's growing number of branches have
been recently established ~ a further regional branch has opened at Luton during the year ~ and
where small scale businesses are found in considerable numbers. To achieve industrial resurgence
for the country as a whole, we believe the foundations must be laid at a local Jevel and, with this
in mind, plans are well advanced for opening many more branches of the Bank in selected medium-
sized towns over the nextten years. During 1980 branches will be operied in Brighton, Southampton,
and Exeter,

Offering an efficient and attractive banking service in all parts of the United Kingdom depends as
much on a high quality of centralised back-up service as a good performance at the jocal banking
outlet, In this respect the Bank’s Customer Service Bureau at Skelmersdale is of vital importance,
It started operations in spring 1979 using the latest technology to process the increasing amounts
of paper flowing through the system. By late summer the new main frame computer, housing the
total customer records and with many times the capacity of the old configuration in Manchester, had
been installed, tested and become fully operational. Centralisation of production to meet many
customer requirements ~- cheque books, statements, cherjue guarantee cards, etc, — has also become
possible with the latest word processing equipment making the praduction of the majority of
standard types of letter very simple indeed.

All these moves have presented the Bank with the opportunity to streamline its methods of customer
contact, while at the same time ensuring a more personal and friendly approach. As routine back-
room activity is automated, branch staff will have more time to deat with specific customer require-
ments, Seizing this opportunity, the Bank is introducing a “Personal Banker” approach under
which specially trained staff are allotted specific responsibility for the total requirements of groups
f comers,

While the main emphasis of the Bank's activity over the past year has concerned the domestic
United Kingdom operations, important steps have been taken to pave the way for increased inter-
national activities. In its first entry into the Eurocurrency capital markets the Bank has raised
US $25 million by means of a floating rate capital note issue. The supporting management group
for the issue included many of the major European co-operative banks. This relationship was
consistent with the Bank's important role within the Association of Co-operative Savings and
Credit Institutions of the EEC, based in Brussels, and the International Confederation of Popular
Credit (CICP) in Paris, The Bank has acted as host to various meetings of both these and other
international groups during the past year, including a meeting of the policy making central committee
of the CICP, the Banking Committee of the international Co-operative Alliance, and has also
participated in a number of exhibitions and trade fairs in Europe. In ._..sequence of all this activity
the share of the Bank's total business in international affairs is growing steadily, although there is
a ready appreciation that much remains to be achieved.

F. C. Finance Limited

The adverse effects of high interest rates throughout the year davxayed seriously the profit per-
formance of F. C. Finance Limited. Prevailing conditions neutralised the useful new business
growth achieved in the previous year. Gross income was 24% higher but was inadequate in the
face of a 54% increase in the cost of money and further sharp rises in overheads,

On a more cheerful note the company has a record level of unrealised charges, and is continuing
to expand, wherever feasible, business linked to floating rates. However, in anticipation of eventu-
ally reducing money costs the present emphasis must remain on the higher fixed rate arrangements.
Changes in the mix of business have continued, with activity In personal and industrial credit, and
in leasing, expanding compared with further reductions in property and construction. A creditable
performance was achieved during the year by the associated company Crowngap Limited,

The parent Bank's introduction of Handycard and Handyloan schemes for customers of co-operative
retail societies gave F. C. Finance the oppartunity to concentrate on extending its association with
the retail societies in the traditional areas of leasing and loan and instalment credit facilities for
equipment, plant and vehicles.

Accounting Policies

Aecounting Policies which have changed this year

is) Deferred Taxation

Fotlowing the introduction of SSAP 15, deferred taxation on the liability method is only provided on short-term
timing differences as it is thought that taxation deferred by other timing differences v.u:1 not become payable
in the foreseeable future. No provision is made in respect of capital allowances on fixed assets or leased plant
and equipment. Previously provision was made on the liability methad on all accsunting and taxation timing
differences and for the potential taxation on surpluses atising from the revaluation of properties,

(b) Losses on Advances

The amount charged against operating profit for losses on advances comprises specific provision against
identitiable losses and a general provision to cover latent but unidentifiable losses due to doubtful debts. Both
provisions are based on a year end appraisal of advances. Advances are shown on the balance sheet after

deducting these provisions,

This year the level of the general provision has been reassessed in the light of current general banking practice.
The comparative figures for 1978 have been restated and the release relating to earlier years has been credited
to reserves,

(c) Premium on acquisition of shares
The premium paid on the acquisition of shares in subsidiaries, being the excess of the amount paid over the

net tangible assets at the date of acquisition, has now been written off against reserves, Previously this premium
was shown on the balance sheet as an intangible asset,

(d) Comparative figures

The comparative figures for 1978 have all been restated to conform with the revised accounting policies stated
above,

Accounting Policies which have not changed

(e) Basis of consolidation and accounting date

The accounts of the group which are made up for the 52 weeks to 12th January, 1980, incorporate the accounts
of F, C. Finance Limited and its subsidiaries which are made up for the year ended 31st December, 1979.
Since the group accounting date is virtually coterminous with the calendar year 1979 the financial year's
figures are headed 1979 and the corresponding figures for the previous year are headed 1978,

(f) (i) Investment income

Income from investments includes amortization of premiums and discounts on dated stocks, credit for which
is taken in equal annual instalments from the date of acquisition to maturity. The premiums and discounts on
investments acquired from CWS are based on the market value of these investments at 10th July, 1971, at
which date the banking activities of CWS were transferred to and vested in Co-operative Bank Limited.

(ii) Sale of Investments

Realised profits and losses on sales of investments are taken to profit and loss account in the year in which
they arise.

12
(g) Depreciation

Depreciation of freehold buildings is provided at 24% of the original purchase price or professional valuation.
Depreciation of leasehold land and buildings is provided at 24% or in equal annual instalments over the life
of the lease if it is less than 40 years. Depreciation of equipment and fittings is provided in equal annual
instalments over the estimated working lives of the assets,

(h) Hire Purchase and instalment credit agreements

Credit is taken in the profit and loss account for finance charges on hire purchase and instalment credit agree-
ments, net of dealers’ commission, using the “rule of 78,” with the exception of agreements having a duration
of five years and over where finance charges are allocated on an actuarial basis.

(i) Leasing
Leased equipment is valued at the actuarial proportion of future rental income attributable to original cost.

(j) Development land and work in progress

Development land and work in progress is valued at the lower of cost, including normal finance charges, and
net value realisable in the ordinary course of business. Receipts in respect of part disposals are deducted from

cost. Credit is taken for 80% of the estimated profit included in receipts on developments which are substan-
tially complete.

(k) Extraordinary items

If items arise which are not within the normal activities of the Bank and its subsidiaries then they are dealt
with in the profit and loss account as extraordinary items. These include profits and losses on the disposal of
the companies’ premises. ‘

(Il) Associated companies

In the consolidated balance sheet associated companies are shown at cost, including advances, less amounts
written off, and the group’s share of the retained profits and losses from the date of acquisition. .

(m) Exchange rates
Balances in foreign currencies are expressed in sterling at the rate ruling at the balance sheet date.

Miecting the Challenge

The Bank maintained a remarkable rate of growth in
numbers of accounts during the 1970's. In the last
five years the total number increased by at least 16 per
cent each year on a steadily rising base to reach nearly
700,000 by the end of 1979.

At the same time the Bank developed and expanded
its range of services to personal customers, to retail
co-operative societies and to business customers.

The new decade promises further growth both in
numbers of customers and accounts, and in the range
of services offered, further advances which require
careful planning to ensure that the increased workload
does not have the effect of damaging the Bank's repu-
tation for friendly and personal service.

The key to achieving this is the development of the
Customer Service Bureau at Delf House, Skelmersdale.
The advanced technology installed there has the
Capacity to deal with the ever growing amounts of

paper which are an inescapable part of the life of a
ank,

Indeed, if it were not for Delf House, the Co-operative
Bank's branches might well have ground slowly to a
halt as an ever rising tide of paper swamped their
back offices.

The Customer Service Bureau has taken the burden- °

some and often mundane task of handling paper work
away from branch office staff, thus allowing them more
time to concentrate on providing the best possible
service to the customer. ’

Far from the CSB’s high technology making the Bank
seem more remote to customers, the development is
in fact bringing branch staff and customers closer
together.

An important move in that direction has been the
introduction of Personal Bankers in a number of
branches-specially trained staff whose job it is to make
sure that particular customers allocated to their care
receive the best possible service from the Bank.

In a successful pilot scheme at six branches~Bristol,
London (Leman Street), Glasgow, Newcastle, Man-
chester and Derby—selected Personal Bankers were
allocated a group of customers who became their
special responsibility so that a permanent relationship
could be built up.

Customers with a problem or query knew who to turn
to for advice; they would ask for their own Personal
Banker, who would make it his or her business to ensure
that any query was dealt with quickly, efficiently and
with the personal touch.

The pilot scheme was so popular with customers that
it is to be extended to a further 17 branches during
1980, and may eventually be introduced to every bank
branch.

The prime objective of both the Customer Service
Bureau and Personal Banker developments is to main-
tain and build on the Bank's good name for personal
service. That, together with the convenience offered
to customers through the Handybank and Cash-a-
cheque network, ‘forms the basis of the Bank's plans
to meet the challenge of the 1980's.
