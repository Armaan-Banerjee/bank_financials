Business Model

Redwood Bank (the “Bank’) has, in its second full year of trading, continued to pursue its purpose of meeting the funding
needs of both smalt and medium sized enterprises (SMEs) and individual property investors, mainly in its Heartlands
(Hertfordshire, Bedfordshire, Buckinghamshire, Cambridgeshire, Norfolk and Suffolk) and the North West. The business
model is based on the development of tailored business relationships, through a broker network, with customers and
manually underwriting of all lending. Lending takes the form of commercial, residential and refurbishment secured
mortgages, funded through Best Buy savings accounts and savings bonds offered to businesses, charities, clubs and
associations both directly and through cash management services, with varying maturities from 35 days to 1 year.

The Bank has identified four core values which guide the Bank’s behaviours, being simplicity, integrity, certainty and
tirelessness. The Bank prides itself on offering simple, transparent and time conscious business solutions to its customers,
which have begun to deliver brand loyalty and trust amongst customers. Lending products are targeted to be underwritten
within 48 hours, and savings products can be accessed almost instantly through the Bank's online infrastructure,
demonstrating the Bank's understanding of the value of customers’ time. The Bank's entire infrastructure is cloudbased,
allowing it to be nimbie in response te change, and resilient as staff can service customers from any location.

COVID 19

At the time of signing these accounts, the world is experiencing unprecedented disruption caused by the COVID-19
pandemic. This represents not only a public health and economic crisis, but a massive societal upheaval that is touching
each and every one of us. The Board will continue to assess the impact of COVID-19 on the Bank and its business model
as well as undertaking stress tests in line with regulatory guidance. The Bank is assessing its growth plans in line with new
information becoming available. The Employees of the Bank continue to work remotely with minimal disruptions due to the
Bank's cloud-based infrastructure.

Performance review

The Bank has increased its total lending by £90.8m in the year, mainly as a result of new fending totalling £101.8m during
the year, £29.8m higher than in 2018 (£72.0m) and partially offset by repayments and redemptions. Total deposits held by
the Bank increased by £108.5m during the year, with the Bank demonstrating an ability to generate liquidity quickly and
when necessary. The use of cash management services as an alternate source of deposits was successfully tested and
introduced during the year. The Bank held £86.8m in liquid assets at the year end, cornprising cash at bank, deposits at
the Bank of England, UK Treasury Bills and UK Gilts. The liquidity is sufficient for the Bank's immediate lending needs,
including a book acquisition carried out post year end (see post balance sheet events).

The Bank delivered a loss before tax of £1.2m, which was an improvement from the 2018 loss before tax of £3.8m. The
Bank invested in building its footprint through the acceleration of the appointment of BDMs and underwriters, whilst
extending its governance framework through the appointment of a director of finance, a risk manager and a new non-
executive director. The overall staff complement grew to 78 (61: 2018). The Bank also invested in a “slotting model" to
better categorise commercial loans for risk weighting purposes, thereby maximising its use of capital.

The Bank continued to utilise technology with the launch of CRM, which allowed a better tracking of customer information
and more robust Mi on lending performance. The Bank also began the implementation of a suite of new software modules,
including a regulatory reporting module, a treasury management module and an ALM module. Further modules being
considered in 2020 include a financial planning module, Effective Interest Rate (EIR) module, hedge accounting and Single
Customer View (SCV) modules.

The Bank continued to measure its performance with its customers and employees. The Bank obtained average customer
satisfaction scores of 85% for deposits and 83% for lending customers during the year. The Bank actively pursued both
internal reviews of employee satisfaction, as well as taking part in the Banking Standards Board Employee survey, in which
the Bank ranked first in 5 out of the 9 characteristics out of 29 banks for employees perception of Openness, Reliability,
Resilience, Responsiveness and Shared Purpose. The Bank has expanded its maternity and paternity policy in the year to
better support employees who are expanding their families and ensure we are competitive when attracting women to the
business. The Bank has signed up to the Women in Finance (WIF) Charter. The Bank has a target to include 30% female
represenation in senior managment by the end of 2021. The charter aims to increase the number of women in senior roles
within the financial sector is a commitment by HM Treasury as well as other financial institutions to work together to build a
more balanced industry.

The Bank was also the recipient of 12 Banking industry awards during the year and having been a finalist for a further six
awards.

The Bank’s shareholders have continued to show faith in the Bank’s business model and strategy by investing a further

Other key performance indicators

All staff have completed general conduct training and have each passed the requisite tests, and customer feedback has

Strategic Plan

The Bank continues to update its strategic plan annually, taking into consideration the current market climate and potential
future trends. The Board approved five-year plan considered significant macro-economic changes, such as those relating
to an economic downturn as a result of Brexit and/or COVID-19 and highlights the Bank's sensitivities to various factors
including base rate and NIM contractions. In line with the plan to grow the balance sheet both organically and through
acquisitions the Board approved the first purchase of a smail loan book, of £16.4m which was concluded on the 23rd

January 2020.

The Bank does not envisage any significant departures from its business model although it does consider the introduction
of more diversified lending product revenue streams during the current planning cycle. The Bank still expects the core of its
lending to remain property backed mortgages to SMEs including professional tandlords, who are commercial and
residential property owners.

Within this Framework the Bank operates a typical banking “Three Lines of Defence’ risk mitigation structure, as follows:

First line: Operationally, the Executive and senior management have primary responsibility for the identification,
measurement and management of all risks. Clear ownership of each specific risk management responsibility is set out in
Board approved policy documents. in addition to the day to day risk management processes of the Bank, risk is managed
through a number of executive committees. All executive and senior management and their teams, with the exception of
the Risk & Compliance team, constitute first line.

Second line: The Risk & Compliance team, led by the CRO, conduct independent reviews of risk management in the Bank
and provides chailenge to the Executive and senior management regarding the adequacy and accuracy of MI used in risk
management decision making. In addition, the team provides expertise and guidance on appropriate risk management
practices, including good practice, policies and procedures. It is the CRO’s responsibility to update relevant personnel and
committees at the Bank regarding new and amended rules, regulations or guidance, and ensure that the Bank responds
with appropriate actions. The CRO reports to the CEO and directly to the Chairman of the Audit and Compliance
Committee (ACC) and to the Chairman of the Risk Committee. The Risk & Compliance team constitutes second line.

Third line: {internal Audit is responsible for the independent review of the effectiveness of risk management at the Bank.
The Internal Audit function has been outsourced to Deloitte, who report directly to the Audit and Compliance Committee.
Deloitte conducts frequent internal audit reviews as part of an assurance map that is updated annually and that looks to set
the key areas of focus of the internal audit function for a 3-year period. Internal audit findings are reported to the Risk
Committee and to the Audit & Compliance Committee.

The Bank identifies five main risk categories:

Capital: There are a number of risks which could threaten the Bank's capital adequacy, including credit risk losses from
business loans and liquidity counterparties, operational risk losses from service interruptions, error or fraud, and
mismanagement of the business resulting in either inadequate margins or capital loss. The Bank ensures that enough
capital is held to remain solvent, even if such risks materialise. The Bank also proactively manages its planned future
capital injections to ensure future capital availability.

Credit Risk: The Bank earns a margin from taking funds from depositors, and lending to SMEs, and investing in liquid
assets. The lending and investment in liquid assets gives rise to credit risk. Poor underwriting could lead to arrears and
defaults that are in excess of forecast. The Bank, to date, has a strong credit history with only one significant loan

impairment and write off required, and a low level of arrears which are carefully monitored and resolved.

Liquidity and Funding Risk: The Bank is financed primarily with deposits from specialist markets such as businesses,

year there has been increasing evidence of depositor confidence within the FCA £85k protection limit, and more evidence

Operational Risk: The Bank faces a range of operational risks which are managed within its risk governance framework
continuously, with action undertaken as required. Key risks are identified in a risk register and graded according to a
frequency x impact methodology. The Bank looks to mitigate risks by ensuring that adequate controls and procedures are
in place and are identified and monitored as part of risk and contro! self-assessment exercises as well as by frequent
reviews by the Internal Audit function. The Bank holds adequate levels of regulatory capital to absorb any potential losses
that may arise from operational risk and reassess the level of capital holdings required to offset potential operational risk
losses as part of its ICAAP.

Business Risk: The Bank could be exposed to the failure to meet its loan origination targets, which would negatively impact
the Bank’s long-term profitability and capital position, and these origination targets are closely monitored and acted upon
through a weekly Trading Meeting attended by the CEQ, CFO and Chief Development Officer as well as Finance, Risk,
Credit and Operations staff. The Bank could also be exposed to operating at an inadequate net interest margin, which
could result from a range of potential issues, particularly in times of stress, including under-estimating the cost of liquidity,
deposit funding or incorrect pricing of credit risk. The Bank also considers the factors that might lead to an existential risk,
such as fundamental regulatory or political change.

COVID-19: A current risk that the Bank faces is the impact of the COVID-19 outbreak. This creates significant uncertainty
as to both the short and longer term impact on the Bank. As a cloud based Bank the operational change of staff working
from home and the closure of the Banks two offices has been managed well and the Bank has continued to operate
efficiently and effectively and remained open to service both current and potential new customers. The Bank has
performed both a number of new sensitivity analyses which cover a range of potential outcomes and also used the stress
test modelling utilised through the Banks current ICAAP. Regular Management Information is provided to both senior
management and the Board to enable an ongoing assessment as to the impact of the COVID-19 outbreak and to enable
suitable action to be taken, Risk mitigation steps have been undertaken with Board approved actions taken on pricing and
credit assessment as needed. Management is currently assessing the proposed funding schemes announced by both the
Bank of England including the Term Funding Scheme for SMEs (TFSME) and also the British Business Bank Coronavirus
Business Interruption Loan Scheme (CBILS). Redwood has been pleased to immediately pass on in full the recent interest
rate decreases to their lending customers and continues to ensure that the Bank treats all of its customers fairly throughout
this exceptional situation.

Economic and political environment

The UK’s decision to withdraw from the European Union could potentially have an adverse impact on the UK econamy due
to significant uncertainty and disruption. The Bank is unlikely to be directly affected by the UK leaving the EU as it operates
entirely in the UK. However, as part of its Strategic Pian, ICAAP and ILAAP, the Bank has considered several stress
scenarios that could be triggered by an event such as a disorderly Brexit and has evaluated the impact on the Bank's
capital and liquidity position. The scenarios consider the possible impact on the Bank of changes to base rate, house price
inflation, property interest rates and cost inflation.

Whilst recognising a negative impact to the Bank's performance and capital position, the Bank is considered to be
sufficiently capitalised to continue in business, even under severely stressed economic conditions. Although the full impact
of Brexit is yet to be seen, the Board is confident that the risks have been appropriately captured within the Bank’s
Strategic Plan.

On the 11th March 2020 the Bank of England reduced the base rate by 50bps to 25bps in response to the COVID-19
outbreak, in order to prevent any potential severe damage to the UK economy. Subsequent to this a further rate reduction
was announced from 25bps to 10bps on 19th March 2020. Both rate changes have been passed on in full to the Banks
customers. Management continue to review developments and their impact on the potential risks to the Bank. The Bank
has performed an impact assessment of the COVID-19 outbreak and the resultant changes in economic environment and
has approved an adjusted Strategic Plan which reflects the Bank’s best understanding of the impacts of the current
economic changes. Sensitivity analyses have been performed to understand potential ranges of outcomes to enable the
forming of the central view. However, the Board is confident that the impacts of the outbreak, whilst unwelcome, will not
impact the Bank's viability as a going concern.

Going concern

Notwithstanding a loss before tax for the year ended 31 December 2019 of £1.2m the financial statements have been
prepared on a going concern basis which the directors consider to be appropriate for the following reasons.

On 30 March 2020 the Bank raised £3.2m through the issuance of Tier 1 capital to existing investors. Additionally, the
Bank has signed an agreement to raise a further £9m by the issuance of Tier 2 capital to a new investor, and on the 15
April 2020 received an initial £3m of this committed £9m.

The directors have prepared base case and sensitised forecasts, which consider both capital requirements and liquidity, for
a period of at least 12 months from the date of approval of these financial statements. In preparing those forecasts, the
directors have considered the current economic uncertainty, in particular the effects of the Coronavirus outbreak.

The effects of the Coronavirus have been severely felt throughout the worid in recent months and the future impact on the
results of the Bank are difficult to predict. The Directors expect the uncertainty to reduce the level of new business
generated over the next year. However, the Directors do not anticipate a significant impact on the Bank’s ability to serve its
customers.

In response to the uncertainty arising from COVID-19, the Directors have considered severe but plausible downside
scenarios, including stress tests aligned to the ICAAP. These stress scenarios are considered to model stresses that are at
least as severe as the 2007/2008 financial crisis. These forecasts demonstrated that the Bank would continue operate as a
Going Concern, in what are considered to be severe but plausible downside scenarios.

The Bank is still in the growth stage and the Directors expect the Bank to require additional funding from its investors to
fulfil its business plan and achieve sustainable profitability.

Consequently, the going concer basis of accounting has been used to prepare these financial statements.

2.3 {nterest income and interest expense

interest income and expense are recognised in comprehensive income using the effective interest rate
method. The ‘effective interest rate’ is the rate that exactly discounts the estimated future cash payments
and receipts through the expected life of the financial asset or financial liability (or, where appropriate, a
shorter period) to the carrying amount of the financial asset or financial liability. When calculating the
effective interest rate, the Bank estimates future cash flows considering all contractual terms of the
financial instrument, but not future credit losses.

The caiculation of the effective interest rate includes those transaction costs and fees paid or received,
which are deemed to be an integral part of the effective interest rate. Transaction costs include
incremental costs that are directly attributable to the acquisition or issue of a financial asset or financial
liability.

Interest income and expense presented in comprehensive income include:
- interest on financial assets and financial liabilities measured at amortised cost calculated on an

effective interest basis; and
- interest on available-for-sale investment securities calculated on an effective interest rate basis.

2.6 Financial instruments

Recognition

The Bank initially recognises loans and advances, deposits, and debt securities on the date on which they
are originated. A financial asset or financial liability is measured initially at fair value plus, for an item not
at fair value through profit or loss, transaction costs that are directly attributable to its acquisition or issue.
Classification

Financial assets

The Bank classifies its financial assets into one of the following categories:

Loans and advances to customers

‘Loans and advances to customers’ are non-derivative financial assets with fixed or determinable
payments that are not quoted in an active market and that the Bank does not intend to sell immediately or

in the near term.

Loans and advances are initially measured at fair value plus incremental direct transaction costs, and
subsequently measured at their amortised cost using the effective interest method (see Note 3).

Available-for-sale

Available-for-sale investments are non-derivative investments that are designated as available-for-sale or
are not classified as another category of financial assets. Available-for-sale investments comprise
treasury bills and gilts and are measured at fair value after initial recagnition.

Interest income is recognised in profit or loss using the effective interest method (see Note 3).
impairment losses are recognised in profit and loss.

Other fair value changes, other than impairment losses, are recognised in ‘Other Comprehensive Income’
and presented in the available for sale reserve within equity. When the investment is sold, the gain or loss
accumulated in equity is reclassified to profit and loss.

Financial Liabilities

‘Trade creditors’ are initially measured at transaction price plus attributable transaction costs, and are
subsequently measured at amortised cost, using the effective interest rate method.

Measurement

Amortised cost measurement

The amortised cost of a financial asset or financial liability is the amount at which the financial asset or
financial liability is measured at initial recognition, minus principal repayments, plus or minus the
cumulative amortisation using the effective interest method of any difference between the initial amount
recognised and the maturity amount, minus any reduction for impairment.

Fair value measurement

Fair value is the amount for which an asset could be exchanged, a liability settled, or an equity instrument

granted could be exchanged, between knowledgeable, willing parties in an arm’s length transaction.

When available, the Bank measures the fair value of an instrument using the quoted price in an active
market for that instrument. A market is regarded as active if transactions for the asset or liability take
place with sufficient frequency and volume to provide pricing information on an ongoing basis.

If there is no quoted price in an active market, then the Bank uses valuation techniques that maximise the
use of relevant observable inputs and minimise the use of unobservable inputs. The chosen valuation
technique incorporates all of the factors that market participants would take into account in pricing a
transaction.

The best evidence of the fair value of a financial instrument at initial recognition is normally the
transaction price — i.e. the fair value of the consideration given or received. If the Bank determines that
the fair value at initial recognition differs from the transaction price and the fair value is evidenced neither
by a quoted price in an active market for an identical asset or liability or based on a valuation technique
that uses only data from observable markets, then the financial instrument is initially measured at fair
value, adjusted to defer the difference between the fair value at initial recognition and the transaction
price. Subsequently, that difference is recognised in profit or loss on an appropriate basis over the life of
the instrument, but no later than when the valuation is wholly supported by observable market data or the
transaction is closed out.

Identification and measurement of impairment

At each reporting date, the Bank assesses whether there is objective evidence that financial assets not
carried at fair value through profit or loss are impaired. A financial asset or a group of financial assets is
‘impaired’ when objective evidence demonstrates that a loss event has occurred after the initial
recognition of the asset(s) and that the loss event has an impact on the future cash flows of the asset(s)
that can be estimated reliably.

Objective evidence that financial assets are impaired includes:

- significant financial difficulty of the borrower or issuer;

- default or delinquency by a borrower;

~ the restructuring of a loan or advance by the Bank on terms that the Bank would not consider
otherwise;

~ indications that a borrower or issuer will enter bankruptcy;

- the disappearance of an active market for a security; or

- observable data relating to a group of assets such as adverse changes in the payment status of
borrowers or issuers in the group, or economic conditions that correlate with defaults in the group.

The Bank considers evidence of impairment for loans and advances at both a specific asset and a
collective level. All loans and advances are assessed for specific impairment. Those found not to be
specifically impaired are then collectively assessed for any impairment that has been incurred but not yet
identified. Impairment losses on assets measured at amortised cost are calculated as the difference
between the carrying amount and the present value of estimated future cash flows discounted at the
asset's original interest rate.

3. Accounting estimates and judgements

The preparation of financial statements in conformity with FRS 102 requires management, from time to
time, to make judgements and estimates that affect the application of policies and reported amounts of
assets, liabilities, income and expenses.

These estimates are based on experience and various other factors that are believed to be reasonable
under the circumstances. Actual results may differ from these estimates. The estimates and underlying
assumptions are reviewed on an ongoing basis. Revisions to accounting estimates are recognised in the
period in which the estimates are revised and in any future periods affected.

Effective interest rate

FRS 102 requires interest earned from loans and advances to be measured under the Effective Interest
Rate Method (EIRM). Management must therefore use judgement to estimate the expected life of each
instrument and hence the expected cash flows relating to it.

The key assumptions applied by management in the EIR methodology are regarding the behavioural life
of the assets and the quantum of future fees and costs directly related to the instrument. The expected
life behaviours are subjected to changes in internal and external factors and may result in adjustments to
the carrying value of loans which must be recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss and Other
Comprehensive Income,

Management has limited historical experience of the Bank’s customer behaviours due to the relative
immaturity of the portfolios and therefore models expected behaviour based on market trends and
experience. The actual behaviour of the portfolios are compared to the modelled behaviour on a quarterly
basis and the modelled behaviours are adjusted if the modelled behaviour materially deviates from actual
behaviour, with adjustments recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss and Other Comprehensive
Income.

A 10% increase or decrease in expected life of loans result in an increase in income of £23k (2018:
£5.9k) and decrease in income of £19k (2018: £4.9k) respectively. The impact of this is not considered to
be material.

or more events that occurred after the initial recognition of the asset (a ‘loss event’) and that loss event
(or events) has an impact on the estimated future cash flows of the financial asset or group of financial
assets that can be reliably estimated.

Regulators and central banks have set the goal of improving the robustness of financial benchmarks, especially

London Interbank Offered Rate ("“LIBOR’) is uncertain. In the UK, the FCA has announced that it will no longer
compel panel banks to submit rates for the calculation of LIBOR after 2021.

As a result, LIBOR may be modified or discontinued after 2021. The Working Group on Sterling Risk-Free
Reference Rates has proposed the Sterling Overnight Index Average ("SONIA") as the basis of a replacement for
LIBOR.

The Bank has therefore commenced a project to transition away from LIBOR to using a compounded SONIA rate
on the assumption that this will replace LIBOR as the market standard. Whilst not directly impacting the Bank, the
impact of this transition will be achieved through the migration of LIBOR to SONIA as required through the market
basis for LIBOR linked financial instruments.

Operational risk

Operational risk is the risk of direct or indirect loss arising from a wide variety of causes associated with the Bank's
processes, personnel, technology and infrastructure, and from external factors other than credit, market and
liquidity risks, such as those arising from legal and requlatory requirements and generally accepted standards of
corporate behaviour. Operational risk arises from all of the Bank’s operations.

The Bank's objective is to manage operational risk so as to balance the avoidance of financial losses and damage
to the Bank’s reputation with overall cost effectiveness and innovation. Bank policy requires compliance with all
applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

The Board of Directors has delegated responsibility for operational risk to its Risk Committee, which is responsible
for the development and management of controls to address operational risk.

Compliance with standards is supported by a programme of periodic reviews undertaken by Internal Audit.
The results of Internal Audit reviews are discussed in the Audit & Compliance Committee.
Capital risk management

The Bank's objective in managing its capital is to maintain appropriate levels of capital to support its business
strategy and meet regulatory requirements, including holding capital for undrawn credit lines.

The Bank's capital resources are comprised of 100% CET1 regulatory capital. The total capital ratio sits at 20.3%
(unaudited) (2018: 23.2%) and the Bank's regulatory capital requirements for CET‘, Tier 1 and total capital are
comfortably met. The Bank also holds capital for undrawn credit lines.

The Bank manages its capital under Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) and Capital Requirements Directive
(together referred to as CRDIV). The framework is enforced by the Prudential Regulatory Authority (PRA). The PRA
sets and monitors the Bank's capital requirements.

The PRA's approach to the measurement of capital adequacy is primarily based on monitoring the relationship of
the capital resources requirement to available capital resources. The regulator sets the total capital requirement
(TCR) for each bank in excess of the minimum resources requirement of 8%. A key input to the TCR setting
process is the Bank's Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP).

The Bank's policy is to maintain a strong capital base to maintain investor and market confidence and to sustain the
future development of the business.

The Bank has elected to use the standardised approach for credit risk. Under CRDIV the Bank must set aside 8%
of it's total risk weighted assets to cover its ‘Pillar 1’ capital requirements. The Bank must also set aside additional
‘Pillar 2' capital to provide for additional risks. This is calculated by multiplying the Pillar 1 capital by the TCR ratio.
The TCR ratio is based on the various risks which the Bank faces and is agreed by the PRA. The Bank's capital
base was in excess of the minimum required under the TCR.
