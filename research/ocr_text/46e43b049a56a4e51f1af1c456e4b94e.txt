The past few years have seen the Co-operative Bank take an increasingly important and refreshingly
different stance within the British banking sector, with a consequent unprecedented growth in
business in particular market areas. Although a Co-operative Banking Service has been in existence
for well over one hundred years, the past decade has witnessed the generation of a new impetus

pioneering investments at this stage are designed to reap substantial future dividends for the Bank
Group and enable us to continue to offer a service appropriate to the needs of our broadening
customer base, particularly in the evolving Co-operative Movement.

An Evolving Co-operative Movement

Throughout the past year, discussions have taken place between Government officials and represen-
tatives of the Co-operative Movement on the appropriate role for a Co-operative Development
Agency. For the spirit of Co-operation to thrive and evolve through adaptation to changing circum-
stances, there is continuing need for the pooling of ideas. Eventually, perhz.s, more formal links
involving physical resources might prove possible and lead to an even more effective Co-operative
Sector. Realistically, however, ine conclusions reached by the majority of the working group
examining this subject and published in the Government White Paper entitled “ Report of the
Working Group on a Co-operative Development Agency” were that the Agency should start from
a modest base with limited objectives and should confine Itself initially to giving advice and guidance
to and on all aspects of the Movement's activity, These views have broad support within the Move-
ment. There is a need now to get on and do something positive whilst enthusiasm is still with us.
The Prime Minister has indicated his support and a Bill has been introduced by the Government
which | hope will be widely supported and passed through the House with due expedition. From our
point of view, this moment cannot come too soon, The different types of Co-operative organisations
need a focal and reference point and the Agancy, as envisaged in the Government White Paper,
should provide this,

The Co-operative Bank Group could have an important role to play within the Agency's sphere of
operations, both as a provider of finance itself, and increasingly of finance-related advice linked to
the further development of Co-operative operations,

i have been particularly impressed during the past few years by the dedication and enthusiasm,
touched with the necessary amount of idealism, which characterises the newer aspects of the
evolving Co-operative Movement. And after almost a century and a half of existence, the main
body of the Co-operative Movement, representing largely consumer societies, can only benefit from
a resurgence of interest and activity based on fundamental Co-operative principles in a wider field
of human endeavour, The new units of activity are characterised, as in early pioneering days, by
very close personal involvement which generates a spirit of enthusiasm towards a cause far beyond
the level possible within a more traditional organisational structure. Where this considerable
motivation is reinforced by personal financial commitment of the members, whether from hard-
earned savings, redundancy pay or on a short-term loan basis, | can well see that given a market
for the product or service involved and a basis for efficient production, this could represent the bagin-~
nings of an important new addition to the total Co-operative sector of the economy and further
validate the claim that Co-operation is an efficient and acceptable third force between the private
and public sectors.

Economic Revival?

The climate of relative euphoria in some financial circles deriving from prospects for an economy
built on the benefits of North Sea Oil, contrasts dramatically with the atmosphere prevalent in
similar areas just a year ago and the still significant lack of optimism in the “veal” aconomy, The
overseas trading figures, which promised so much during the second half of last year, are once again
demonstrating their extrome sensitivity to actual and anticipated increases in consumer spending
power. The impact of North Sea Oil exports is now very considerable and there is evidence of a
tendency to allow ourselves to drift into a situation where we are using this bonus as a cushion
or support for inaction in industry in general rather than as an inspiration for renewed effort. It is
to be hoped that the White Paper on this subject recently issued by the Government will initiate a
dialogue to the point of general agreement not only on the disposition of the revenue from the oil,
but also on other policies which need to be implemented to secure a base for continued economic
expansion both during the life of the oil bonanza and thereafter.

The Unemployment Problem

Whilst not wishing to identify too closely with some of the more excitable areas in the City of
London, the Co-operative Bank has also taken a positive attitude in the past year towards likely
benefits to be derived from improvements in overall economic growth and has moved ahead its
necessary preparation for continuing progressive activities in the 4980s. In its own way, it has
given new hope to one of the areas of highest unemployment in the country — Skelmersdale ~ by
initiating the development of a Customer Service Bureau in the town, which it is hoped will create
up to one thousand new jobs over the next few years. _

Naturally, the high rate of unemployment has continued to reflect the distinct lack of growth in
productive activity, and it is disturbing to note the reported reluctance of industry to invest in the
future whether the reasons therefor be valid or not. The future economic prosperity of this country
is dependent on the creation of an atmosphere -f confidence which will be translated into new plant
and factories and utilisation of opportunities created through identified markets for existing and new
products. There is an urgent need for a more energetic and direct analysis of this problem and for
the facts to be established and placed squarely before the nation with recommendations for remedial
action which recognises the rights and aspirations of interested and sometimes conflicting groups,
but does not allow any minority interests to frustrate the greater interest of the whole.

A Small Firms Solution?

It is pleasing to note the increasing acceptance of a requirement to direct attention to these matters
across the whole spectrum of industry, to include the many thousands of vital small firms or operating
units on which we are going to depend very much to find jobs for a significant proportion of the
people displaced by the substantial new investment for which so much pressure has been generated
in recent years. An increasing amount of capital per employee invested in large scale manufacturing
will inevitably result in a more efficient use of manpower, and thus fewer jobs for the same level

of production. There will thus be a growing need to encourage innovation and new product
development, new ideas and new skills and for which purpose the individuality and enterpreneurial
spirit of the small firms and companies are particularly well suited, There is no doubt that in the
relatively small number of large organisations which dominate much of our basic industry there are
problems which flow from the fact that the individual members of the work force tend to lose their
individual identity ~and thus weir sense of loyalty, purpose and satisfaction. From my own
experience, | know well the value of people being, able to see soine positive benefits deriving from
their own ideas for improving activities in the work place.

| have mentioned earlier that a revitalised and expanded Co-operative sector has an important role
to play in the future development of this country and | suggest that further develapment in this
sector will algo help in solving the long-term unemployment problem.

Because of the basic democratic nature of a body based on Co-operative membership and principles,
the typical new” Co-operative ” is small in size and concentrates mainly on local markets, although
there could well be ways of broadening these outlets with the assistance of Government bodies or
perhaps the proposed Co-operative Development Agency. We must, however, be realistic on these
matters. Not all the owners of, or indeed the workers within, small firms currently see the attractions
of co-operatively-based enterprises, Nevertheless, they all have a role to play within the economy
and, as a Co-operative Bank, we must be sufficiently flexible to meet the needs of all aspects of
this sector of industry.

Over the past year we have welcomed the attention which the Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster,
Mr. Harold Lever, has been paying to the problems of small firms and we have been very pleased
to provide information, particularly on the working experiences and support given to this part of the
economy in various European countries by our colleagues in the major Co-operative banks in Europe.
In particular, our membership of the International Confederation of Popular Credit, bringing to-
gether banks specialising in the needs of small and medium-sized enterprises, has been a valuable
experience,

Additionally, during the year, the Bank became increasingly concerned by the low lavel of awareness
~ particularly ‘among smaller organisations ~ of the financial facilities available to corporate enter~
prises through the institutions of the European Community. Membership of the Association of
Co-operative Thrift and Credit Institutions of the E.E.C, has enabled our Bank officials, who parti~
cipate in working groups, to become aware of the largely untapped resources available for a variety
of business activities. Plans are well advanced for the Bank to take furthér initiatives with selected
customers who may well be ina position to use these available facilities.

Inflation Controlled?

Thankfully, one of the main depressive influencas on businoss optimism — cost and price inflation ~
appears to have been brought under a degree of control in the past year and it is to be hoped that
we will never again revert to the disastrous and distorting situations of just over two years ago. With
the benefits of hindsight, the total impact of price inflation at 25/30% and more can now he fully
appreciated and it will remain in the memory as a nightmare experience which must never be
allowed to return. Albeit, having established a more sensible situation in this country, we must now
ail seek to maintain it. Whether we can keep the year-on-year increase in the general level of
consumer prices under 10% is uncertain, but it is vital that we maintain inflation levels at around,
or even below, those being achieved by our major international competitors.

Already in the second half of last year, with the steady — although perhaps only temporary - strength-
ening of the pound sterling, export growth was beginning to slacken. {f, however, domestic price
inflation, inspired from whatever source, is allowed to anninish our export competitiveness further,
we will be in serious danger of dissipating al! the positive advantages which are being won by
exploitation of the wealth from under the North Sea,

While | would not subscribe fully to, nor indeed, on occasions, even claim to understand some of
the more obscure theses of the school of monetarist economists, | can well see the need to control
the overall supply of money circulating around the financial evstom in the interests of restraining
inflation. As | said in my Statement last year, the promised discipline in monetary expansiol over
the next fow years could provide the basis for a fresh start. In consequence, | am pleased to note
that the Authorities broadly kept the money supply v,ichin the limits agreed for 1977 with the Inter-
national! Monetary Fund, largely through sales of Government Stock by non-inflationary means
outside the banking system but also as a result of continuing slack demand for bank finance
acilities.

Increasing Competition

indeed, at the year end, and assessed in real terms, total advances by the London Clearers were
marginally lower than in December, 1976; in contrast, our own Bank recorded a significant real
increase in its total lending. Deposits of the Co-operative Bank were also well ahead of the total
rate of increase achieved by the other Clearers. These performances were achieved in spite of
increasing competition for retail deposits within the financial sector. The building societies have
been attracting a particularly large proportion of available savings in the past year and naturally
this has been reflected in a slackening of the flow of funds into the clearing bank sector in particutar.
We do not regard this switching of emphasis as disastrous. The important aspect from the country's
point of view is that people are still willing to save — at an increasing rate in fact — and that they
are prepared to utilise stable and reputable institutions such as the building societies. If banks wish
to have a part of those savings, then they must be prepared to offer an attractive range of services,
coupled with ready access when the customer has need of them. For our part within the Co-operative
Movement, we fee! the service we offer is meeting some of those requirements and the flow of
business to the Bank appears to confirm that view.

In the interests of fairer competition, however, we consider it necessary to correct anomalies which
favour certain institutions; primarily the building societies, which can offer interest compounded
with a concessionary rate of tax, but also the National Savings and Trustee Savings Banks, which
provide interest free of tax on funds deposited with them up to levels attractive to the small saver.
The Co-operative Bank has felt some of the impact of the outflow of funds to these other institutions
but, in particular, within the Co-operative Movement, the steady slowing down of deposits from mem-
bers of retail co-operative sucieties which is necessary to help finance the future development of the
Movement, has continued. Legislation has now been passed to make possible an increase in the
amount of deposits which Industrial and Provident Societies, including Co-operative Societies,
can take without being classified as carrying on the business of banking. This will partly assist in
remedying the situation but the main worry in this area during the past year, from the Movement's
point of view, has been related to proposed legislatior on deposit-taking institutions which, if carried
through in detail, according to the published Government White Paper on “The Licensing and
Supervision of Deposit-Taking Institutions“, would have created problems for some Retail
Co-operative Societies by requiring the maintenance of specific balance sheet ratios in order to
qualify for a deposit-taker's licence. lam pleased to say that the Government has given a sympathetic
hearing to the Movement's observations and we are now nearer to an acceptable solution. How-
ever, the advantages from tax privileges appear still to be stacked in favour of specific types of
institutions near to the public sector. In the Co-operative Movement's evidence to Sir Harold
Wilson's Committee to Review the Functioning of Financial Institutions”, it was pointed out that
” such is the importance of the Movement within the U K, economy that the subsequent employment
of (deposit) resources in trading activity or in capital investment must have considerable impact
and benefit to society as a whole“. Such is the significance of the role played by the Co-operative
Movement in assisting overall welfare that it has a reasonable claim to warrant at least parity with
the concessions granted to these other institutions.

Financial Situation

The improvement over the year in the country’s financial situation was reflected in sharp reductions
ininterest rates. The year opened with the Bank of England's minimum lending rate (MLR) standing at
141% and Bank Base Rates at one-half percentage pointlower. By October, MLR had fallen to 5% —
the lowest level for five years — although it then climbed back to 64%, probably a more realistic
level, by the year-end, Bank Base Rates also followed this pattern with a low point for the
Co-operative Bank during the year of 6% in October, reverting to 7% in December. On the basis of
past experience, we would have expected these fower borrowing costs to have had a positive
stimulus on bank borrowing. Unfortunately, we no Jonger live in a world where market forces
react according to textbook principles. Indeed, the official control on bank deposit-taking (the
Supplementary Speceal Deposit scheme or “ Corset which has a consequent impact on lending
within the banking sector as a whole, was suspended in August, having never effectively operated,
with deposits, in the main, well within the restraining bands.

Group Financial Performance

Consolidated group operating profit for the year to 14th January, 1978, shewed a significant
increase over the previous year of one-third to reach £4:Om. After sundry minor additions, rapre-
senting the share of profits in associated companies and minority interests, and deduction of tax,
retained earnings reached £1°8m —~ more than half as much again as in 1976.

Co-operative Bank Limited and Co-operative Commercial Bank Limited recorded a combined
increased operating profit of £3-0m compared with £2+6m in 1976, while F. C. Finance Limited,
the other major participant in the Group, almost doubled its profit before taxation to £1 -2m.

The Group's consolidated balance sheet total of £416m at 14th January, 1978, showed an increase
of 13°5% on the year before, broadly in line with the rate of increase related to the parent bank,
Co-operative Bank Limited, which reached a total of £335m,

Total personne! and other operating costs during the year again rose sharply but the considerable
influx of new business meant that unit costs were held at an acceptable level.

Co-operative Bank Limited

The parent bank of the Group made further substantial increases in the number of customers during
the year; the retention throughout the year of a free current account banking service for personal
customers attracted thousands of new accounts and, with the assistance of television advertising
in selected areas and a new range of brochures, posters and display equipment, existing customers
have been further encouraged to take advantage of the wide range of available services,

The number of all types of accounts held by the Bank rose by 184% compared with 16% in 1976
and 83% in 1975. The number of personal customers joining the Bank during the year rose by well
over one-fifth, and over the past four years the total has increased by almost one-half,

This new business has consolidated the Bank's position as a leading institution in certain banking
markets, and notably in the Personal Sector. While steady growth has been achieved at almost all
the established branches of the Bank, there has been an outstanding performance at some of the
new branches, During the past year, new regionally-based branches were opened at Chatham,
Southend, Plymouth and Sunderland, all of which helped to fill in some of the more obvious gaps
in the national picture, By contrast, our Northampton Branch recorded its “ Silver Jubilee” with
an appropriate celebration attended by many local dignitaries.

There still remains a small number of population centres which do not have appropriate proximity
toa regional branch of the Bank although, of course, they are invariably well serviced for most
banking needs by in-store agency services operated by Retail Co-operative Societies on our behalf.
Over the next few years, we intend to deal with this situation and also to start to introduce the type
of distribution outlet appropriate to the needs of the 1980s and 1990s.

The past few months have seen considerable and growing attention focused on the problems
associated with the existence of intensive branch networks. In avoiding this problem ourselves, we
have been fortunate in having the full support of our colleagues within the Retail Co-operative
Societies. The in-store agency banking service they offer was revitalised on a regional basis during
1977 and now comprises “ Handybanks "of which there are 700, providing access to all the main
Bank services during shopping hours, and 3,800 “ Cash-a-Cheque “ points situated at check-out
and cther sales points in a wide variety of stores. Rapid expansion invariably places a considerable
strain on existing methods of operation and to anticipate this effect, the Bank announced in November
its plans to set up a central Customer Service Bureau at Skelmersdale New Town in central Lancashire.
To he established within a 100,000 sq. ft. purpose-built development, the Bureau will eventually
service all the Bank’s Branches through centralisation of the traditional “’ back office “ activities.
Work has also begun in Corporation Street, Manchester, on the construction of a new Head Office
building for the Bank. The new building is due for completion during late 1979 and will incorporate
more extensive facilities for the main Manchester Branch of the Bank, as well as accommodation
for Head Office Departments.

Throughout the past year, the Bank continued to apply Co-operative principles by providing a
service at the lowest possible price consistent with convenience to the consumer. In circumstances
in which tariffs generally have been raised and there are indications that increasing costs could
cause them to be raised even further, the Co-operative Bank has decided to keep its free service
throughout 1978 for those current accounts maintained in credit. The Bank has also taken advantage
of generally lower rates of interest and has reduced its Personal Loan interest rate, thus maintaining
it at the lowest available on the market,

The re-styled and commission-free Budget Account service, with its borrowing facilities stretching
to 24 times the monthly transfer on payment into the account, has been extremely popular with
householders who wish to spread the impact of large fuel or rate bills, while usage of the general
investment and insurance advisory service through the Bank's ‘’ Moneymaster facility has
increased sharply, In consequence, increasing numbers of the Bank's customers are coming to
realise the benefits which can be secured from a complete " package ” of financial services to meet
their requirements at a very positive price advantage,

The continuing strong rate of expansion in the Personal Sector markets has not, of course, been at
the expense of developing new business elsewhere. In particular, the Bank has been very conscious
of its increasing role in meeting the needs of the evolving Co-operative Movement. The main
enmhasis of activity has in fact been on servicing the requirements of Wholesale and Retail
Co-operative Societies but there has also been an increasing awareness of financial require-
ments elsewhere in the Movement,

Increasing support for other corporate sectors was provided during the year in a variety of deposit-
taking, lending and advisory functions, Of particular note is the fact that fencing to Clubs more than
doubled, The Bank also participated in a number of Local Authority supported seminars and exhi-
bitions designed to assist small businesses. The Bank's mobile service points were again a prominent
feature of the support services at the Labour Party Conference and various Trade Union conferences,
and at which the efficient and helpful service provided by Bank officials was much apprectated and
attracted much favourable comment. For the first time, the Bank had a stand at the East of England
Agricultural Show held in Peterborough to celebrate the centenary of the Borough.

F, C, Finance Limited

Despite the abnormally high cost of money which prevailed throughout most of the first half of the
year, F, C, Finance Limited improved its overall performance and its pre-tax profits for the year,
Continued development and growth in the traditional business of the group — personal and industrial
credit and leasing advances were more than one-half higher than in 1976 ~ was assisted by the
overall reduction in rates during the second half of the year. The company has reinforced its links
with the Co-operative Movement during the year and currently has business on the books in various
categories and types of finance, with most of the major Retail Co-operative Societies. Significant
progress continues to be made in the company’s handling of its involvement in property and con-
struction. Declining interest rates in 1977 and the consequent restoration of some confidence in the
property sector as a whole were major factors in an enhanced selling programme,

a final dividend of 4% is to be paid.

1. Bases of Consolidation and Accounting Date

(a) The accounts of the group which are made up for the 53 weeks to 14th January, 1978, incorporate the
accounts of F. C, Finance Limited and its subsidiaries which are made up for the year ended 3ist December,
1977. Since the group accounting date is virtually coterminous with the calendar year 1977 the financial
year’s figures are headed 1977 and the corresponding figures for the previous year are headed 1976. These
comparative figures are only for a 52-week year,

(b) Investments

(i) Income from investments includes amortization of premiums and discounts on dated stocks, credit for
wiich is taken in equal annual instalments frora the date of acquisition to maturity. The premiums and discounts
en assentments acquired from CWS are based on the market value of these investments at 70th July, 1971, at
which date the banking activities of CWS were transferred to and vested iri Co-operative Bank Limited,

(ii) Realised profits and losses on sales of investments are taken to investment suspense account, and are
subsequently transferred to profit and loss account in five equal annual instalments, commencing with the
year of realisation.

(c) Losses on advances

The amount charged against operating profit for Insses on advances in Co-operative Bank Limited is based on
the average experience of the current and four preceding years and the present volume of lending. If at
any balance sheet date the provision is not adequate to meet anticipated losses on advances, or is axcessive
to a material extent, the appropriate shortfall or excess is charged or credited to profit and loss account. Inthe
subsidiary companies anticipated losses on advances are charged against profits in the year in which they arise.

(d) Depreciation

Depreciation of land is provided in Co-operative Bank Limited at 14% and of buildings at 24% of the original
purchase price, Depreciation of freehold land and buildings in the other group companies is not provided for,
Depreciation of equipment and fittings is provided in equal annul instalments over the estimated working
lives of the assets. The cost and aggregate depreciation on those assets transferred from CWS at 10th July,
1971, are those relating to the original acquisition of the assets by CWS.

(e) Hire purchase and instalment credit agreements
Credit is taken in the profit and loss account for finance charges on hire purchase and instalment credit

agreements, net of dealers’ commission, using the “ rule of 78,” with the exception of agreements having a
duration of five years and over where finance charges are allocated on an actuarial basis.

(f) Deferred Taxation

Provision is made at the current rate of corporation tax for taxation deferred by reason of timing differences in
the accounting and taxation treatment of certain items. Provision is also made for potential taxation on surpluses
arising from revaluation of properties, but not on realised profits which have been “rolled over” for tax
purposes,

44
(g) Development land and work in progress

Development land and work in progress is valued at the lower of cost, including normal finance charges, and
net value realisable in the ordinary course of business. Receipts in respect of part disposals are deducted from
cost. Credit is taken for 80% of the estimated profit included in receipts on developments which are substan-
tially complete.

(h) Extraordinary items
All items which are not within the normal activities of the Rank and its subsidiaries are dealt with in the profit and
loss account as extraordinary items, These include profits and lossas on the disposal of the companies’ premises,

() Associated companies

In the consolidated balance sheet associated companies are shown at cost, including advances, less amounts
written off, and the group's share of the retained profits and losses from the date of acquisition.

(j} Exchange rates
Balances in foreign currencies are expressed in sterling at the rate ruling at the balance sheet date,

(k) Premium on acquisition of shares in subsidiaries

The premium paid on the acquisition of shares in suvsidiaries, baing the excess of the amount paid over the
net tangible assets at the date of acquisition, is shown in the consolidated balance sheet at the full amount
as it is considered that the value of these subsidiaries to the group is not less than the original cost.
