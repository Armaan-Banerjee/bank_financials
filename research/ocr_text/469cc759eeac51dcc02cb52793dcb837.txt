Chairman’s Statement
For the year ended 31 March 2020

The financial year ending March 31 2020, like the preceding years continued to be very
challenging for the Bank. There were many difficulties for the business which the Board and
Management could not influence or control due to their political nature. The political pressures
on [ran caused by the decision of the United States of America’s administration to not to abide
by the JCPOA agreement, and lack of clearing facilities in London continue to be a severe
restriction. Much of our existing business is, naturally, derived from and based in Iran, both
from customers based in Iran and customers trading with Iran, and this will remain the case for
the foreseeable future.

The outbreak of Covid-19 pandemic also affected the operation of the bank as well as other
parts of the financial services and broader economy both in the UK and Iran. The bank has not
been in a position to take advantage of the UK government’s various support schemes, because
of its lack of access to UK clearing. This however, has not impacted the bank’s financial
position significantly because of the pre-existing restrictions referred to above. Therefore, the
main tasks facing the board and management in response to the pandemic have been to rapidly
introduce home working and provide colleagues with the tools and technology for working
from home. These were achieved in good time and the indications are that they have been
successfully achieved.

I said last year that the Board is also mindful of the impact of Brexit on its ability to hold its
High Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) with the German Central Bank and management are
planning for viable alternatives. | am delighted to report that the bank has now obtained
regulatory clearance and established a Eurozone branch in Milan, Italy, in record time despite
the prevailing adverse conditions. This is a great testament to the dedication and hard work and
ingenuity of management and staff in London and our colleagues in Italy. This development
will ensure that our access to Target 2 clearing as well as the ability to hold Euro denominated
(HQLA) after Brexit is secured. Italy’s level of business with Iran is also expected to grow and
this bodes well for the future, when normality should return in the medium term.

The Bank’s liquidity and capital base remain strong to support the existing scale of operations
for the foreseeable future. Although, the Board expect the general climate in which the Bank
operates to remain extremely difficult for some time and the outlook for a swift return to
resumption of normal business unclear in the short term.

The Board continues its focus on ensuring that the infrastructure and operation of the Bank is
kept up-to-date and capable of dealing with the resumption of business, and that its regulatory
obligations are met on an ongoing basis.

I wish to thank my Board colleagues and the staff of the Bank for their continuing hard work
and dedication. Likewise, I am sure that I speak for the whole of the Board in expressing our
appreciation and gratitude for the continued support of our two Shareholder Banks.

Strategic Report
For the year ended 31 March 2020

The Directors are pleased to present their strategic report for the Persia International Bank PLC
for the year ended 31 March 2020.

Introduction

Persia International Bank PLC (“PIB” or “the Bank”) is an authorised institution under the
Financial Services and Markets Act 2000. The Bank’s principal activity is the provision of
commercial banking services to its customers. Although financial sanctions imposed in 2010
were lifted on 16 January 2016. The USA’s decision to unilaterally withdraw from the Joint
Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) and subsequent re-imposition of OFAC sanctions
means the normal banking activities have not yet been fully resumed owing to continuing
difficulties in arranging UK clearing facilities and in establishing correspondent relationships
with international banks. In June 2016, the Bank joined the Target 2 payments and clearing
system through the Deutsche Bundesbank.

Business Review

Historically, the Bank has offered a comprehensive service in trade finance and credit facilities
to its customers. Under trade finance, the Bank used to receive and advise letters of credit, add
confirmation and provide short term credit facilities by discounting the bills drawn under them.
PIB also extended credit facilities to its corporate and institutional customers either on bilateral
terms or as part of syndication. Iranian corporate customers constituted a major portion of the
Bank’s customer base. A strong customer base was built up with a robust trade finance and
corporate credit portfolio. The Bank had been performing well since its inception, meeting all
its short and medium term goals until 2010 when financial sanctions were imposed.

The Banks in the process of establishing its branch in Milan, Italy as it has received permission
from the PRA and the central bank of Italy, La Banca d'Italia (Bd'l). This will enable the Bank
to expand its trade finance and credit facilities. The Bank will also access Target 2 to process
trade payments and receipts and will hold high quality liquid asset (HQLA). Dubai branch
which was opened in August 2005 but had its operations suspended by the Dubai regulator
(DFSA) in March 2010 as a result of the sanctions. Since then the branch was inactive and
currently in the process of closure.

The Statement of Financial Position total assets of the Bank have increased mainly due
additional placement by shareholder banks. Before the sanctions Trade Finance had been an
important portfolio in PIB’s business lines. In recent years the Bank did not have this
opportunity and saw a downward trend in its trade finance activities. Further resumption of
trade finance activities is possible only when the issues surrounding correspondent banking
relationships are resolved. Although there was no trade finance business at the end of March
2020 the Bank will however consider all viable options in the future years.

The outlook for the year 2020-21 will, however be challenging, in the midst of continued
economic and political uncertainties, ongoing regulatory changes and Covid-19 pandemic:
Looking ahead the Bank sees significant opportunities and challenges in its key market. The

Financial Performance during the year 2019-20 and key performance indicators

The Bank’s financial performance for the year is better than the expectations set out at the
beginning of the year. The interest income for the year ending 31 March 2020 increased by
17% to €2,406k (FY2019: €2,055k). Fee & commission income received was €1,565k
(FY2019: €122k). The latter was mainly due to fee received on arranging syndication facilities.
The fee & commission payable was €15k (FY2019: €216k) and other income was €3k
(FY2019: €150k).

The total administrative expenses for the financial year were €5,087k (FY2019: €7,131k). The
Bank has been closely monitoring and controlling its expenses in order to achieve efficiencies.
Depreciation cost was €388k (FY2018: €462k). Costs in all categories are lower than previous
year and the Bank is continuously looking for efficiencies. The Bank has reported a Loss of
€1,884k (FY2019: €6,025k) before IFRS9 expected credit loss (ECL) provision. The loss for
the year after IFRS9 expected credit loss provision is €6,418k. The Bank's income is presently
derived mainly from interest earned on money-market placements, new loan assets as well as
loans arranged prior to the sanctions. There is a significant fee income from PIB lead
syndication as well as some from limited trade finance activities and payment services.

The total assets of the Bank as at 31 March 2020 were €219,994k (FY2019: €166,729k). The
biggest asset class is loans and advances to the banks including money market placements
€70,065k (FY2019: €34,000k). Money Market placements are kept for varying periods of
maturity. The management envisage replacing money market lending with corporate loans
‘when the opportunity arises for new lending facilities.

The capital position of the Bank remains strong with CET] ratio at a healthy level. The
collateral secured against loans continue to be above 150% on average. Both these provide
adequate head room for further growth and expansion during FY2020-21.

The Board has defined responsibilities for the overall direction, supervision and control of the
Bank. This includes assessment of the Bank’s competitive position, approval of strategic and
financial plans taking into consideration the likely consequences of any decisions in the long
run and review of the performance and financial status. It reviews and approves significant
changes in the Bank’s structure and organisation, establishes the risk framework and the overall

credit, large exposures, impairment, liquidity and operational risk. The Board also approves
and monitors the Bank’s policies, procedures and processes in connection with the fight against
financial crime. The Board and the management believe that they have taken adequate
measures to mitigate the primary risks. Steps have been initiated to diversify as far as possible
GBP payment arrangements and advance payments have been made to secure essential services
and supplies until participation in the local GBP clearing system is established. The Bank's
overall liquidity position is strong with major liabilities settled and the remaining liabilities
manageable. The mismatch in the maturities of the Statement of Financial Position assets and
liabilities is not a cause for concern as a significant portion of its short-term funds are kept with
Deutsche Bundesbank and Bank Markazi (the Central Bank of Iran) and some with other banks
in Iran. The Bank has access to sufficient liquid funds within the EU to meet its obligations
and operating costs for the foreseeable future, and as a consequence the Directors are satisfied
that the Bank will remain a going concern and remain confident regarding its longer-term
prospects.

organisation, These activities include guidelines and instructions, approvals, authorisations,
verifications, reconciliations, analytical reviews, and segregation of duties. The Board has also
established a Risk Committee chaired by an Independent Non-executive Director. This
committee monitors, reviews and updates all risks faced by the Bank internally and externally.
In terms of internal controls and financial reporting, external and internal information is
obtained systematically and the Bank's management is provided with relevant information on
the Bank’s activities. Timely, current and accessible information relevant for financial
reporting purposes is provided to the appropriate functionaries, such as the Board, the senior
management group and the relevant Board committees.

The Board has overall responsibility for proper arrangement of internal control and risk
management over financial reporting. The Board has appointed the Audit Committee (members
are two Independent Non-executive Directors) to undertake more specific tasks in relation to
the financial reporting process, controls, and monitoring the efficiency of the Bank's internal
control and risk management. The Audit Committee also reviews regularly the main features
of the internal control and risk management systems pertaining to the financial reporting
process.

Brexit

There is still significant uncertainty around the outcome of the UK’s negotiation with the EU.
The impact of a “no-deal” could pose a number of unknown consequences and the situation is
being closely monitored. The management is however confident that through its Milan branch
the Bank will have access to Target 2 and HQLA. Maintenance of HQLA is essential to
conform to the requirements of Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR). At present part of the funds
with Deutsche Bundesbank under Target 2 are earmarked as HQLA. PIB management
envisages that Milan branch will enable the Bank to potentially obtain other business from
southern Europe.

Funds denominated in euro to corporate customers in Iran can be made available through one
of the Shareholder Banks and Bank Markazi. Money Market placements with banks in Iran can
also be intermediated by Shareholder Banks and Bank Markazi. Similarly, repayments from
corporate customers and banks in Iran can be through intermediation of Shareholder Banks and
Bank Markazi.

Risk weighting of assets

The EU notification on 12 December 2014 on the equivalence of the supervisory and regulatory
requirements of non-EU countries has excluded Iran from the list of those countries, and
therefore exposure to Iran will continue to be risk weighted at 150%. Given that our business
activities are mainly focused on Iranian corporate and financial institutions, these risk
weighting measures will impact the business level that the Bank can achieve and therefore its
profitability.

2.4 Financial instruments — initial recognition and subsequent measurement

Financial assets and financial liabilities are recognised in the Bank’s Statement of Financial
Position when the Bank becomes a party to the contractual provisions of the instrument.
Recognised financial assets and financial liabilities are initially measured at fair value.
Transaction costs that are directly attributable to the acquisition or issue of financial assets and
financial liabilities ( other than financial assets and financial liabilities at FVTPL) are added to
or deducted from the fair value of the financial assets or financial liabilities, as appropriate, on
initial recognition.

Transaction costs directly attributable to the acquisition of financial assets or financial
liabilities at FVTPL are recognised immediately in profit or loss. If the transaction price differ
from fair value at initial recognition, the Bank will account for such difference as follows:

- if fair value is evidenced by a quote price in an active market for an incidental asset or
liability or based on a valuation technique that uses only data from observable markets,
then the difference is recognised in profit and loss on initial recognition (i.e. day 1 in
profit and loss);

- In all other cases, the fair value will be adjusted to bring in line with the transaction
price (i.e. day 1 profit or loss will be deferred by including it in the initial carrying
amount of the asset or liability).

After initial recognition, the deferred gain or loss will be released to profit or loss on a rational
basis, only to the extent that it arises from a change in a factor (including time) the market
participants would take into account when pricing the asset or liability.

Transaction costs directly attributable to the acquisition of financial assets classified as at

presented on the statement of financial position represent all amounts receivable including

2.6 Financial assets at amortised costs or at FVTOCI

The Bank assess the classification and measurement of a financial asset based on the
contractual cash flow characteristics of the asset and the Bank’s business model for managing
the asset. For an asset to be classified and measured at amortised costs or at FVTOCI, its
contractual terms should give rise to cash flows that are SPPI.

For the purpose of SPPI test, principal is the fair value of the financial asset at initial
recognition. That principal amount may change over the life of the financial asset (e.g. if there
are repayments of the principal). Interest consist of consideration for the time value of money,
for the credit risk associated with the principal amount outstanding during a particular period
of time and for other basic lending risks and costs as well as a profit margin. The SPPI
assessment is made in the currency in which the financial asset is denominated.

Contractual cash flows that are SPPI are consistent with a basic lending arrangement.
Contractual terms that exposure to risk or volatility in the contractual cash flows that are
unrelated to a basic lending arrangement, such as exposure to change in equity price or
commodity price, do not give rise to a contractual cash flow that are SPPI. An originated or an
acquired financial asset can be a basic lending arrangement irrespective of whether it is a loan
in its legal form.

The Bank determine the business model at a level that reflect how groups of financial assets
are managed together to achieve a particular business objective. The Bank’s business model
depends on management’s intentions for an individual instrument.

The Bank has more than one business model for managing its financial instruments which
reflect how the Bank manages its financial assets in order to generate cash flows. The Bank’s
business models determine whether cash flows will result from collecting contractual cash

2.10 Impairment

The Bank recognises loss allowances for ECLs on the following financial instruments that are
not measured at FVTPL:

- loans and advances to banks;

- loans and advances to customers;

- debt instrument securities;

- lease receivable;

- loan commitments issued; and

- financial guarantee contracts issued.

No impairment loss is recognised on equity instruments.
ECLs are required to be measured through a loss allowance at an amount equal to:

- 12-month ECL, i.e. lifetime ECL that result from those default events on the financial
instrument that are possible within 12 months after the reporting date, (referred to as
Stage 1); or

- Full lifetime ECL, i.e. lifetime ECL that result from all possible default events over the
lifetime of the financial instrument, (referred as to Stage 2 and Stage 3).

A loss allowance for full lifetime ECL is required for a financial instrument if the credit risk
on that financial instrument has increased significantly since initial recognition. For all other
financial instruments, ECLs are measured at an amount equal to the 12-month ECL.

ECLs are probability-weighted estimate of the present value of credit losses. These are
measured as the present value of the difference between the cash flows due to the Bank under
the contract and the cash flows that the Bank expect to receive’ arising from the weighting of
multiple future economic scenarios, discounted at the asset’s EIR.

- For undrawn loan commitments, the ECL is the difference between the present value
of the difference between the contractual cash flows that are due to the Bank if the
holder is of the commitment draws down the loan and the cash flows that the Bank
expect to receive if the loan is drawn down; and

- For financial guarantee contract, the ECL is the difference between the expected
payments to reimburse the holder of the guaranteed debt instrument less any amount
that the Bank expect to receive from the holder, the debtor or any other party.

The Bank measure ECL on an individual basis. The measurement of the loss allowance is based
on the present value of the asset’s expected cash flows using the asset’s original EIR, regardless
of whether it is measured on an individual basis.

2.10.1 Credit-impaired financial assets

A financial asset is ‘credit-impaired’ when one or more events that have a detrimental impact
on the estimated future cash flows of the financial asset have occurred. Credit-impaired
financial assets are referred to as Stage 3 assets. Evidence of credit-impairment includes
observable data about the following events:

- significant financial difficulty or the borrower or issuer;

- A breach of contract such as a default or past due event;

- The lender of the borrower, for economic or contractual reasons relating to the
borrower’s financial difficulty, having granted to the borrower a concession that the
lender would not otherwise consider

- The disappearance of an active market for a security because of financial difficulties;
or

- The purchase of a financial asset at a deep discount that reflects the incurred credit
losses.

It may not be possible to identify a single discrete event — instead, the combined effect of
several events may have caused financial asset to become credit-impaired. The Bank assess
whether debt instruments that are financial assets measured at amortised cost or FVTOCI are
credit-impaired at each reporting date. To assess if sovereign or corporate debt instruments are
credit impaired, the bank consider factors such as bond yields, credit ratings and the ability of
the borrower to raise funding.

A loan is considered credit-impaired when a concession is granted to the borrower due to a
deterioration in the borrower’s financial condition, unless there is evidence that as a result of
granting the concession the risk is not receiving the contractual cash flows has reduced
significantly and there are no other indicators of the impairment. For financial assets where
concessions are contemplated but not granted the asset is deemed credit impaired when there
is observable evidence of credit-impairment including meeting the definition of default.

2.10.2 Definition of default

Critical to the determination of ECL is the definition of default. The definition of default is
used in measuring the amount of ECL and in the determination of whether the loss allowance
is based in 12-month or lifetime ECL, as default is a component of the probability of default
(PD) which affects both the measurement of ECLs and the identification of a significant
increase in credit risk.

The bank considers an event of default to be when a borrower is unlikely to pay its credit
obligations to the bank in full.

This definition of default is used by the Bank for accounting purposes as well as for internal
credit risk management purposes. The definition of default is appropriately tailored to reflect
different characteristics of different type of assets.

2.10.3 Significant increase in credit risk

The bank monitors all financial assets, issued loan commitments and financial guarantee
contracts that are subject to the impairment requirements to assess whether there has been a
significant increase in credit risk since initial recognition. If there has been a significant
increase in credit risk the Bank will measure the loss allowance based on lifetime rather than
12-months ECL.

The Bank’s accounting policy is not to use the practical expedient that financial assets with
‘low’ credit risk at the reporting date are deemed not to have had a significant increase in credit
risk. As a result the bank monitors all financial assets, issued loan commitments and financial
guarantee contracts that are subject to impairment for significant increase in credit risk.

In assessing whether the credit risk on a financial instrument has increased significantly since
initial recognition, the bank compares the risk of default occurring on the financial instrument
on the reporting date based on the remaining maturity of the instrument with the risk of a default
occurring that was anticipated for the remaining maturity at the current reporting date when the
financial instrument was first recognised. In making this assessment, the Bank consider both
qualitative and quantitative information that is reasonable and supportable, including historical
experience and forward-looking information that is available without undue cost or effort,
based on the Bank’s historical experience and expert credit assessment including forward-
looking information.

For corporate lending forward-looking information includes the future prospects of the
industries in which the Bank’s counterparties operate, obtained from expert economic expert
reports, financial analysts, governmental bodies, relevant think-tanks and other similar
organisations, as well as consideration of various internal and external sources of actual and
forecast economic information.

The Bank allocates its counterparties to a relevant internal credit risk grade depending on their
credit quality. The quantitative information is a primary indicator of the significant increase in
credit risk and is based on the change in lifetime PD.

The PDs used are forward-looking and the Bank uses the same methodologies and the data

2.11 Modification and derecognition of financial assets

A modification of a financial asset occurs when the contractual terms governing the cash flows
of a financial asset are renegotiated or otherwise modified between initial recognition and

of the financial asset. A modification affects the amount and/or timing of the contractual cash
flows either immediately or at a future date. In addition, the introduction or adjustment of
existing covenants of an existing loan would constitute a modification even if these new or
adjusted covenants do not yet affect the cash flows immediately but affect the cash flows
depending on whether the covenant is or is not met (e.g. a change to the increase in the interest
rate that arises when covenant is breached).

The Bank negotiate loans to customers in financial difficulty to maximise collection and to
minimise the risk of default. The revised terms in most of the cases include an extension of the
maturity of the loan, changes to the timing of cash flows of the loan (principal and interest
repayment), reduction in the amount of cash flows due (principal and interest forgiveness) and
amendments to covenants.

When a financial asset is modified the bank assesses whether this modification results in
derecognition. A modification results in derecognition when it gives rise to substantially
different terms. To determine if the modified terms are substantially different from the original
contractual terms the Bank considers the followings:

- Qualitative factors, such as contractual cash flows after modification are no longer
SPPI, change in currency or when rights to cash flows between the original
counterparties expire because a new debtor replace the original debtor (unless both
debtors are under common control), the extent of change in interest rates, and maturity.
If these do not clearly indicate a substantial modification, then;

- A quantitative assessment is performed to compare the present value of remaining
contractual cash flows under the original term with the contractual cash flows under the
revised terms, both amounts discounted at the original effective interest.

If the difference in present value is greater than 10% the Bank deems the arrangement is
substantially different leading to derecognition. When performing a quantitative assessment of
a modification or renegotiation of a credit-impaired financial asset that was subject to a write-
off, the bank consider the expected (rather than the contractual) cash flows before modification

28. Risk Management

The following note on Risk Management states the Bank's policy in normal circumstances.

In transacting its normal business the Bank is exposed to the following principal risks: liquidity,
credit, operational and market risk

It is the responsibility of the Risk Committee ("RC"), a Board Committee, to review and to
monitor the Bank's risk appetite and profile, risk management policies and procedures, and its
risk management in practice, and to make recommendations to the Board on these matters. The
RC comprises 5 members, an Independent Non-executive Director as Chairman, the CEO,
CFO, COO and one other Independent Non-executive Director and the Chief Risk Officer
(CRO). The RC meets at least four times a year, and from time to time as circumstances may
require.

Overall responsibility for monitoring and managing the Statement of Financial Position risk is
delegated by the Board to the Asset and Liability Committee ("ALCO"). It concerns itself with
the structure of the Statement of Financial Position, in particular its capital adequacy, liquidity,
funding requirements, and tax capacity and planning, and also with credit, operational and
market risks. The Committee is responsible for ensuring that the Bank does not exceed its
agreed risk appetite, and to that end it receives regular reports of the Bank's exposures.

Membership of the ALCO comprises the CEO as Chairman, the CFO, COO, one Independent
Non-executive Director, the Financial Controller, Operations Manager, Treasury Manager, the
Chief Risk Officer and Credit Manager as Secretary. ALCO is required to meet once a month
and is chaired by the CEO. It can meet more regularly if required, e.g. to consider the impact
of significant changes in market situations. The monthly meeting follows a fixed agenda but it
also reviews additional material as necessary, e.g. qualitative risks such as reputation or
political risk. The agenda includes reports on the four prime risks above, the bank’s monthly
management accounts and comparison to budget, the regulatory (PRA) requirements and new
business, if any. The directors are required to review the ALCO and Risk Committee minutes
at the quarterly Board meetings.

28.1 Liquidity risk

Being adequately liquid at all times is one of the most significant risks for the Bank. However,
due to continuing impediments in establishing correspondent banking relationships and
participating in clearing arrangements, the Bank is unable to function normally and expand its
business activities. This has resulted in the Bank holding net liquid assets of €70.1m in cash
and central bank balances including balances with Bundesbank and also about €5.6m in nostro
balances with banks primarily in the euro zone, posing no significant liquidity risk in the near
future. Liquidity risk, in normal circumstances occurs when the bank is unable to meet its
financial obligations in a timely manner as and when they fall due.

Liquidity is monitored on a maturity mismatch basis and various stress scenarios are normally
tested regularly, The Bank is currently holding €52.7m as part of its High Quality Liquid Assets
with Bundesbank to manage the liquidity risk. The Bank has no access to the wholesale money
markets and no bank will intermediate money transfers.

Management of liquidity risk

The Bank’s liquidity is managed by its treasury function. The Treasurer must ensure that the
Bank can meet its obligations to its customers and also fully comply with the PRA's
requirements. The CFO (executive director) who has full knowledge of potential new assets
and can influence their timing or negotiate additional shareholder funding accordingly. The
asset and liability maturity profile is set in Note 29

New business / assets will not be taken on unless the bank is confident of its ability to fund
them.

28.2 Credit risk

Credit risk arises where the possibility exists of a counterparty defaulting on its obligations.
Credit risk exposures are required to be controlled through close monitoring of positions and
credit ratings. Day-to-day management of credit risk is carried out by the credit department.
The credit committee meets as and when required. It considers all new credit applications. It
can approve those up to € 4,000k and recommends those above € 4,000k for Board approval.
The credit exposure reports and credit committee minutes are discussed at the ALCO meetings.
The Bank normally takes legally enforceable security for facilities which it grants; this may be
security over customer's assets, or in some instances cash and in yet others, securities listed on
the Tehran Stock Exchange, which are charged to the Bank either by the borrower itself or by

a third party.

28.5 Market risk

Non-trading market risk

The Bank does not trade either interest rate or exchange rates. Interest rate and currency risk
arise in the Bank's banking book through holding of financial instruments, including loans and
deposits. Exposure to movements in interest rates arises from mismatches between interest
rate sensitive assets and liabilities. The risk is monitored and managed on a daily basis by the
Bank's Treasury function. The Bank similarly does not take (trading) open positions in foreign
currencies and so is exposed to a minimal foreign exchange risk.

Interest rate risk

The Bank does not have interest rate gaps in excess of one year. Non-interest bearing assets
and liabilities are included with capital funds which are also undated. The potential effect of a
1% adverse movement in interest rates across all currencies and all dates at 31 March 2020
would increase this year's loss by €108k (2019 €132k). This calculation is determined by

Currency risk

The exposure to currency risk is measured and monitored daily. The small remaining open
positions resulting from banking operating transactions are closed out in the market as it is
practical to do so. See note 30 for the position analysis and geographical concentrations.

Derivative instruments / hedging

The Bank does not hold derivative instruments for trading purposes. Exposures to interest rate
risk, e.g. fixed rate security or loan, are limited through the use of hedging instruments; an
interest rate swap may be used to convert the interest flows from a fixed rate asset to floating
rates so matching the associated funding. However, the Bank has not adopted hedge
accounting.

33. Immediate and ultimate parent company

The immediate and ultimate parent undertaking is Bank Mellat, which owns 60% of the share
capital of the Bank, a company incorporated in the Islamic Republic of Iran. Bank Mellat is the
parent of the smallest and largest groups into which the results of the Bank are consolidated.
Copies of the Group financial statements of Bank Mellat are available from its principal place
of business at No: 276, Taleghani Avenue, 15817 Tehran, Iran. The Bank's other major
shareholder is Bank Tejarat which owns 40% of the share capital of the Bank.

34. Related parties

Bank Mellat owns 60% and Bank Tejarat owns the remaining 40% of Persia International Bank
PLC share capital. Bank Mellat also owns 26.30% in EIH Bank. A significant amount of
transactions are entered into with related parties in the normal course of the Bank's business.
However, due to fact that the Bank has been under sanction and difficulties in correspondent
banking there was no new business during the period. No interest was paid on Bank Mellat's
current and call accounts. During normal operations, the Bank receives from its Shareholder
Banks overnight placements and deposits with maturity terms of up to one year. When the
Bank is fully in operation, a large number of LCs discounted by the Bank are usually issued
originally by the Bank’s shareholders. During the financial year, Bank Mellat, the owner of the
London Head Office, decided to waive the rental fee on the building starting from June 2019
until further notice. The rental value of the premises used before this was at the rate of £20 SFT
per annum. This translated to £500k per annum.

35. Post balance sheet events

In financial year 2021 the Bank will reclassify its property held in Dubai to an investment
property. From that date the future intention of the property is to rent it out to external parties
and no longer using it for it its own trading operations. This was originally planned to take
place in May 2019, however due the Dubai property still having a valid banking trading license
it was decided to keep it in operation for this financial year. The directors have assessed the
fair value of the property to be €4,343k as at 31 March 2020. The property will be revalued
and held at fair value in 2020/21 financial statements. The directors will again revalue the
property as at 31 March 2021.
