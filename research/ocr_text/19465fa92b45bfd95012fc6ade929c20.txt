Strategic Report

Who we are

Our bank

HSBC Innovation Bank Limited (‘HINV’,
formerly Silicon Valley Bank UK Limited,
‘SVBUK’), the financial partner to the
innovation economy, helps entrepreneurs,
investors and the world’s most innovative
companies achieve their ambitious goals. Our
mission is to empower technology and life
science/healthcare businesses at every stage
with the expertise, energy, and connections
they need to share their vision with the world.

We offer flexible banking solutions for start-
ups, scale-ups, growth businesses and
investors. We offer a range of banking and
financing solutions, including traditional term
loans, asset-based loans, revolving lines of
credit, warehouse facilities, recurring revenue
facilities, mezzanine lending, acquisition
finance facilities, corporate working capital
facilities and credit card programs. These
loans may be unsecured or secured by clients’
assets or future cash flows.

Our client treasury management products and
services include a wide range of deposit
services including current and term deposit
accounts, payments and cash management
solutions.

Our Strategic Fund Services (‘SFS') business
provides banking products and services _
primarily to our global private equity (‘PE’) and
venture capital (‘VC’) clients.

We were part of the SVB Financial Group
(‘SVBFG’) at the end of the period to which
these accounts relate.

Our goal

We are dedicated to supporting entrepreneurs
and clients of all sizes and stages throughout
their life cycles. Our vision is to create an
innovation centre of excellence that will
support breakout early-stage companies up to
FTSE 100 sized corporates, and the VC and
PE funds that provide capital to help these
organisations grow and scale.

Our clients

We provide products and services to serve the
needs of our clients in the UK and through our

representative offices in Sweden and
Denmark.

Our technology clients tend to be in the
industries of enterprise software and hardware
(such as infrastructure software, applications,
software services), consumer software and
internet (such as digital content and
advertising technology), frontier technologies
(such as semiconductors, communications,
data, storage and electronics), financial
technologies, and climate technology and
sustainability. Our life science/healthcare
clients primarily tend to be in the industries of
biopharma, healthtech, medical devices,
healthcare services and diagnostics and tools.

We serve our commercial company clients
throughout their life cycles, beginning with the
early-stage and progressing through later
stages as their needs mature and expand. We
serve these clients primarily through the

Our history

Silicon Valley Bank US (‘SVB US’) established
a business development office in the UK in
2004, referring credit opportunities to the US.
In 2012 the UK Branch of SVB US opened its
doors, providing cash management and FX
products, as well as credit products.

On 28 March 2018, the Prudential Regulation
Authority (PRA) issued Supervisory
Statement 1/18 ('SS1/18') requiring UK
branches of international banks who meet
certain threshold criteria to operate as a
subsidiary, and to be supervised on a stand-
alone basis by the PRA and the Financial
Conduct Authority (‘FCA’).

In response, the legal structure of the UK
business was changed: a UK limited company
was formed, and upon obtaining a banking
license from the UK financial regulators, the
court-based transfer of banking activities from
the UK branch of SVB US to SVBUK was
completed on 31 July 2022.

We continue to invest in the business, and we
have hired both client-facing and support staff
to support our operations, to ensure we fulfil
our regulatory commitments and to ensure we
are positioned for future growth.

Capitalisation

We were incorporated in the UK on 3 April
2020 with share capital of 1 ordinary share,
with a nominal value of £1 per share, allotted
to our former immediate parent SVB US, a

California state-chartered bank and a member
of the US Federal Reserve System. Our
ultimate parent at the end of the period to
which these accounts relate was SVBFG, a

’ diversified financial services company

incorporated in Delaware, US, which was
listed on NASDAQ until 10 March 2023.

We are a private company incorporated,
domiciled and registered in the UK. We are
authorised by the PRA and regulated by both
the PRA and the FCA.

On 31 May 2022, SVB US injected an
additional £10 million of capital into SVBUK,
consisting of 10 million shares, each with a
nominal value of £1 per share. On 20 June
2022, SVBUK was granted authorisation under
Part 4A of the Financial Services and Markets
Act 2000 (‘FSMA’). On 18 July 2022, SVB US
injected a further £1,323 million capital via
£968 million issuance of 968 million ordinary
shares, each with a nominal value of £1 per
share, £322 million perpetual Additional Tier 1
Notes and £33 million subordinated debt (Tier
2). On 25 July 2022, the transfer of business
was approved at a High Court hearing as part
of a legal process under Part VII of the FSMA.

Liquidity event and sale to HSBC

On 8 March 2023, SVBFG took actions in the
market to raise $2.25b capital following the
realisation of a $1.8b loss on US Treasury
Bonds. This action caused extensive concern
among investors and clients alike, and
resulted in rapid and significant withdrawals of
deposits which ultimately ended with the
suspension of trading of SVBFG’s shares on
the NASDAQ with the Federal Deposit
Insurance Company (‘FDIC’) taking over
operations of SVB US on 10 March 2023.

Despite the UK balance sheet being stand-
alone from the SVB US and having a stronger
capital profile than SVB US, the US concerns
caused a contagion event, which resulted in a
significant outflow of deposits from SVBUK.
On 10 March 2023, given the actions taken by
the FDIC in SVB US, and with consideration to
both the operational reliance SVBUK had on
SVB US and the considerable uncertainty over
possible future outflows, the Bank of England
announced that SVBUK would be put into an
insolvency procedure unless SVBUK could be
stabilised. On 13 March 2023, it was
announced that HSBC UK Bank plc (‘HBUK’),
the UK ring-fenced bank of HSBC Holdings
plc, had bought SVBUK, providing significant
funding and bringing the strength, safety and
security of the HSBC Group to the business.

The liquidity event of 10 March has had an
impact on the short-term performance of the
business, as clients look to diversify their
deposits in line with a broader industry trend
and we have reshaped our balance sheet in
response. The integration to the HSBC Group
has created opportunities for our existing and
future client base, and we are excited to bring
HSBC’s products, platforms and access to
geographic markets to our clients.

In acquiring us, HSBC strengthened their
commercial banking franchise and enhanced
their ability to serve innovative and fast-

growing firms. The acquisition accelerates
HSBC's future innovation sector plans by 3 to
4 years by bringing in capabilities immediately,
such as our deep industry sector knowledge
and embedded industry ecosystem
relationships with founders and funders.

Business review

Market and business overview

Market volatility and increasing economic
uncertainty were the backdrop in 2022 and
both had an impact on our target markets and
the broader ‘innovation economy’. Rising
interest rates, lack of clarity on private
company valuations, and recession risks
cooled investment activity and reduced M&A
and IPO volumes.

However, we continued to see a deployment of
capital overall, despite the significant
slowdown in VC investment in the second half
of 2022.

Private fundraising is a key driver of our
deposit growth. The current tempered
investment environment, coupled with
elevated levels of operational cash burn by our
clients, have slowed our deposit growth
trajectory. However, significant amounts of
investment capital (‘dry powder’) could lead to
an increase in venture investment activity as
confidence returns. Demand for lending
remains as companies seek to extend their
cash runway and manage expenses until
conditions in equity markets improve and
valuations for public and private companies
normalise.

Despite the turbulence of 2022 and the early
signs of a downturn in investor activity towards
the end of the year, we were profitable, Credit
losses remained low throughout the period,
underpinned by our reliable underwriting
model, diligent portfolio management, and
deep subject matter expertise in the markets
we serve.

The outlook for technology and healthcare
innovation industries is supported by the
growing importance in the role they play in
advanced economies like the UK and potential
returns presented to investors. We are
positioned to play a key role in supporting
future growth and the continued maturation of
these ecosystems through our industry

Environmental matters

We recognise the significant societal,
ecological and economic threats of climate
change. Given the products and services we
offer to support niche markets within the
innovation economy, we see many clients
tackling the climate crisis in numerous ways
through their innovations and investments, and
we take seriously our responsibility to reduce
our own climate impact.

Climate risks

Climate risk is defined as the actual or
potential negative impacts of climate-related
conditions and events on our financial
statements, business operations, or value
chains as a whole. The financial risks from
climate change, which we are subject to,
include the ability of a client to repay their
loans. We are also subject to transition risk,
which arises from efforts to reduce or eliminate
greenhouse gas emissions, such as through
changes in policy or regulatory requirements,
as well as changes to business models or
technology required to reach national climate
change targets. These risks can also have
direct and indirect effects through supply
chains and changes to client operating
models.

We acknowledge and are aware of the climate
impacts of both our supply chains and our
operations. Post the acquisition of HSBC, we
are in the process of evaluating and, where

Anti-bribery and corruption risk

Bribery is corruption which exists at all levels
of society in varying degrees. It is regarded as
a symptom of power and greed. Inadequate
governance and corrupt practices in:high level

political, commercial and industry dealings are.

detrimental to the economy and society at

We take a zero-tolerance approach towards
bribery and corruption and we are committed
to complying with applicable legal and
regulatory requirements, primarily the UK
Bribery Act 2010. This enables the prosecution
of individuals and commercial organisations
that do not have effective controls in place to
prevent bribery and corruption.

Overall accountability for Risk Management
lies with the UK Board. However, it is the
Money Laundering Reporting Officer (MLRO’),
in their role as Senior Manager Function
(‘SMF 17') who is accountable for our
compliance with our anti-bribery and
corruption obligations. The MLRO, together
with the Financial Crime Team, keep our Anti-
Bribery and Corruption Policy, procedures and
underlying supportive guidance up to date.
These help us develop systems and controls
which reduce our exposure to the risk of
bribery and corruption.

To ensure all of our colleagues understand
their roles and responsibilities in preventing
and detecting bribery and corruption, a full set
of guidelines have been put in place. To help
colleagues stay up to date, all must complete
anti-bribery and corruption training annually.
We also engage employees with additional
training sessions and relevant updates, when
required, to ensure all are aware of their
obligations.

Our Anti-Bribery and Corruption Policy
contains a set of key principles. These include:

1. The Policy clearly defines prohibited
activities. These include facilitation
payments and corrupt activity, as well as
the offer, request, giving or receiving of a
bribe.

2. All colleagues are prohibited from making
political contributions.

3. Giving/receiving cash or cash equivalent
gifts (e.g. gift cards/vouchers/E-Cards)
regardless of the value is prohibited.

4. Reimbursing Public Official expenses is
strictly prohibited.

5. Charitable or sponsorship payments can
only be made having obtained the
appropriate authorisations.

6. Any gifts and entertainment received are
recorded in our register, and any gifts
offered are captured in our expense system
and are subject to thresholds and approval
limits.

We are open about the bribery and corruption
related issues that we face, and these are
discussed regularly at our Financial Crime
Committee.

We ensure that independent, risk-based
oversight and assurance activity is in place
across all three lines of defence. This allows
us to determine the effectiveness of our anti-
bribery and corruption framework which
facilitates compliance with the relevant legal
and regulatory obligations within the UK.

Impairment of loans, undrawn loan
commitments and financial investments

As a lender, we face the risk that our
borrowers will fail to repay their loans when
due. If borrower defaults cause large
aggregate losses, it could have a material
adverse effect on our business, results of
operations or financial condition. We reserve
for such losses by establishing an ECL
reserve, which results in a charge to our

earnings as a provision for credit losses.
Although we have established an evaluation
process designed to determine the adequacy
of our ECL that uses historical and other
objective information reflective of the
classification of loans and fixed income
investments, the establishment of credit losses
are also dependent on macroeconomic
forecasts as well as the subjective experience
and judgement of our management. Actual
losses, which have been low to date, are
difficult to forecast, especially if such losses
stem from factors beyond our historical
experience, or are otherwise inconsistent with
our credit quality assessments. There can be
no assurance that our ECL will be sufficient to
absorb future credit losses or prevent a
material adverse effect on our business,
financial condition or results of operations.

Our ability to accurately forecast estimated
credit losses depends on whether our credit
loss model and related inputs correspond to
actual loss rates.

A commitment to extend credit is a formal
agreement to lend funds to a client as long as
the conditions established under the
agreement have been satisfied. However, the
actual borrowing needs of our clients may
exceed or be lower than our expected funding
requirements. For example, our client
companies may be more dependent on our
credit commitments in a challenging economic
environment due to the lack of available credit
elsewhere, the increasing costs of credit
through other channels, or the limited
availability of financings from PE or VC firms
when certain clients increase utilisation of
credit lines to secure liquidity. In addition,
limited partner investors of our PE and VC
fund clients may fail to meet their underlying
investment commitments due to liquidity or
other financing difficulties, which may impact
our clients’ borrowing needs.

Interest rate risk

IRRBB is the risk to earnings and capital
arising from changes in interest rates and the
impact of those changes on interest-sensitive
assets and liabilities.

— Duration risk is the estimate of adverse
market value sensitivity from re-pricing of
banking products following a change in
market interest rates that are mismatched
across time horizons.

— Basis risk is the risk that arises when
banking book items re-price differently, in
terms of timing and magnitude, in relation
to different reference rates.

— Optionality risk arises from the discretion
that a bank's clients and counterparties
have in respect of their contractual relations
and embedded interest rate caps and
floors.

- Where possible we seek to match assets and

liabilities creating a natural interest rate hedge
and actively hedge residual positions to
ensure risk exposure stays within risk limits.
Our primary IRRBB metrics are Net Interest
income (‘NII') and Economic Value of Equity
(EVE') risk measures.

~ Nil at risk measures the one year impact to
net interest income of a 100 basis point
parallel shift to the yield curve. This metric
captures short term sensitivity of earnings
to a change in interest rates.

~ EVE at risk measures the change in the net
present value of our assets and liabilities
under an immediate 200 basis point
parallel shift to the yield curve. This metric
considers a longer time horizon, measured
on a present value basis, than the Nil
based metric.

IRRBB is monitored monthly and Board risk
appetite is set against a 12 month view of NIl
at risk for all currencies and maturities. EVE at
risk is also a determinant of Board risk
appetite and monitored to ensure longer term
risk is managed effectively.

(a) Income and expense

Interest income and expense

interest income and expense for ail financial instruments, excluding those classified as held for
trading or designated at fair value, are recognised in ‘Interest income’ and ‘Interest expense’ in the
income statement using the effective interest method. Interest on credit-impaired financial assets is
recognised using the rate of interest used to discount the future cash flows for the purpose of
measuring the impairment loss.

The effective interest rate is the rate that discounts the estimated future cash flows from the financial
asset to the financial asset’s amortised cost before any allowances for expected credit losses. For
investment in financial investments, this would imply the effective interest rate is the rate that will
discount the financial investments’ future interest payments and its maturity value to the financial
investments’ market price at the time of purchase.

We calculate the effective interest rate based on the estimated cash flows (for example, prepayment,
extension, call and similar options), but excludes expected credit losses, over the expected life of the
financial asset. The calculation includes all fees paid or received between parties to the contract that
are an integral part of the effective interest rate, transaction costs, and all other premiums or
discounts.

Any income received by us that is an integral part of the effective interest rate for a financial
instrument is treated as an adjustment to the effective interest rate and hence falls under the scope of
IFRS 9.

Any fees that are not integral to the effective interest rate of the financial instrument are accounted for
under IFRS 15, Accounting for Revenue Recognition (Contracts with Customers)

~Non-interest income and expense

We generate fee income from services provided at a fixed price over time, such as account service, or
when we deliver a specific transaction at a point in time, such as syndication fees and spot foreign
exchange services. Fee income is generally earned from short-term contracts with payment terms
that do not include a significant financing component.

We recognise fees earned on transaction-based arrangements at a point in time when it has fully
provided the service to the client. Where the contract requires services to be provided over time,
income is recognised on a systematic basis over the life of the agreement. Where we offer a package
of services that contains multiple non-distinct performance obligations, such as those included in
account service packages, the promised services are treated as a single performance obligation. If a
package of services contains distinct performance obligations, such as those including both account
and foreign exchange services, the corresponding transaction price is allocated to each performance
obligation based on the estimated stand-alone selling prices.

(c) Recognition and derecognition of financial instruments
Recognition

Financial assets are recognised when the Company becomes a party to the contractual rights and

Derecognition

Financial assets, or a portion thereof, are derecognised when the contractual rights to receive the
cash flows from the assets have expired, or when substantially all the risks and rewards of ownership
have been transferred.

(d) Valuation of financial instruments

All financial instruments are initially recognised at fair value. Fair value is the price that would be
received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market
participants at the measurement date. The fair value of a financial instrument on initial recognition is
generally its transaction price (that is, the fair value of the consideration given or received). However,
if there is a difference between the transaction price and the fair value of financial instruments whose
fair value is based on a quoted price in an active market or a valuation technique that uses only data
from observable markets, we recognise the difference as a trading gain or loss at inception (a ‘day 1
gain or loss’). In all other cases, the entire day 1 gain or loss is deferred and recognised in the income
statement over the life of the transaction either until the transaction matures or is closed out or the
valuation inputs become observable.

The fair value of financial instruments is generally measured on an individual basis. However, in
cases where we manage a group of financial assets and liabilities according to its net market or credit
risk exposure, the fair value of the group of financial instruments is measured on a net basis but the
underlying financial assets and liabilities are presented separately in the financial statements, unless
they satisfy the IFRS offsetting criteria. Financial instruments are classified into one of three fair value
hierarchy levels, described in Note 11, ‘Fair values of financial instruments carried at fair value’.

(e) Classification of financial instruments

In classifying each financial instrument, we assess the objective of the business model in which the

Factors considered by us in determining the business model for a group of assets include past
experience on how the cash flows for these assets were collected, how the asset's performance is
evaluated and reported to key management personnel, how risks are assessed and managed and
how managers are compensated.

(h) Impairment of financial assets

Expected credit losses (‘ECL’) are recognised for loans and advances to clients, financial investments
held at amortised cost, and certain loan commitments. At initial recognition, allowance (or provision in
the case of some loan commitments) is required for ECL resulting from default events that are
possible within the next 12 months, or less, where the remaining life is less than 12 months, ('12-
month ECL’). In the event of a significant increase in credit risk, allowance (or provision) is required
for ECL resulting from all possible default events over the expected life of the financial instrument
(‘lifetime ECL’).

We maintain a systematic process for the evatuation of individual loans and portfolio segments for
inherent risk of estimated credit losses for loans. At the time of approval, each loan is assigned a
credit risk rating. Credit risk ratings are assigned on a scale of 1 to 10, with 1 representing loans with
a low risk of non-payment, 9 representing loans with the highest risk of non-payment and 10
representing loans which have been written-off. The credit risk ratings for each loan are monitored
and updated on an ongoing basis. This credit risk rating process includes, but is not limited to,
consideration of such factors as payment status, the financial condition and operating performance of
the borrower, borrower compliance with loan covenants, underlying collateral values and performance
trends, the degree of access to additional capital, the presence of credit enhancements such as third
party guarantees (where applicable), the degree to which the borrower is sensitive to external factors
and the depth and experience of the borrower's management team. Our policies require a committee
of senior management to review, at least quarterly, credit relationships with a credit risk rating of 5
through 9 that exceed specific monetary values.

Measurement of ECL

ECL is a probability-weighted estimate of the present value of credit losses, taking account of forward-
looking information that includes a range of possible economic outcomes. ECL is measured as the
difference between contractual cash flows and expected cash flows, discounted at the asset's
effective interest rate.

When measuring ECL, we assign a ‘stage’ to each asset (Stage 1, 2 or 3) which is based on changes
in credit risk as at the reporting date compared to its credit risk at initial recognition. Further we assess
the probability of default, the expected exposure at the time of default, and the loss that is expected to
arise on default. The maximum period considered is the maximum contractual period over which we
are exposed to credit risk.

Credit-impaired financial assets

A loan is considered credit-impaired when, based upon currently known information, it is deemed

agreement. We continuously monitor our loan portfolio for impairment. Within each class of loans, we
review individual loans for impairment based on credit risk ratings. Loans risk-rated 5 through 7 are
performing loans; however, we consider them as demonstrating higher risk, which requires more
frequent review of individual exposures. Such loans translate to an internal rating of ‘Performing
(Criticised)’.

For each loan identified as impaired, we measure the impairment based upon the present value of
expected future cash flows discounted at the loan’s effective interest rate and probability weighting
different outcome scenarios. In limited circumstances, we may measure impairment based on the
loan's observable market price or the fair value of the collateral less selling costs if the loan is
collateral dependent. Impaired loans with collateral will have independent appraisals completed and
accepted at least annually. The fair value of the collateral will be determined by the most recent
appraisal, as adjusted to reflect a reasonable marketing period for the sale of the asset(s) and an
estimate of reasonable selling expenses. If it is determined that the value of an impaired loan is less
than the recorded investment in the loan, net of previous write-offs and payments collected, we
recognise impairment through the allowance for expected loan losses as determined by its analysis.

For those assets that have become credit-impaired, interest revenue is subsequently calculated by

Impairment stages

Measurement of ECL depends on the ‘stage’ of the financial asset, based on changes in credit risk
occurring since initial recognition.

Stage 1 is assigned to a financial instrument when first recognised. For Stage 1 instruments,
expected credit losses ('ECL') are measured based on expected defaults that are possible within 12
months after the reporting date ('12-month ECL’). Newly originated facilities in a given period
inherently qualify for Stage 1 unless there is an unlikely event where a loan is originated and
immediately shows a significant decrease in credit risk or credit impairment.

Stage 2 is assigned to financial instruments that have had a SICR since initial recognition, but that do
not have objective evidence of impairment. For Stage 2 instruments, lifetime ECL are recognised, as
being the ECL that result from all possible default events over the maximum contractual period during
which the entity is exposed to credit risk.

Stage 3 is assigned to financial assets that have objective evidence of impairment at the reporting

date. For Stage 3 instruments, lifetime ECL are recognised based upon an individual impairment
assessment on the exposure.

Significant increase in credit risk (‘SICR’) - Stage 2

If a loan or investment security shows a significant increase in credit risk, it is transferred to Stage 2
unless it meets the criteria for Stage 3. The assessment criteria for SICR include a combination of
absolute and relative criteria and supported by a backstop criteria. The most recent full re-
underwriting date will be used as the comparative period to the current reporting period when
assessing SICR based on the relative criteria as it (i) captures the most recent point where the risk
related to the credit was fully resized and terms and pricing were updated accordingly, and (ii) If there
is a significant modification leading to a derecognition of the existing facility and recognition of a new
facility, the full re-underwriting point would essentially become the new origination date for that loan.

Absolute Criteria

For loans and undrawn loan commitments, the absolute criteria are designed to identify facilities
where the internal Credit Risk Rating (‘(CRR') or modification status clearly indicate that there has
been a SICR versus the initiation date without having to directly compare the current relevant data
point to equivalent data points at the initiation date. Where a facility is marked with a CRR of 5 to 7
and we have not granted a concession due to the financial hardship of the borrower then it will fall into
Stage 2. Where a facility is marked with a CRR of 8 to 9 then it will fall into Stage 3. The absolute
criteria automatically bucket loans with a CRR 5 or worse into Stage 2 or Stage 3.

Relative criteria

For loans and undrawn loan commitments, the relative criteria leverages changes in CRR since the
initial recognition date to approximate the change in the risk of default since the inception of the loan.
This will be achieved by comparing the current CRR (as of the measurement date) to the CRR at the
initial recognition date. A downgrade in CRR (i.e. risk rating notches) since the initial recognition date
is used to determine whether there has been a SICR. The relative criteria are therefore focused on
the number of risk rating notch downgrades a pass rated facility (CRR 1 to 4) could undergo before
being deemed to have experienced SICR.

The current CRR must be better or equal to the CRR at origination (or last full re-underwriting) in
order to reverse a prior SICR designation. If the current CRR is worse than the CRR at origination (or
last full re-underwriting), then it either remains SICR, if previously identified as such, or is designated
as SICR.

Days past due “backstop”

Any facilities that are 30 or more days past due (‘DPD’) are deemed to have experienced SICR and
are included in stage 2, insofar as the absolute and relative criteria discussed above have not already
identified the loan as stage 2. The 30 DPD rule functions as a “backstop” to catch any remaining
credits that should be in stage 2, unless that credit has met the criteria for credit impairment (stage 3)
based on the absolute criteria defined above.

(n) Leases

We have entered into an operating lease for our premises. At the inception of a lease, it is evaluated
to determine whether the lease will be accounted for as an operating or finance lease. We had no
finance lease obligations at 31 December 2022 and 31 December 2021. We have made an
accounting policy election not to recognise right-of-use ("ROU') assets and lease liabilities that arise
from short-term leases and leases of low-value for any class of underlying asset.

ROU assets represent our right to use an underlying asset for the lease term and lease liabilities
represent our obligation to make lease payments arising from the lease, Operating lease ROU assets
and liabilities are recognised at the commencement date based on the present value of lease
payments over the lease term. As most leases do not provide an implicit rate, we use our incremental
borrowing rate based on the information available at the commencement date in determining the
present value of lease payments. Lease expense for lease payments is recognised on a straight-line
basis over the lease term. We review our ROU assets for impairment whenever events or changes in
circumstances indicate that the carrying amount of an asset may not be recoverable. The resulting
impairment charge, if any, is allocated to the underlying assets on a pro rata basis using their relative
carrying amounts.

Amounts expensed to the Income Statement represent the interest costs on lease liabilities presented

within Interest Expense and the depreciation charge on right-of-use assets, which is presented within
Depreciation and Impairment of Property, Plant and Equipment and Right-of-Use Assets.

: 11. Fair values of financial instruments carried at fair value

Our derivative instruments are financial instruments recorded at fair value on a recurring basis. We
make estimates regarding valuation of assets and liabilities measured at fair value in preparing our
financial statements. The fair value of financial instruments is measured on an individual basis for -
financial assets and liabilities.

We measure fair values using the following fair value hierarchy, which reflects the significance of the
inputs used in making the measurements.

~ Level 1: Inputs that are quoted market prices (unadjusted) in active markets for identical
instruments.

~ Level 2: Inputs other than quoted prices included within Level 1 that are observable either directly
(i.e. as prices) or indirectly (i.e. derived from prices). This category includes instruments valued
using: quoted market prices in active markets for similar instruments; quoted prices for identical or
similar instruments in markets that are considered less than active; or other valuation techniques in
which all significant inputs are directly or indirectly observable from market data.

~ Level 3: Inputs that are unobservable. This category includes all instruments for which the valuation
technique includes inputs that are not observable and the unobservable inputs have a significant
effect on the instrument's valuation. This category includes instruments that are valued based on
quoted prices for similar instruments for which significant unobservable adjustments or ,
assumptions are required to reflect differences between the instruments.

Our accounting policy in relation to the valuation of financial instruments is given in Note 2(d).

Post model adjustments

We consider certain qualitative factor adjustments, or post model adjustments, to the results obtained
through our quantitative ECL models to address known limitations of the impairment model used to
calculate ECL, including model imprecision, emerging risk assessments, trends and other subjective
factors that may not be adequately represented in the quantitative ECL models.

We consider PMAs to the modelled ECL provisions on a periodic basis to ensure the reserve
adequately reflects expected losses. The adjustments are taken into consideration during regular
model development exercises. These are reviewed and approved by the IFRS 9 Risk Oversight
Committee and the Audit Committee, focusing on the rationale and quantum of the adjustments.
Given the current processes and risk monitoring by us, management believes the combination of the
quantitative model results and the qualitative factor adjustment represents a reasonable and
appropriate estimate of ECL.

At 31 December 2022, a single PMA of £7.0m was applied, increasing the ECL for the idiosyncratic
risk of a loss within the Strategic Fund Services loan portfolio. The PMA is based on the upper quartile
of borrowers of SFS borrowers with credit risk rating of 4.
