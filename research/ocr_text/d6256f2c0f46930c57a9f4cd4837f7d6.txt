Commitment to the Co-operative ‘Alternative’

The Co-operative Bank was formed as
part of a movement which aimed to give
ordinary people more power over their
economic destiny. This purpose has
remained and the programme of
investment and technological advance
which characterised the Bank’s activity
last year was designed to strengthen its
independent capability and its position as
an alternative within the financial world,

The longer term thinking has been seen
not only in the determined attitude to
investment, but also in the search for
new ways to help economic development,
It has always been unwise to pretend
that the British banking system, for all its
merits, was finite and in ways
appropriate to its means the Bank has
been looking to the future.

‘In London during the year, work was

completed on a new clearing centre, east
of the City, with capacity to serve the
needs of other financial institutions. The
development costs of the clearing centre
have been borne in 1982, but future
operating expenses will be defrayed by
income derived from our acting as
clearing agents for other financial
institutions. As new clearers, National
Girobank decided it made sense to use
our modern and efficient centre and the
bank reached agreement with Abbey
National Building Society to clear its new
cheques. The latter move broke new

ground in providing direct access to
clearing facilities for members of
building societies and underlined the
Bank’s view that it is the customers of
financial institutions who deserve
priority of care.

In future years, the clearing centre will ,
become not only a symbol of the Bank’s
determination to make its own way, buta
useful source of contribution to income,

These moves can only benefit the Bank's
status and it is also with particular
pleasure that | report the appointment
during 1982 of the Bank's Chief General
Manager to the Council of the Institute of
Bankers.

Discussions with trade union leaders
have led to the blueprint for the
establishment of a new financial
institution within the. UK. A document
was circulated to the general secretaries
of all TUC-affiliated unions during the
summer, and the institution will bring
the trade unions directly into the
financial arena and strengthen ties
between the trade union and co-
operative movements.

While the start-up capital of this new
Joint venture financial institution will be
initially modest, the establishment of
such an institution marks a significant
development in the Bank's history and
undoubtedly carries potential for
bringing a whole range of the Co-
operative Bank's many services to the
benefit of trade unien members.

1982 have ensured that considerable
benefits accrued to the Co-operative
Retail Societies, the ultimate owners of
the banking group through our holding
organisation, the Co-operative Wholesale
Society (CWS).

In financial terms alone, the Handycard
and Handyloan services have meant that
the Retail Societies have been able to
release collectively substantial amounts
of working capital which would
otherwise have been tied up in consumer

Other Corporate Sector Development
‘In publishing a standard business tariff

during 1982, the Bank once again scored
a ‘first’ in the banking industry, while
simultaneously underlining its own
commitment towards the provision of
assistance to the business sector,
thereby helping in revitalising the
economy of the nation.

The Bank recognised that, against a
background of rising unemployment, one
hopeful development had been the
growth in worker co-operatives. The
Bank's Chief General Manager serves on
the Board of the Co-operative Develop-
ment Agency and more than half of

Britain's worker co-operatives are our
customers. In practical terms, they are a
particular band of small businesses

which require special consideration .

because of their constitutions. In many
areas, they generate new employment
opportunities and it is to be hoped that
the government will strengthen the arm
of the CDA in future months.

Personal Sector Development

In the personal sector, the year began
with the launch of the ‘Cheque and Save‘
account, an interest-bearing money
transmission service, the introduction of
which helped to enhance the reputation
of the Bank as an innovator in the
provision of financial services. The
potential for further’ development of
‘Cheque and Save’ is demonstrated by
the intention to launch it through the
Bank's branch network in 1983,

Branch network development continued
at Colchester, Islington and Lincoln.
1983 will see the opening of further new
branches in Kingston-upon-Thames,
Oxford and Chesterfield, marking a
significant step towards achieving the
plan of establishing around 100
branches by 1990, Futhermore, the
Bank's City Office has been extended and
refurbished at its 78/80 Cornhill

‘- premises. This development had been

planned for some time and now provides
a suitable focal point for the Bank's
growing City and International activities.

Other personal sector developments

_during the year have included substantial

further penetration of the unbanked
sector of the population, In the latter
respect, support of the committee of
London Clearing Bankers’ working party
on the direct payment of wages has
proved a great asset, as has the highly
developed in-store credit card package
offered by the Bank.

In general terms, the year has been a
successful one in terms of the
development of the personal sector. Once
again, a sharp increase in the number of
personal customers utilising the Bank's
services has been recorded and | feel
secure in my belief that this trend will
continue in the future.

Operating Performance

Despite the difficulties of 1982, the Bank
has followed its co-operative principles
and charted a responsible course with
the longer term in mind.

It is unfortunate that, at a time of heavy
planned investment, the Bank had to live
both with the impact of the windfall
profits tax, which confiscated four-fifths
of the 1981 profits, and an unexpectedly
severe loss of £2,319,000 from the
finance house subsidiary, First Co-
operative Finance, a reflection of the
depth of the current economic and
business depression. However, the
benefit of firm cost control and business
development strategies began to be felt
during the year and, over the last quarter,
the company’s trading performance
showed a significant turnround such that
we are now hopeful that First Co-
operative will resume as a major
contributor ta the Bank Group’s profits
during 1983.

The casts of the continued programme of
investment, together with First Co-
operative’s losses and the impact of the
windfall profits tax, produced an
unavoidable dip in the Banking Group's
profitability during 1982, the parent
bank’s operating profit declining from
£5.3 million to £3.9 million.

Fortunately, as a co-operative institution,
we are sot required to maximise short
t-y78 yes titability and we have, therefore,
be’n? w.2 to continue undeterred in the
platined establishment of a sound
business base for long term development.

However, an increasing capital base,
maintained from retained earnings, is
essential to the achievement of the
Bank's objectives. At the end of the year,
the authorised share capital was
increased from £8 million fully issued to
£20 million by the creation of 12 miltion
new shares of £1 each. The Board of the
CWS, the Bank's holding company,
subscribed at par for 3.2 million of these
new shares, bringing the issued share
capital to £11.2 million. The sum
involved was equivalent to the sum lost
in windfall profits tax,

Accounting Policies

(a) Basis of consolidation and accounting date

The accounts of the group are made up for the 52 weeks to 8th January, 1983. Since the group accounting
date is virtually coterminous with the calendar year 1982 the financial year’s figures are headed 1982 and the
corresponding figures for the previous year are headed 1981. The Accounts have been prepared under the .-
historical cost convention and in accordance with Section 152A and Schedule 8A of the Companies Act 1948,

{b) Investments 3
(i) Investment Income :

income from investments includes amortization of premiums and discounts on dated stocks, credit for which is ;
taken in equal annual instalments from the date of acquisition to maturity, . :
4

t

(ii) Sale of Investments

Realised profits and losses on sales of investments are taken to profit and loss account in the year in which — - .
they arise.

.

(c) Losses on Advances . i
The amount charged against operating profit for losses on advances comprises specific provision against |
identifiable losses and a general provision to cover latent but unidentifiable losses due to doubtful debts, Both :
provisions are based on a year end appraisal of advances, Advances are shown on the balance sheet after f
deducting these provisions, : '

(d) Depreciation

Depreciation is provided on a straight line basis at the following rates which are estimated to write down the
assets to realisable values at the end of their useful lives:

Freehold and long leasehold buildings 2°5 per cent per annum
Short leasehold buildings life of lease

Equipment 10 to 20 per cent per annum
Vehicles 25 per cent per annum

14

{e) Leasing

Income is credited to the profit and loss account under the ‘Investment Period’ principle except in the case of
_ motor vehicle leasing, where the ‘Rule of 78’ formula applies.

‘Leased equipment is valued at original cost less depreciation, which is calculated to write off that cost over the
primary period of the Jease. Depreciation for the period represents the full amount of leasing payments due in
the period fess the amounts credited to the profit and loss account.

ee pee

(f) Hire Purchase and instalment credit agreements

Credit is taken in the profit and loss account for finance charges on hire purchase and instalment credit

agreements, net of dealers’ commission, using the ‘Rule of 78,' with the. exception of agreements having a
duration of five years and over where finance charges are allocated onan actuarial basis.

. (9) Deferred taxation

Provision is made for taxation on timing differences between profits stated in the accounts and profits -
computed for taxation purposes at the current rate of corporation tax, only if there is a reasonable probability ©
that such taxation will become payable in the foreseeable future. Provision is made for short term timing
differences that reverse in the following year only where such provisions result in a liability.

_ (h) Associated companies

In the consolidated balance sheet associated companies are shown at cost, including advances, less amounts
written off, and the group’s share of reserves from the date of acquisition.

(i) Exchange rates

4 ‘ Balences in foreign currencies are expressed in sterling at the rate ruling at the balance sheet date. All
exchange differences are dealt with in the profit and loss account.

(j) Premium on acquisition of shares

The premium paid on the acquisition of shares in subsidiaries, being the excess of the amount paid over the net
tangible assets at the date of acquisition, has been written off against reserves.

Basis of calculation,
The current cost accounts have been prepared on the basis of Statement of Standard Accounting Practice
No. 16.

2, Adjustment for inflation.
(a) Monetary working capital
The monetary working capital adjustment allows for the effect of price changes on the net monetary
working capital by applying the change in Retail Price Index to the average monetary working capital
during the year. For this purpose monetary working capital includes al! assets other than fixed assets
less liabilities arising from operating activities.
(b) Depreciation adjustment
The depreciation adjustment is the difference between depreciation based on the current
replacement cost of fixed assets and the depreciation charge in the historical cost accounts, In
estimating the value to the business the following bases have been adopted:
{i} Freehold and leasehold premises were professionally valued in 1981, These values have been
adjusted to current cost in 1982 by the use of relevant indices,
{ii) Computer hardware and other electronic equipment, together with any related capital
expenditure on software, have been valued at current replacement cost.
(ili) Other fixed assets have been valued at historical cost adjusted to current cost by the use of ”
relevant indices,
(c) Gearing adjustment
The gearing adjustment reduces the monetary working capital and depreciation adjustment by the
proportion of capital provided other than by sharehosders’ funds.
(d) Corresponding amounts
The corresponding amounts for the previous period are shown in values relating to last year, without
further adjustment.
(e) Except as set out above the policies used in the current cost accounts are the same as those used in
the historical cost accounts.
