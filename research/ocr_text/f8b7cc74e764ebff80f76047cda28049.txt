Managing Director’s Statement

Although there are currently no UN, EU or UK sanctions against Bank Sepah International Pic (“the Bank”) the
United States’ unilateral withdrawal from the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) in 2018 and the
consequential re-imposition of Office of Foreign Asset Control (OFAC) sanctions against numerous Iranian
entities and their international subsidiaries including the Bank continues to severely restrict Iranian trade and
banking business in Europe and the UK. Access to the SWIFT network was suspended for the majority of Iran’s
State-owned banks including the Bank in November 2018.The conflict between Russia and Ukraine, and the
ongoing events in the Middle East, whilst not directly affecting the Bank, continue to impact the negotiations that
were taking place between the original parties to the JCPOA. Talks have recently re-commenced but the Bank
does not expect any significant improvement in relations in the short term.

The above factors and the current political situation in the US and the UK are unlikely to have a positive impact
on the Bank’s operating environment in the near term and so the Bank continues to take a cautious approach to
asset recognition and business opportunities during the year. Recognising the PRA focus on strong risk controls,
financial and operational resilience the Bank’s three-year business plan remains similarly cautious. In addition,
under the Board’s direction the Bank has taken professional advice on contingency planning and is in the process
of updating the recovery options within its Recovery and Resolution Plan (RRP).

Since the permission to resume business given by the PRA in 2016, political events have resulted in little of the
potential business developing but the Bank has been successful in staff retention and further recruitment where
necessary, and in 2023-2024 refreshed its IT infrastructure to future proof operational functionality. These
achievements have enabled the Bank to deliver on the shareholder’s stated requirement that the Board and
Management manage the Bank to meet all PRA threshold conditions and ensure the Bank’s staff and systems
can meet any current operational needs as well as future business development once the political situation
improves. Following a cyber-attack in November 2024 the Bank was able to recover with no loss of data or
impact on customer service. The Bank has since upgraded controls, monitoring and security to further improve
its resilience to any future attack.

The Bank has continued to maintain strong capital and liquidity positions, utilising excess liquidity to achieve
good returns. The Bank’s High Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) have been supported by the purchase of state
issued Eurobonds and UK Gilts, under a Board-approved mandate. The Bank’s key capital and liquidity ratios
remain comfortably above regulatory minimum levels. As set out in note 2 we are satisfied that the Bank will
continue to be able to meet its liabilities as they fall due.

The success of the Bank’s remote working processes and infrastructure have led to the implementation of an
ongoing hybrid working from home programme providing staff with flexible arrangements and no reduction in
operational efficiency or of the ability of the Bank to function normally. This policy has proved effective and been
well received by staff. Management will continue to monitor business levels and Key Performance indicators to
ensure any need to change this policy is implemented in good time and with full staff consultation.

The Bank’s results for the year ended 31 March 2025 are set out in the Annual Report commencing on page 25.
The Bank’s assets are predominantly in Euros where interest rates have fallen over the past year, but with good
cost control the Bank has maintained profitability. Bank Sepah, tran and the Bank’s Board continue to see a
positive future for the Bank once the current political situation improves.

The ongoing support of the Shareholder and the Board’s continued stewardship have enabled the Bank, the
management team and staff to have another successful year in the face of major challenges. | would like to

directors old and new, have provided challenge and guidance to the Executives whilst maintaining good

Business strategy

The majority of the Bank’s credit exposure is to Iranian financial institutions in Europe and Iran. The ongoing US
action and the UK’s departure from the EU have all heavily restricted the Bank’s ability to pursue its Euro
business strategy. Lack of direct access to the UK payment systems continues to prevent Sterling business.

Currently the difficulties around the flow of funds and/or the perfection of security to support bilateral or
syndicated lending persist. The loss of SWIFT access has continued to restrict regular trade finance business.

Despite this, the Bank’s long-term strategy remains resolute: future growth of Trade Finance business alongside
good quality lending. The lack of opportunities to expand the lending book however has resulted in money market
placements to maintain profitability at plan levels.

To support its business strategy, in the years immediately following the implementation of the JCPOA, the Bank
recruited quality staff and upgraded its core IT systems. In 2024/2025 it refreshed core IT hardware and software
to cope with technology upgrade and to be ready to accommodate future business volume increases. Active
engagement with trade associations, advisory services and government information services support efforts to
ensure all staff are equipped with the skills to meet their roles. In line with Board and regulatory focus,
governance continues to be reviewed and enhanced embedding Financial and Operational Resilience as well
as Environmental, Social, Governance and Climate Change awareness in everything we do, with a suite of
policies and procedures which fit together and are regularly reviewed.

Financial and Operational Resilience

In line with PRA focus and despite the restrictions the Bank faces, Financial and Operational Resilience are core
to how the Bank is managed and operates. A strict Credit Policy with all exposures reviewed by Credit and/or
Risk Committees, and exposures predominantly to regulated Financial Institutions. The Bank maintains liquidity
and capital ratios well above regulatory requirements and has demonstrated its financial resilience in its ILAAP,
ICAAP and Recovery Pian stress test results.

Operational Resilience poses significant issues to the Bank as many third-party suppliers are unable to service
the Bank due to their banks’ or their own compliance policy as a result of US Sanctions. Despite this the Bank
has implemented technology upgrades, is able to support all staff working remotely and has a fully remote
disaster recovery provision. The Bank identified its Important Business Services (IBS), set impact tolerances,
mapped vulnerabilities, undertaken scenario tests and assessed their impact, in good time for the March 2025
PRA deadline.

Environmental, Social, Governance and Climate Change
The Bank has implemented an Environmental, Social, Governance and Climate Change Strategy taking account

of physical and transitional climate risks and embedding these in its Risk Framework and credit processes. The
Banks considers that its strategy complies with the requirements of the Companies Act.

A formal Climate Change assessment is incorporated within every credit application including positive scoring
for those which are environmentally sustainable or provide a net positive outcome. The Bank is an equal
opportunities employer, does not tolerate harassment or prejudice with applicable policies in place and has a
strong governance structure, with tone led from the top.

The Bank recognises that the economy of its home market, Iran, has traditionally been dominated by fossil fuel
and mineral resource sectors but Iran’s government has recently looked to support renewables and
diversification. Although the Bank has provided financing for traditional sectors in the past, there is currently
limited involvement in these areas. In time, the Bank’s focus will be on supporting sustainable projects and
facilitating the transition of these traditional sectors into more environmentally-friendly and socially-responsible
practices. This aligns with both the Iranian Government and the Shareholder’s strategy, and should in time lead
to good business opportunities.

Capital Adequacy -

The Bank has adopted the PRA’s implementation of the EU’s Capital Requirements Directive (CRD V). The
CRD V framework consists of three Pillars.

Pillar 1 determines the minimum capital requirements of banks to cover credit, market and operational risks.

Pillar 2 can be divided into two major components: (i) institutions are expected to establish sound, effective and
complete strategies and processes to assess and maintain, on an ongoing basis, the amounts, types and
distribution of internal capital commensurate to their risk profiles, an Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment
Process (ICAAP), as well as robust governance and internal control arrangements and (ii) a supervisory review
and evaluation process (SREP). The key purpose of the SREP is to ensure that institutions have adequate
arrangements, strategies, processes and mechanisms as well as capital and liquidity to ensure a sound
management and coverage of their risks, to which they are or might be exposed, including those revealed by
stress testing and risks that the institution may pose to the financial system.

As part of the CRD V requirements an ICAAP has been approved by the Board which identifies a number of
other (non-pillar 1) risks faced by the Bank which attract capital under the Pillar 2 rules. The total capital
requirement of the Bank is determined as the sum of the Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 capital requirements.

Pillar 3 of the CRD V requires all banks to publish certain details of their risk exposures, capital adequacy and
risk management procedures over and above that provided in the Annual Report and Financial Statements. This
information, which is not audited, has been published on the Bank’s website www.banksepah.co.uk.

The Bank’s capital has been well in excess of both the combined Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 and the PRA’s requirements
throughout the year. At 31 March 2025, the Bank’s total capital requirement amounted to €32.1m (2024: €39.1m)
which means that, with Tier 1 capital of €172.3m (2024: €171.4m), the Bank had a capital surplus of €140. 3m
(2024. €132.1m) at the year-end or 437.5% (2024: 337.6%). - . ~

Liquidity

The Bank is subject to the requirements set out in the PRA’s Rulebook. Individual Liquidity Guidance (iLG) is
set by the PRA through a Liquidity Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (LSREP) which, in turn, is guided
by the Bank’s own Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP).

The Treasury Department ensures that the Bank’s balance sheet remains robust and maintains liquidity in
accordance with both internal and regulatory requirements. The Bank continues to maintain sufficient liquidity to
meet all known and likely demands which could be made upon it by its customers and ensures that such liquidity
is available on a day-to-day basis.
Strategic Report (continued)

The Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) requires a bank to maintain unencumbered High Quality Liquid Assets
(“HQLA”) that are equal to or in excess of 100% of its total net cash outflow assumptions over a 30-day period.
The Bank maintained a healthy liquidity position during the year which was significantly in excess of the
regulatory minimum. The LCR showed a steady increase during the year primarily due to the purchase of
additional sovereign bonds which increased HQLA. The LCR at the year-end was 2034%.

Cash, cash equivalents and deposits placed with banks comprise 90% of the Bank’s total assets as at 31 March
2025 (2024: 90%).

Credit operations

The Bank has a conservative credit risk policy and credit risk appetite which are set by the Board. These are
monitored by the Risk Committee and implemented through the Bank’s Credit Committee which has a limited
delegated authority. All exposure limits are presented to Credit Committee with applications and reviews
including full credit analysis, input from Compliance and Risk, with Climate change risks assessed.

All limits are reviewed at least annually with Board approval for those above the Credit Committee’s delegated
authority.

Loans and Trade Finance

The bilateral and syndicated loan market has remained subdued as Iranian counterparties’ access to hard
currency for repayment is managed by the Central Bank of Iran and the Bank requires good quality security
which is difficult to perfect outside of Iran.

Although the Bank has access to the SEPAM messaging system which allows the transmission of L/Cs, a
shortage of hard currency allocation to standard L/C transactions in Europe by the Central Bank of Iran as it
prioritises strategic transactions, has resulted in no L/C business during the year. The bank continues to engage
with counterparties to access strategic transactions through forfait arrangements or indirect lending and meet
its business plan objectives. Meanwhile Nostros, Money Market lending and its bond portfolio generate the banks
interest income.

Total net outstanding commercial lending to customers represents 0% (2024: 0%) of the Bank’s total assets.

Treasury

Treasury operations are conducted under guidance of the Bank’s Asset Liability Committee (“ALCO”) and in
accordance with approved credit limits. Activities are reviewed by the Risk Committee. Interest rate risk and
foreign exchange risk are also monitored by ALCO. The Bank does not take proprietary positions or enter into
any derivative contracts.

The Bank has seen active non-Iranian correspondent banking relationships reduce since 2018, consequent to .
the US withdrawal from the JCPOA and the Bank’s OFAC listing. Treasury continues to seek new relationships
and strengthen existing ones with a daily financial markets commentary and regular contact.

At the year end, the Bank’s cash and cash equivalents, being cash on hand and current account balances with
correspondent banks, represent 47% of the Bank's total assets at 31 March 2025 (2024: 49%).

Risk management

The Board of Directors is responsible for setting the Bank’s overall risk appetite and documenting this within a
Risk Appetite Statement (“RAS”). A Risk Framework document which sets out the risk management structure,
its roles and operational systems is also approved by the Board, as is the Bank’s Risk Register.

The Bank operates a “three lines of defence” methodology. Individual departments within the Bank are the first.
line of defence and the senior managers in the department are the risk owners. There is appropriate segregation
of activities between departments consistent with the scale of the Bank’s business. Each department is subject
to relevant policies and have their own departmental procedures. Policies are approved by the Board and
procedures are approved by Executive Management Committee (EMC).

The second line of defence, comprising the separate Risk and Compliance departments, provide independent
oversight of the first line of defence. In accordance with the Risk Framework the first and second lines are
responsible for proposing changes to the policies and procedures to mitigate risk and for identifying emerging
risks.

The Risk department provides particular oversight of credit, liquidity, market and operational risk. Compliance
provides oversight in respect of regulatory risk and risk reporting. This second line of defence delivers regular
standing and ad hoc reports to the Risk Committee which review these and provides recommendations to the
Board. These reports include the Risk Register and the latest Monthly Risk Report. The latter report details the
principal risks facing the Bank and records month-end figures against the Early Warning Indicators and the
minimum level of risk appetite. The Bank’s principal and emerging risks are reviewed by the EMC and the Risk
Committee on a regular basis, alongside associated financial, operational and compliance controls. These
documents, along with Operational Risk Events and Loss notification reports, and their associated remedial
plans and mitigative actions, are formally reviewed by the Risk Committee on a quarterly basis.

The third line of defence consists of the internal audit function which provides independent assurance and
challenge, reporting to the Audit Committee. The internal audit function has been outsourced to an appropriately
qualified firm of accountants separate from the Bank’s external auditors.

Principal risks and uncertainties

The principal inherent risks that the Bank faces are credit, market, operational, concentration and liquidity risks.
Information on these principal risks is provided in note 25. The Directors have also identified the lack of access
to GBP clearing facilities, loss of key personnel, lack of diversity of IT service providers and the geo-political
uncertainties as giving rise to further major risks to which the bank is exposed.

The Bank is committed to maintaining robust and effective governance to ensure the efficient and prudent —
running of its operation. The responsibility for the risk management framework is discharged and administered
through a number of delegated committees, both Board and Executive. In addition, the Managing Director has
executive responsibility for the overall operational risk management of the Bank.

Day-to-day risk oversight is delegated to key control departments, namely, Risk, Compliance, Operations and
the Finance department ensuring that there is a robust management structure with clear segregation of duties
and responsibilities, where possible, in keeping with the scale and complexity of the Bank.

The Board has ultimate responsibility for the setting of the Bank's strategy, its risk appetite and on-going
management of the Bank's risk exposure. This is communicated and embedded in the Bank's culture through
a suite of policies and procedures. The Bank’s key risk principles are set out in its Risk Framework.

The Board and the management have taken appropriate measures to mitigate its key risks as far as possible
and have established other means to facilitate its GBP payments until such time it can re-join the GBP clearing
system. Meanwhile additional contingency planning is underway, including professional advice to help the Bank
mitigate its sterling liquidity risks. /

Further to the above risks, the Bank has also considered the potential risk and impact of the prominent global
crises on its operations such as ESG and climate change which have been covered at the start of this report.

Compliance and financial crime

The Bank has comprehensive policies and procedures in place to ensure that all its activities are compliant with
existing regulations, any applicable sanctions regimes and the Board's risk appetite.

requirements. The Bank recognises that its risk profile is high and therefore understands that enhanced systems
and controls must be put in place to ensure its adherence to

help mitigate financial crime and sanctions risks.

There is no “straight-through” processing of transactions — there is manual intervention to ensure stringent

In line with current regulations and best practice, all staff are trained in the Bank’s financial crime procedures
and are required to complete annual Anti-Money Laundering (AML) training. In addition, Compliance issue
guidance to staff on emerging threats or new legislation as they materialise throughout the year.

Through effective customer due diligence and ongoing monitoring of transactions, the Bank has implemented

Going concern basis of accounting

The Directors have reviewed the current and potential future business activities and financial position of the
Bank, including an assessment of capital and liquidity requirements for the foreseeable future in line with its
business plan. The review covers the going concern up to 31 July 2026. The going concern assessment is
prepared by Executive Management, reviewed and approved by the Board.

The planned business activities are very cautious and take into account the current geopolitical issues and
restrictions the Bank operates within.

As at 31 March 2025, the Bank held eligible capital of €172m (excluding current year profit) and had a capital
surplus of €140m (2024 €132m). In line with previous years, the 2025 profits will be retained to further Strengthen
the capital position.

The Bank's latest ICAAP and ILAAP documents’ stress tests, which were updated during the financial year,
demonstrate its resilience given its significant capital surplus and liquidity position. The bank ended the year
with very strong capital and liquidity ratios.

Based on this review, the Directors have a reasonable expectation that the Bank has adequate resources to

continue in operational existence for at least up to 31 July 2026. Therefore, the financial statements have been
prepared on a going concern basis.

Future developments
The Bank will continue to explore possibilities within the EEA following the UK's departure from the EU to

(b) Interest income and expense

Interest income and expense for all interest-bearing instruments are recognised in profit or loss, on an accruals
basis, using the effective interest method. The effective interest method is a method of calculating the amortised
cost of.a financial asset or liability and of allocating the interest income or interest expense. The effective interest
rate is the rate that exactly discounts the estimated future cash payments or receipts over the expected life of.
instrument or, when appropriate, a shorter period, to the net carrying amount of the financial asset or financial
liability.

The effective interest rate is calculated on initial recognition of the financial asset or liability, estimating the future
cash flows after considering all the contractual terms of the instrument but not future credit losses. The
calculation includes all amounts paid or received by the Bank including fees, expected early redemptions and
related penalties and premiums and discounts that are an integral part of the overall return as well as direct
incremental transaction costs related to the acquisition, issue or disposal of a financial instrument. For fixed rate
financial assets this is the original effective interest rate and for floating rate financial assets this is the current
effective interest rate.

Fees and commissions are generally recognised at the point in time when the performance obligation is satisfied.
Loan participation fees are amortised over the term of the loan through inclusion in the effective interest rate.
Commissions arising.from trade finance related business such as advising and payments under letters of credit
are recognised at the transaction date.

The classification and basis of measurement are subject to Bank’s business model for managing the financial
assets and the contractual cash flow characteristics of the financial assets, as detailed below.

Business Model Assessment

An assessment of the bank’s business model for managing financial assets is fundamental to the classification
of the financial asset. Financial assets are measured at amortised cost if they are held within the business model
whose objective is to collect contractual cash flows, and their contractual cashflows represent solely payments
of principal and interest. This constitutes the Bank’s HQLA. Financial assets are measured at FVOCI if they are
held within a business model whose objective is achieved by both collecting contractual cash flows and selling
financial assets, and their contractual cash flow represents solely payment of principal and interest. Other
financial assets are measured at FVTPL.

Solely Payments of Principal and Interest Test

The Bank assesses the contractual terms of financial assets to confirm they meet this test. Principal is defined
as the fair value of the financial asset at initial recognition and may change over the life of the asset if there are
repayments of principal or amortisation of premium/discount. The interest for a financial instrument reflects the
credit risk, duration and currency of the asset. Contractual terms that give rise to volatility in contractual cash
flows require the instrument to be measured at FVPL. The Bank had no such financial instruments.

Impairment Basis

IFRS 9 requires a forward-looking expected credit loss methodology. All financial assets not held at FYPL and°*
any undrawn commitments are assessed for an allowance for expected credit loss.

The expected credit-loss (“ECL”) is calculated as the probability of default (“PD”) applied to a forecast exposure
at default (“EAD”) multiplied by the Loss Given Default (“LGD”), which is the inverse of the anticipated recovery
rate, to calculate the expected credit exposure (“ECE”). The ECE is then discounted by the effective interest
rate (“EIR”) to calculate the ECL.

Expected Credit Loss Methodology
For a financial instrument ECL is calculated as the Expected Credit Exposure (“ECE”) discounted by EIR.

The ECE is calculated for the following exposures:
e Financings and debt instruments not held at FVPL

Amounts due from financial institutions and customers

Cash and balances with banks

e Other assets representing amounts held in advance of payments to creditors

e Off-balance sheet credit exposures such as undrawn committed facilities, letters of credit and
guarantees. (The Bank has no undrawn commitments as at year end).

Financings subject to the calculation of ECE are allocated into three stages.
Stage1: This is the stage allocated to the exposure when recorded. At the time of recording an ECE is calculated

based on a one-year probability of default. This continues until derecognised or the exposure moves to stage 2
or 3.

Stage 2: Where a credit exposure has experienced a significant increase in credit risk since origination the Bank
allocates the exposure to Stage 2. The PDs and LGDs are recalculated over the contractual maturity of the

Stage 3: Credit exposures considered impaired are transferred to stage 3. The PD for these exposures is fixed

(j) Corporation Tax

Corporation tax on the profit or loss for the year comprises current tax and deferred tax. Corporation tax is
recognised in profit or loss except to the extent that it relates to items recognised directly in other comprehensive
income in which case it is recognised in other comprehensive income.

Current tax is the tax expected to be payable on the taxable profit for the year, calculated using rates applicable
at the Statement of Financial Position date, and any adjustment to tax payable in respect of previous years.

Deferred tax is accounted for using the liability method in respect of temporary differences arising from
differences between the carrying amount of assets and liabilities in the Financial Statements and the
corresponding tax basis used in the computation of taxable profit.

In principle, deferred tax liabilities are recognised for all taxable temporary differences and deferred tax assets
are recognised to the extent that it is probable that taxable profits will be available against which deductible
temporary differences can be utilised.

Deferred tax is calculated at the rates substantively enacted at the time of signing of the accounts. Deferred tax
is charged or credited in the Statement of Comprehensive Income, except when it relates to items credited or
charged directly to other comprehensive income, in which case the deferred tax is also dealt with in other
comprehensive income. ,

The directors believe that the judgements made in determining the Bank’s tax liabilities are reasonable and
appropriate; however actual experience may differ and materially affect future tax charges.

Critical judgements in applying the Bank’s accounting policies

The following are the critical judgments, apart from those involving estimations (which are dealt with separately

most significant effect on the amounts recognised in the financial statements:

Significant increase of credit risk - As explained in note 2(h), ECL is measured as an allowance equal
to 12-month ECL for stage 1 assets or lifetime ECL for stage 2 or stage 3. An asset moves to stage 2
when its credit risk has increased significantly since initial recognition. In assessing whether the credit
risk of an asset has significantly increased the Bank considers qualitative and quantitative reasonable
and supportable forward-looking information.

Provision for credit losses - The bank recognised provisions for credit gains of €120k (2024: €5,513k
losses). Note 12 to the financial statements provides quantitative measurement of provisions for credit
losses. The bank uses historical and forward-looking data from external rating agencies to calculate
specific components of expected credit losses, probability of default and loss given default. The Bank
sources this data externally as there is no sufficient historical internal data to use. Therefore, there is
considerable judgement about the appropriateness and relevance of this data. The judgments are around
the reliability and relevance of the data to the Bank’s specific risk assets. The bank mainly uses external
and publicly available credit ratings to rate each obligor. When an exposure is not externally rated, the
Bank uses its internal rating model to determine a credit rating for each obligor. The credit rating system
and movements therein, determine the staging classification of each exposure in the expected credit loss
model.

The internal rating model uses a range of financial and non-financial inputs. And assessing the credit
grade gradings and the impact of the current macro challenges in the market the bank has considered a
number of qualitative and quantitative factors.

Deferred tax asset - The management of the Bank has to make judgements in respect of tax assets to
assess the likelihood that such tax differences may be realised in future years. Following the imposition
of US sanctions against the Bank in November 2018 and in the view of the future income forecast
(including uncertainty on Bank's ability to lend new loans) the Bank has decided not to recognise a
deferred tax asset arising from tax losses as set out in Note 18.

Impairment losses on financial assets - Under IFRS 9, the Bank uses judgements in relation to ECL
based upon the PD of the counterparty, the LGD of the exposure and the expected exposure amount.
The PD is based upon the Bank's internal rating. Where an external rating is available the Bank will map
this to an internal rating. Where there is not an available external rating for the counterparty the Bank will
use its judgement based upon factors such as the external weighting of the relevant sovereign and
counterparty type. The LGD is determined by the type of exposure, counterparty type and external rating
which will produce an appropriate LGD. The Bank has no off-balance sheet balances at the year end
and the expected exposure amount is based upon drawn balances. The relevant PD, LGD and exposure
amount was be used to calculate the ECL.

26. Operational risk management

Operational risk is the risk that the Bank will incur direct or indirect losses resulting from inadequate or failed
internal processes, people and systems, or from external events.

The Bank has an Operational Risk Management Policy that is reviewed annually, which includes a framework
to identify, monitor, measure and where possible, to mitigate the operational risks. Any changes to the
-Operational Risk Policy require Board approval. Regulatory, reputational and legal risks are mitigated through
procedures which are subject to operational risk reporting.

Operational risk exposures are “owned” by individual departments and monitored by the Risk Department.

A monthly report of key indicators and other identified risks forms the basis of a monthly report to the Executive

Management Committee. The operational risk reporting is proportional to the volume and scope of business
activities that has occurred during the year.
