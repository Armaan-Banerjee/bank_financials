REPORT OF THE SHARIAH SUPERVISORY BOARD

TO THE SHAREHOLDERS OF KUWAIT FINANCE HOUSE PLC

As-salaamu A’laikum Wa Rahmatuallahi Wa Barakatuh.

In compliance with our terms of appointment with Kuwait Finance House PLC (“the Bank”), we, the Shariah Supervisory
Board (“the SSB”), are required to submit the following Shariah compliance report to you annually.

The ultimate parent company of the Bank, Kuwait Finance House K.S.C.P, acquired Ahli United Bank B.S.C.(c), the
Bank’s parent company, on 2 October 2022. As a result, the Bank converted to an Islamic Bank on 22 August 2024. This
report to you therefore relates to the period from 22 August 2024 to 31 December 2024 (the “Relevant Period”).

We have reviewed all material transaction documents and contracts signed with third parties and have conducted an
overall review to form an independent opinion that the Bank has complied with Shariah rules and principles and with the

specific pronouncements, rulings and guidelines issued by us.

Please note that the management is responsible for ensuring that the Bank conducts its business in accordance with Islamic
Shariah rules and principles and guidance provided by the Shariah Supervisory Board. It is our responsibility to form an
independent opinion and report to you, based on our review of the operations of the Bank.

Conversion of the Bank
During and prior to the conversion of Kuwait Finance House PLC (formerly Ahli United Bank (UK) PLC) we reviewed

the memo relating to the conversion of non-compliant assets, structure for the transfer of loans to the Special Purpose
Vehicle (SPV) and the associated documentations. The SSB is satisfied that they meet the Shariah requirements for the
purpose of conversion of non-compliant assets. Any gains that contravene Islamic Shariah have been addressed in

accordance with the decisions of the Shariah Supervisory Board

Shariah Supervision
The SSB has supervised the Bank’s operations to the extent it is relevant to Shariah compliance and carried out its role in

directing the Bank to comply with Islamic Shariah principles and the SSB’s Shariah pronouncements.

Policies & Procedures
As a newly converted Shariah compliant Bank, it was necessary to review and amend the existing relevant policies and

procedures to make sure that they comply with the principles of Shariah. The SSB members confirm that with the
assistance of external and internal Shariah advisors, these have been amended to comply with the Islamic Shariah

principles.

Bank’s Contracts
Since the conversion, the Bank has entered into various contracts and financing agreements. These include obtaining

services from third parties to efficiently manage the Bank and providing financing through Ijarah and Murabaha to clients
in a Shariah-compliant manner to generate an income. The SSB has reviewed the contracts and agreements presented
during the Relevant Period and conveyed their pronouncements which the Bank implemented and, where necessary,
amended the relevant documents to comply with Shariah principles.

Shariah Audit
The SSB has carried out Shariah Audits of the Bank’s business activities during the Relevant Period. The Shariah audits

included:
(i) a review, on a sample basis, of some transaction documents executed by various departments of the Bank; and

(ii) an audit of all business-related expenses incurred by employees of the Bank.

In all material respects, the SSB found the operation of the Bank to be following the principles of Islamic Shariah and
thanks the senior management for their efforts in maintaining the Shariah compliant status of the Bank.

Balance Sheet
The SSB has perused the Bank’s Balance Sheet, the attached financial statements therewith and complementary notes

thereto. The SSB indicates that the Balance Sheet is within limits of information presented by the Bank’s management
representing the Bank’s Assets and Liabilities. One notable difference is the reporting of assets and liabilities prior to the
conversion and Islamic assets and liabilities after the conversion.

Zakat
Zakat is calculated on completion of a full financial year for Islamic banks, as the Relevant Period is less than a year, the

SSB concluded that the Shareholders should calculate their own Zakat based on their personal wealth.

CLIMATE CHANGE RISK (continued)

Climate risk manifests through, and has the potential to impact, the Bank’s existing primary financial and non-financial
risks. For example, physical and transition risk, the impairment of asset values may impact the creditworthiness of the
Bank’s customers and result in currently profitable businesses deteriorating over the term of agreed facilities.

The Bank considers climate-related risks resulting from the following drivers:

* Physical risks — changes in climate or weather patterns which are acute, event driven (e.g. flood impacts on the
underlying real estate collateral in relation to the Bank’s financing portfolio), or chronic, longer-term shifts (e.g.
rising sea levels);

"Transition risks — associated with the move towards a lower carbon economy, e.g. changes to policy, legislation and
regulation, technology, market, reputational and changes to customer preferences.

The ability to identify, measure and manage the risks associated with climate change is integral to our Enterprise Risk
Management Framework (ERMF). This is consistent with other key risks that we manage. However, the time horizon
in which climate risk will present itself may be a significantly longer time horizon than we have previously experienced
compared to other risks considered by the Bank.

Climate risk includes the risk of financial loss associated with a changing climate and the related legislative and societal
shifts that are taking place in response to that change. We are committed to understanding and assessing the risks
associated with climate change and the impact on the Bank’s financial results. In preparing the financial statements, we
have considered the impact of climate-related risks on our financial position and performance. While the effects of climate
change represent a source of uncertainty, there has not been a material impact on our financial judgements and estimates
due to the physical and transition climate-related risks in the short to medium term.

Governance
The Bank’s governance structure provides clear oversight and ownership of the Bank’s management of climate risk at

Board and Executive levels. The Board has overall oversight of all Climate change/Environmental, Social and
Governance (ESG) matters and is engaged on a regular basis in our climate risk agenda, receiving ongoing training to
develop an understanding of climate risk.

The Bank’s Risk Committee is responsible for the oversight of climate-related risks, including alignment with regulatory
expectations, embedding into the Bank’s Risk Management Framework and implications for risk appetite.

The Risk Committee supports the Bank’s Board in fulfilling its risk governance and oversight roles and responsibilities.
It is responsible for oversight of the risks the business faces from climate change, including the transition to net zero. The
Bank receives standalone updates to monitor progress and assess the evolving risk management capabilities and approach.

Climate risk is considered on a monthly basis in the Bank’s risk reporting with qualitative updates provided, while regular
updates are presented quarterly to the Board Committee regarding the Bank’s climate risk management activities and key

developments.

These more detailed updates are intended to ensure the Board has oversight of:

* Overall climate risk programme management across the Bank, including climate scenario analysis and stress testing;
*  Climate-related risk appetite development and approval at both a Business and individual sector level;

" Metrics associated with the climate risk programme and risk appetite.

OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE RISK

The risk that the Bank fails to design resilience into business operations, underlying infrastructure, and controls (people,
process, technical) to withstand external or internal events that could impact the continuity of operations or alternatively
the failure to respond to events in a way which meets stakeholder expectations and needs when the continuity of operations

is compromised.

Effective operational resilience ensures the Bank designs resilience into its systems, is able to withstand and/or recover
from a significant unexpected event occurring and can continue to provide services to its customers. A significant outage
could result in customers being unable to access accounts or conduct transactions, which as well as presenting significant
reputational risk for the Bank would negatively impact the Bank’s purpose. Operational resilience is also an area of
continued regulatory and industry focus, similar in importance to financial resilience.

Failure to manage operational resilience effectively could impact the following other risk categories:

«Regulatory compliance - non-compliance with new/existing operational resilience regulations, for example, through
failure to identify emerging regulation or not embedding regulatory requirements within the Bank’s policies,
processes and procedures or identify further future emerging regulation;

= Operational risk - being unable to safely provide customers with business services;

Conduct risk - an operational resilience failure may render the Bank liable to fines from the FCA for poor conduct.

Key mitigating actions

The annual self-assessment details the Bank’s approach to meeting the regulatory operational resilience requirements,
which includes the identification of important business services and its respective impact tolerances and testing thereon.

Our application of materiality

We apply the concept of materiality in planning and performing the audit, in evaluating the effect of identified
misstatements on the audit and in forming our audit opinion.

- The magnitude of an omission or misstatement that, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected
to influence the economic decisions of the users of the financial statements. Materiality provides a basis for

determining the nature and extent of our audit procedures.

We determined materiality for the Group to be $2.6m (2023: $3.1m), which is five per cent (2023: five per cent) of
adjusted profit before tax. This reflects statutory profit before tax adjusted for certain non-recurring items. We believe
that adjusted profit before tax provides us with the most appropriate and relevant measure of the performance of the
Group for the users of the consolidated financial statements. This measure has not changed from the prior year.

During the course of our audit, we reassessed initial materiality and recalculated it based on year-end profit before
tax, which was lower than forecast at the date of our planning. .

Performance materiatity
The application of materiality at the individual account or balance level. It is set at an amount to reduce to an

appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements exceeds
materiality.

On the basis of our risk assessments, together with our assessment of the Group's overall control environment, our
judgement was that performance materiality was 50% (2023: 75%) of our planning materiality, namely $1.3m (2023:
$2.3m). We have set performance materiality at a lower percentage compared to the previous year, keeping in view
the Bank's conversion to an Islamic bank.

Reporting threshold
An amount below which identified misstatements are considered as being clearly trivial.

We agreed with the Audit Committee that we would report to them all uncorrected audit differences in excess of
$0.1m (2023: $0.2m), which is set at 5% of planning materiality, as well as differences below that threshold that, in

our view, warranted reporting on qualitative grounds.

We evaluate any uncorrected misstatements against both the quantitative measures of materiality discussed above
and in light of other relevant qualitative considerations in forming our opinion.

The Group classifies an investment as "joint venture" when it is a party to a joint arrangement whereby the parties that

The Group uses judgement in determining which entities are structured entities requiring consolidation. If the voting or
similar rights are not the dominant factor in deciding who controls the entity and such voting rights relate to the

identifies such entities as structured entities. After determining whether an entity is a structured entity, the Company

The Group sets up and uses structured entities to facilitate the conversion of legacy conventional loan portfolios and

B) FOREIGN CURRENCY TRANSLATION: TRANSACTIONS AND BALANCES

Transactions in foreign currencies are initially recorded in the functional currency at the rate of exchange prevailing on
the date of the transaction, Monetary assets and liabilities denominated in foreign currencies are translated into the
functional currency at the rate of exchange ruling at the balance sheet date. Any resulting exchange differences are
included in investment income in the consolidated statement of income. Non-monetary assets and liabilities that are
measured at historical cost in a foreign currency are translated using the exchange rates as at the dates of the initial
transactions. Non-monetary assets categorised as fair value through profit or loss and measured at fair value in a foreign

exchange rates when the item is recognised in the income statement.

The classification of financial instruments at initial recognition depends on their contractual terms and the business model

for managing the instruments. Financial instruments are initially measured at their fair value and, except in the case of
financial assets and financial liabilities recorded at FVTPL, transaction costs are added to, or subtracted from, this amount.

E) DATE OF RECOGNITION
All "regular way" purchases and sales of financial assets are recognised on the settlement date, i.e. the date that the Group

accounts for any changes in the fair value of the asset to be received during the period between the trade date and the
settlement date in the same way as it accounts for the acquired asset. The change in value is not recognised for assets

carried at amortised cost.

periodic repayments until maturity. Profit refers to return payable on the outstanding principal at a specific rate of profit

defined within the terms of the contract.

K) IMPAIRMENT OF FINANCIAL ASSETS

The ECL allowance is based on the credit losses expected to arise over the life of the asset (the lifetime expected credit
loss or “LTECL”), unless there has been no significant increase in credit risk since origination, in which case, the
allowance is based on the 12 months’ expected credit loss (“12mECL”). The Bank’s policies for determining if there has

been a significant increase in credit risk are set out in Note 28.

The 12mECL is the portion of LTECLs that represent the ECLs that result from default events on AC assets that are
possible within the 12 months after the reporting date.

ii) ECL calculation
The Bank calculates ECLs based on three probability-weighted scenarios to measure the expected cash shortfalls,

discounted at an approximation to the original effective profit rate (EPR). A cash shortfall is the difference between the

cash flows that are due to the Bank in accordance with the contract and the cash flows that the Bank expects to receive.

The mechanics of the ECL calculations are outlined below and the key elements are as follows:

« EAD - The Exposure at Default is an estimate of the exposure at a future default date, taking into account expected
changes in the exposure after the reporting date, including repayments of principal and profit, whether scheduled by
contract or otherwise, expected drawdowns on committed facilities, and accrued profit from missed payments;

* PD - The Probability of Default is an estimate of the likelihood of default over a given time horizon. A default may
only happen at a certain time over the assessed period, if the facility has not been previously derecognised and is still
in the portfolio; and

* LGD - The Loss Given Default, expressed as a percentage, is an estimate of the loss arising in the case where a
default occurs at a given time. It is based on the difference between the contractual cash flows due and those that the
lender would expect to receive, including from the realisation of any collateral. The recovery rate is defined as the

value of a security when it emerges from default or bankruptcy.

For the Bank's impairment model, the PD and LGD calibration tables are sourced from either:

= A recognised rating agency (S&P and Moody’s) where comparable asset data is available. Non-trading investments
and deposits with Banks are included in this category; and

* The Bank's historical default data for financing receivables, taking into consideration the underlying collateral

available.

When estimating the ECLs, the Bank considers three scenarios (a base case, an upside {'good'} and a downside {*bad’}).

Each of these is associated with different PDs and LGDs.

The ECL calculation is as follows : ECL = [EAD x PD x LGD x discounted by an approximation to the original EPR].

= Stage 1 : The ECL calculation is referenced to the appropriate rates and forecast for the 12 month period following
the reporting date;

« Stage 2: The ECL equivalent calculation is referenced to the lifetime of the underlying AC asset,

« Stage 3 : A specific provision is calculated for those exposures where the discounted recovery values (including
holding costs) of the underlying collateral are lower than the Bank's gross exposure; and

« The current base/good/bad scenario split for the Bank's financing receivables exposure remains at 20%/5%/75% split,
which the Bank considers reflects the current macroeconomic environment.

For the AC assets where collateral has been pledged, the associated collateral is discounted and thereon applied to the
initial ECL to arrive at a final reported ECL. In accordance with the Bank's credit and investment policy (see note 28)
the Bank monitors the market value of collateral and may request additional collateral when necessary in accordance with

the underlying contractual agreement.

iii) Forward looking information
In its ECL models, the Bank relies on a broad range of forward looking information as the inputs. Models for PDs and

LGDs used for calculating the ECL may not always capture all characteristics of the market at the reporting date, as
additional adjustments to the model include economic market data in relation to:

= House price indices;

L) HEDGE ACCOUNTING
The Bank enters into hedging instruments including foreign exchange forwards, profit rate swaps and forward commodity

contracts (“Islamic hedging financial instrument”) to manage exposures to profit rates and foreign currency risks,
including exposures arising from forecast transactions.

The Islamic currency swaps and forward commodity contracts are based on a Wa'ad (promise) structure between two
parties to buy a specified Shariah compliant commodity at an agreed price on the relevant date in future. It is a conditional
promise to purchase a commodity through unilateral purchase undertaking.

For Islamic profit rate swaps, counterparties generally exchange fixed and floating rate profit payments based on a
notional value in a single currency through series of transactions to buy a specified Shariah compliant commodity at an
agreed price on the relevant date in future based on Wa'ad (promise) structure. The profit rate swaps, forward foreign

exchange and forward commodity contracts are being used for hedging purpose.
Islamic derivatives were entered into in 2024, and in 2023 the derivatives were conventional.

In order to manage particular risks, the Bank applies hedge accounting for transactions which meet the specified criteria.
Derivative financial instruments held for hedging purposes are stated at fair value.

At inception of the hedge relationship, the Bank formally documents the relationship between the hedged item and the
hedging instrument, including the nature of the risk, management objectives and strategy for undertaking the hedge. The
Bank performs statistical regression analysis to assess the effectiveness of the hedging relationship, which forms part of
the Bank's documentation. The Bank has adopted IFRS 9 for applying the requirements of hedge accounting.

For the purposes of hedge accounting, hedges are classified into two categories: (i) fair value hedges which hedge the
exposure to changes in the fair value of a recognised asset or liability; and (ii) cash flow hedges which hedge exposure to
variability in cash flows that is attributable to a particular risk associated with a recognised asset-or liability, or a profit

transaction,

i) Fair value hedges

For fair value hedges which meet the conditions for hedge accounting, any gain or loss from re-measuring the hedging
instrument at fair value is recognised immediately in the consolidated statement of income. The hedged item is adjusted
for fair value changes relating to the risk being hedged and the difference is recognised in the consolidated income

statement.

If the hedging instrument expires or is sold, terminated or exercised, or where the hedge no longer meets the criteria for
hedge accounting, the hedge relationship is terminated. For hedged items recorded at amortised cost, the difference
between the carrying value of the hedged item on termination and the value at which it would have been carried without
being hedged is amortised over the remaining term of the original hedge. If the hedged item is derecognised, the
unamortised fair value adjustment is recognised immediately in the consolidated income statement.

In accordance with its hedging strategy, the Bank matches the principal of the hedging instruments to the principal of the
hedged items, including prepayment expectations. The Bank uses pay fixed/receive floating profit rate swaps to hedge
its fixed rate non-trading investments and financing receivables and pay floating/receive fixed profit rate swaps to hedge

its fixed rate liabilities.

The possible sources of ineffectiveness for cash flow hedges are generally the same as those for fair value hedges,
described above. However, for cash flow hedges, prepayment risk is less relevant, and the causes of hedging
ineffectiveness arise from the changes in the timing and the amount of forecast future cash flows.

For hedges which do not qualify for hedge accounting, any gains or losses arising from changes in the fair value of the
hedging instrument are taken directly to the consolidated income statement.

Hedge accounting is discontinued when the hedging instrument expires or is sold, terminated or exercised, or no longer
qualifies for hedge accounting. In the case of cash flow hedges, the cumulative gain or loss on the hedging instrument
recognised in equity remains in equity until the forecasted transaction occurs, unless the hedged transaction is no longer
expected to occur, in which case the net cumulative gain or loss recognised in equity is transferred to the consolidated

income statement.

25. DERIVATIVE FINANCIAL INSTRUMENTS

In the ordinary course of business the Bank enters into various types of transactions that involve derivative financial
instruments. A derivative financial instrument is a financial contract between two parties where its value changes in
response to the change in a specified profit rate, foreign exchange rate or other variable, to manage the risks, there is little
or no initial net investment, and settlement is based in the future.

Derivative financial instruments include financial forwards, profit rate swaps and currency swaps, which create rights and
obligations that have the effect of transferring between the parties of the instrument one or more of the financial risks
inherent in an underlying primary financial instrument. On inception, a derivative financial instrument gives one party a
contractual right to exchange financial assets or financial liabilities with another party under conditions that are potentially
favourable, or a contractual obligation to exchange financial assets or financial liabilities with another party under
conditions that are potentially unfavourable. However, they generally do not result in a transfer of the underlying primary
financial instrument on inception of the contract, nor does such a transfer necessarily take place on maturity of the contract.

Some instruments embody both a right and an obligation to make an exchange. Because the terms of the exchange are
determined on inception of the derivative instruments, as prices in financial markets change those terms may become either

favourable or unfavourable.

The Bank uses forward foreign exchange and swap contracts as part of its asset and liability management process to hedge
underlying on-balance sheet positions. The use of derivative financial instruments is controlled through the Bank's Risk
Management department which is an independent area of the Bank. The Risk Management department is responsible for
ensuring that all profit and exchange rate risk is undertaken within the policy guidelines set by the Board and the Asset and
Liability Committee.

Islamic forward agreements (Wa'ad)

The Bank enters into forward foreign exchange agreements (Wa'ad) to mitigate foreign currency risk. A Wa'ad is a
unilateral undertaking by one party in favor of the second party where the exercise of undertaking is dependent on the
movement in the price of one or more underlying financial instruments, reference rate or index in accordance with Islamic
Shariah. The notional amount, disclosed gross, is the amount of a Wa'ad’s underlying asset/liability and is the basis upon
which changes in the value are measured. The notional amounts indicate the volume of transactions outstanding at the year-
end and are neither indicative of the market risk nor credit risk.

Profit rate swaps .
Profit rate swaps are contractual agreements between two parties and may involve exchange of profit or exchange of both

principal and profit for a fixed period of time based on contractual terms. The notional amounts indicate the volume of
transactions outstanding at the period-end and are neither indicative of the market risk nor credit risk. All of the Banks’s
profit rate swaps are held for hedging.

Profit rate risk

At 3 December 2024, the Bank had profit rate swap contracts in place whereby the Bank pays a fixed rate against underlying assets
(receives for underlying deposits) and thereon receives a variable rate of retum (pays for deposits). The swaps are being used to hedge the
exposure to changes in the fair value of its fixed rate Sukuks, financing receivables and customer deposits.
Repricing risk arises from timing differences in the maturity of fixed rate assets and liabilities in comparison to the repricing floating rate
during the same period. Such repricing mismatches expose returns to abnormal fluctuations where there are unforeseen profit rates
movements. The Bank therefore manages such profit rate risk by entering into profit rate swap contracts on a micro basis, where on an
individual transaction level the critical terms of both the hedge item and hedging instrament match, including notional amount, maturity
date, fixed rate and coupon frequency.
The Bank's Risk management department monitor such risk within the policy framework of ‘profit rate risk in the banking book' and the
results are reported to the Bank's Asset and Liability Committee (ALCO) on a monthly basis. In addition, any hedge ineffectiveness, both
cumulative life to date and current year, are also reported on a monthly basis to the Bank’s ALCO. On the basis of the Bank's micro
hedging strategy, ineffectiveness is not considered to materially impact the hedging relationship, and as at 31 December 2024 represented
0.07% of gross profit rate swap notional.

28 CREDIT RISK

Credit Risk is the risk arising from a counterparty's failure to discharge on time and in full as contracted and cause the other party to
incur a financial loss. Credit Risk arises principally from financing but also from other transactions involving on and off balance sheet
instruments. The Risk Committee in conjunction with the Risk Management department have the responsibility for developing and
implementing policies to ensure that all exposures are properly pre-approved, measured and controlled. With the exception of certain
small retail financing receivables, all credits are independently pre-approved including, for credits above a set limit, by the Parent
Undertaking's Executive Committee. Credit proposals are put through a comprehensive risk assessment which examines the customer’s
financial condition and trading performance, nature of the business, quality of management and market position. In addition, an
internal risk rating model scores these quantitative and qualitative factors. The credit approval decision is then made and terms and
conditions set. Exposure limits are based on the aggregate exposure to the counterparty. All credit exposures are reviewed at least
annually. Measurement of credits includes geographic, product, market and individual counterparty concentration.

All credit exposures are checked daily against approved limits, independently of each business unit, and are reported to senior
management.

The Bank's first priority when making real estate financing receivables is to establish the obligor’s capacity to pay and not rely
principally on security/collateral. Financing receivables are analysed into the following categories:

- Retail : principally individual customers’ consumer financing receivables, residential financing receivables.

- Corporate : principally financing receivables and other credit facilities for corporate and institutional customers. Also included are

credit facilities to high net worth individuals.

The majority of financing receivables are secured on assets, for which the main geographical credit exposure is to the UK market.
Acceptable forms of collateral are defined within the credit risk framework and conservative valuation parameters are also pre-set and
regularly reviewed to reflect any changes in market conditions. Security structures and legal covenants are also subject to regular
review to ensure that they continue to fulfil their intended purpose and remain in line with market practice. Guidelines are in place
regarding the acceptability of types of collateral and valuation parameters.
The principal coilateral types are as follows:

- financing receivables over residential properties,

- charges over business assets such as premises, plant, stock and receivables,

- charges over commercial properties being financed; and

- charges over financial instruments, such as debt securities and equities.

The Bank monitors the market value of collateral, periodically by type of asset using a variety of techniques including indexation.
Where appropriate, and in accordance with the underlying agreement, the Bank can request additional collateral.

In relation to financing receivable exposures, nil possession orders were obtained and enforced during 2024 (2023: nif).

It is the Bank's policy to maintain consistent intemal risk ratings across the credit portfolio. The credit quality ‘of the portfolio of
financing receivables, debt instruments af amortised cost and deposits with banks, that are neither past due nor impaired can be
assessed by reference to the AUB Group's internal credit rating system. This facilitates focused portfolio management of the inherent
risk across all lines of business.

The four credit quality internal risk ratings given can be equated to the following

30 MARKET RISK

Market risk is the risk of potential financial loss that may arise from adverse changes in the value of financial

instruments or portfolios of financial instruments due to movements in profit rates, foreign exchange rates or other

prices and volatilities. This risk arises from asset-liability mismatches, changes that occur in the yield curve, foreign
exchange rates and changes in volatilities/implied volatilities in the market value of derivatives. The Bank classifies
exposures to market risk into either trading or non-trading portfolios. Given the Bank's low risk strategy, aggregate
market risk levels are considered low. The Bank utilises Value-at-Risk (VaR) models to assist in estimating potential
losses that may arise from adverse market movements in addition to non-quantitative risk management techniques. The
market risk for the trading portfolio is managed and monitored on a VaR methodology which reflects the inter-
dependency between risk variables. Non-trading portfolios are managed and monitored using stop loss limits and other
sensitivity analyses.

VaR is a technique which estimates the potential losses that could occur on risk positions taken due to movements in
market rates and prices over a specified time horizon and to a given level of confidence. VaR is calculated on a
historical simulation basis, using one-day movements in market rates and prices, a 99% confidence level and taking
into account the actual correlations observed historically between different markets and rates. The one-day movement
in market prices is calculated by reference to market data from a 260 trading day history. VaR should be viewed in the
context of the limitations of the methodology used.

- The use of a one-day holding period assumes that all positions can be liquidated or hedged in one day; this does not
fully capture the market risk arising from times of illiquidity, when one-day liquidation or hedging may not be
possible.

- The use of a 99% confidence measure does not take account of any losses that might occur beyond this level of
confidence.

- The use of historical data as a proxy for estimating future events may not include all potential events, particularly
those that are extreme in nature.

- Correlations in the future may change from those correlations observed in the past.

- VaR is calculated at the close of business with intra-day exposures not being subject to intra-day VaR calculations.

31 LIQUIDITY RISK

Liquidity risk is the risk that the Bank does not have sufficient financial resources to meet its obligations as they fall
due, or will have to do so at an excessive cost. This risk arises from mismatches in the timing of cash flows. Funding
risk arises when the necessary liquidity to fund illiquid asset positions cannot be obtained at the expected terms and
when required.

The Bank maintains a stable funding base comprising core retail and corporate customer deposits and institutional
balances, augmented by wholesale funding and portfolios of highly liquid assets which are diversified by currency
and maturity, in order to enable the Bank to respond quickly to any unforeseen liquidity requirements.

Treasury limits are set by the Asset and Liability Committee. Specifically the Asset and Liability Committee and the
Bank Treasurer are responsible for:
~ projecting cash flows by major currency under various stress scenarios and considering the level of liquid

35 EMPLOYEE BENEFITS

Within the UK, the Bank operates a defined contribution pension scheme (“the Scheme”) which is open to current employees. Contributions to the
Scheme are charged directly to the statement of income.

The Bank also operates a funded defined benefit scheme (“the Fund”) for its employees who joined prior to | March 2001. The Fund was a final
salary scheme which became closed to future accrual with effect from 31 March 2010. The weighted average duration of the expected benefit

payments from the Fund is around 12 years.

The Fund is registered with HMRC for tax purposes, is operated separately from the Bank and is managed by a set of Trustees. The Trustees are
responsible for the payment of benefits and the management of the scheme’s assets which are held in a separate trustee administered fund. The
Fund is subject to UK regulations, which require the Bank and the Trustees to agree a funding strategy and contributions schedule.

In 2023, the Trustees of the Fund changed the Fund’s investment strategy and invested the Fund’s assets in a buy-in insurance policy ~ an
insurance policy which pays an income to the Fund which matches the pensions to be paid to members. While the Bank remains legally
responsible for supporting the Fund, investing in this policy has removed the Bank’s exposure to investment and funding risks in the Fund.

Contributions are set based on funding valuations carried out every three years. As the insurance policy fully covers benefits payable from the
Fund and the premium paid could be met entirely from the Fund’s existing assets, the Bank and the Trustees agreed that the Bank could cease
paying deficit contributions with effect from 15 June 2023. As a result, the Bank paid no deficit contributions into the Fund during 2024 (2023

actual: $3 million / £2.4 million).

Virgin Media v NTL Pension Trustees II Limited (and others)

On 16 June 2023, the High Court passed judgment in relation to Virgin Media v NTL Pension Trustees II Limited (and others). This judgment
confirmed that certain rules of a contracted-out scheme, cannot be altered without the statutory actuarial confirmation (“Section 37 confirmation”)
having been obtained and that non-compliant alterations would retrospectively become void. The decision was upheld on appeal in July 2024.

The Bank, in conjunction with the Trustees of the Pension Fund and their legal advisers, have undertaken a review of the amendments made to the
Plan during the relevant period. That review has indicated that there were two deeds that would require actuarial confirmation and a further three
deeds where the legal advice is that the deed amendments would not require actuarial confirmation, but a court might rule that written actuarial
confirmation is required. For the two identified deeds the Trustees are actively seeking copies of the relevant written actuarial confirmation, and
until these are located, the risk remains the deeds could be deemed void. Further, any valuation impact cannot be quantified.

Based on the review and the information available, the Bank has concluded that it is not necessary to make any adjustments at the current time as
a result of the Virgin Media case.

GMP equalisation

The Trustees have implemented equalised benefits for female members of the Fund (male members’ benefits did not require an uplift) and these
benefits are covered by the insurance policy. In addition, the Trustees are in the process of paying top up transfers for a small number of former
members of the Fund, with the majority of these top ups were paid over 2023 and 2024. The remaining cost of equalising benefits in respect of
deceased members is known and has been allowed for in the [AS19 figures as at 31 December 2024.

FINANCIAL ASSUMPTIONS - SUMMARY
The key assumptions at 31 December 2024 have been derived consistently with those adopted at 31 December 2023. The changes in i pti

therefore reflect only changes in underlying financial conditions.

Key financial assumptions
Assumptions are set by reference to yield curves at the relevant accounting dates and reflect the Fund's projected benefit payment profile. The above assumptions

are the single equivalent assumptions that give the same value of the LAS 19 liabilities as if the figures were calculated using the yield curves described below.

Discount rate:

IAS 19 requires that the discount rate is set based on yields of high quality corporate bonds at the balance sheet date.

We have constructed a yield curve from high quality (AA rated) corporate bonds. To derive the discount rate, the yield curve is weighted according to scheme cash
flows, which have an average duration of about 12 years (31 December 2023: duration of 13 years). The approach used to set the discount rate is in line with the

“LCP Treasury Model”.

The yield curve used to set the discount rate has been constructed as follows:

* Data on corporate bond yields is obtained as at the ac: ing date;

* Bonds are included in the data set if they are classified as “AA” and “Corporate”, in line with the requirements of [ASI9;

* Bonds are classified as AA if they are rated AA by the index provider,

* Reasonableness checks are carried out on the data, in particular to ensure that we use bonds only where the prices supplied and yields are consistent;

* A yield curve is fitted to the data using least squares optimisation techniques, such that the forward rate at year 30 and above is equal to the average forward rate

over the 15 to 30 year period.

Inflation and pension increase assumptions:
IAS19 requires that assumptions for inflation are best estimate, based on market expectations at the balance sheet date.

Benefits from the Fund depend on both the RPI and CPI price indices, which are constructed differently. The approach to setting the RPI and CPI inflation

assumptions is unchanged from the previous year-end.

The assumption for future RPI inflation is derived from the breakeven measure implied by the difference between nominal yields on fixed interest gilts and real
yields on RPI linked gilts. The single equivalent break inflation rate, weighted by scheme cash flows, is 3.4% pa.

RPI inflation - The best estimate, market consistent assumption for RPI is derived by deducting an inflation risk premium from breakeven inflation. Consistent
with last year, we have allowed for an inflation nsk premium of 0.2% pa. Overall, this results in a single weighted average RPI assumption of 3.2% pa (reflecting
breakeven inflation of 3.4% pa less an average inflation risk premium of 0.2% pa).

CPI inflation - The ption for CPI inflation is set by deducting a “wedge” from the RPI inflation assumption, to reflect structural differences between the

indices. The CPI assumption is derived as follows:

« Pre 2030: a wedge of 1.0% is deducted.
* 2030 onwards: a wedge of 0.1%. This reflects the smal! expected differences between the CPIH index, which will be used to calculate RPI from 2030, and the CPI

index. We assume that CPIH inflation will be higher than CPI inflation because CPIH includes an allowance for owner occupied housing costs, which tend to
increase at a higher rate than the general price level.

Overall, this results in a single weighted average CPI assumption of 2.4% pa, reflecting RPI inflation of 3.2% pa less an average wedge of 0.8% pa,

Pension increases - In setting the assumed level of future pension increases, we have adjusted the relevant inflation ption to allow for the volatility of future
inflation and the maximum and minimum increases, For this purpose, we have used assumed volatility of 1.5% pa for both RPI and CPI inflation, This is the same

approach as was used at the previous year-end,

Operational risk

The Bank has adopted the Basic Indicator approach for operational risk. Under this approach, the regulatory capital requirement for operational
risk is calculated by applying a co-efficient of 15 per cent to the average gross income for the preceding three financial years.

Exposure to profit rate risk in the non-trading book

Details of the nature of this risk and of its measurement by the Bank are included in note 29 to the Financial Statements.

Leverage Ratio

CRD IV requires firms to calculate a non-risk based Leverage Ratio, to supplement risk-based capital requirements. The leverage ratio measures
the relationship between the Tier | capital resources of the organisation and its total assets (pre credit risk mitigation). The purpose of monitoring
and managing this metric is to enable regulators to constrain the build-up of excessive leverage. There is a current minimum requirement of

3.25%.

Remuneration
The Bank's remuneration policy is incorporated into its HR policy which is reviewed by the Board of Directors and the Compensation Committee

on an annual basis,

The total variable amount available is determined firstly by assessing the Bank's profitability compared to budget. The employee's variable
remuneration is based on the result of the annual performance appraisal process. The process assesses the financial and non financial attributes of
the employees awarding grades which result in a formula driven bonus. The resulting awards are then subject to a moderation review by senior
management before being presented to the Group Compensation Committee for further revision and approval,
