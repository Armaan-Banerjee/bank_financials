REPORT OF THE SHARIAH SUPERVISORY BOARD
TO THE SHAREHOLDERS OF KUWAIT FINANCE HOUSE PLC

As-salaamu A'laikum Wa Rahmatuallahi Wa Barakatuh.

In compliance with our terms of appointment with Kuwait Finance House PLC (“the Bank’), we,
the Shariah Supervisory Board (“the SSB”), are required to submit the following Shariah
compliance report to you annually.

We have reviewed all material transaction documents and contracts signed with third parties
and have conducted an overall review to form an independent opinion that the Bank has
complied with Shariah rules and principles and with the specific pronouncements, rulings and
guidelines issued by us taking into account the recent conversion of the bank, and its
operation in a minority Muslim county that faces a number of regulatory, legal and taxation
challenges.

Please note that the management is responsible for ensuring that the Bank conducts its
business in accordance with Islamic Shariah rules and principles and guidance provided by
the Shariah Supervisory Board. It is our responsibility to form an independent opinion and
report to you, based on our review of the operations of the Bank.

Conversion of the Bank

The SSB is satisfied that the Bank is making progress in converting the non-compliant assets
into a Shariah compliant asset by using the ljarah and Murabaha structures that the SSB has
approved. These meet the Shariah requirements for the purpose of conversion of non-
compliant assets. Any gains that contravene Islamic Shariah have been addressed by the
management in accordance with the decisions of the Shariah Supervisory Board

Bank of England Alternative Liquidity Facility (ALF)

During the year, the Bank has entered a Shariah compliant alternative liquidity facility based
on the principles of Wakala with the Bank of England. This allows the Bank to maintain high-
quality liquid assets that meet the Bank’s regulatory liquidity requirements in a Shariah
compliant way and is a notable achievement for the Bank.

Shariah Supervision

The SSB has supervised the Bank's operations to the extent it is relevant to Shariah
compliance and carried out its role in directing the Bank to comply with Islamic Shariah
principles and the SSB’s Shariah pronouncements.

Policies & Procedures

The SSB members confirm that with the assistance of internal head of Shariah compliance
officer, the relevant policies and procedure of the bank have been amended to comply with
the Islamic Shariah principles.

Bank’s Contracts

During the relevant period, the Bank has entered into various contracts and financing
agreements. These include obtaining services from third parties to efficiently manage the
Bank and providing financing through ljarah and Murabaha to clients in a Shariah-compliant
manner to generate an income. The SSB has, with the assistance of the head of Shariah
compliance officer, reviewed the material contracts and agreements presented during the
relevant period and conveyed their pronouncements which the Bank implemented and,
where necessary, amended the relevant documents to comply with Shariah principles.

PRINCIPAL RISKS

The principal business risks faced by the Bank in relation to the financial instruments recognised within the balance sheet

(detailed within notes 27 - 30) are summarised as follows:

* Credit Risk - The risk arising from a counterparty’s failure to discharge on time and in full as contracted and cause
the other party to incur a financial loss;

"Liquidity Risk — The risk that the Bank may be unable to meet its financial obligations as they fall due or may only
be able to do so at a materially higher cost, due to insufficient availability of liquid financial resources. This risk
arises from mismatches in the timing of cash flows. Funding risk arises when the necessary liquidity to fund illiquid
asset positions cannot be obtained at the expected terms and when required;

» Market Risk - The risk of potential financial loss that may arise from adverse changes in the value of financial
instruments, or portfolio of financial instruments, due to movements in external benchmark reference rates, foreign
exchange rates or other prices and volatilities. The hedging framework applied to manage specific market risks is
outlined in Note 24;

« Concentration Risk - The risk arising from the Bank having deposits with customers that are a material percentage
of total deposits.

For each of the aforementioned identified risks, the Bank has in place a management framework and policies to identify,
control and provide management oversight for such risks.

CLIMATE CHANGE RISK

The risk that the Bank experiences losses and/or reputational damage because of climate change, either directly or through
its customers. These losses may be realised from physical events, the required adaptation in transitioning to a lower
carbon economy or because of the responses to managing these changes.

The scale of the potential impact of climate related risks and opportunities and the time horizons over which these will
manifest will vary across the Bank’s business operations. The variability of impacts and the time horizons will be
dependent on several different factors, some of which are in the control of the organisation and some of which are not.

Climate risk manifests through, and has the potential to impact, the Bank’s existing primary financial and non-financial
risks. For example, physical and transition risk, the impairment of asset values may impact the creditworthiness of the
Bank’s customers and result in currently profitable businesses deteriorating over the term of agreed facilities.

CLIMATE CHANGE RISK (continued)

The Bank considers climate-related risks resulting from the following drivers:

« Physical risks - changes in climate or weather patterns which are acute, event driven (e.g. flood impacts on the
underlying real estate collateral in relation to the Bank’s financing portfolio), or chronic, longer-term shifts (e.g.
rising sea levels);

#" Transition risks — associated with the move towards a lower carbon economy, e.g. changes to policy, legislation and
regulation, technology, market, reputational and changes to customer preferences.

The ability to identify, measure and manage the risks associated with climate change is integral to our Enterprise Risk
Management Framework (ERMF). This is consistent with other key risks that we manage. However, the time horizon
in which climate risk will present itself may be a significantly longer time horizon than we have previously experienced
compared to other risks considered by the Bank.

Climate risk includes the risk of financial loss associated with a changing climate and the related legislative and societal
shifts that are taking place in response to that change. We are committed to understanding and assessing the risks
associated with climate change and the impact on the Bank’s financial results. In preparing the financial statements, we
have considered the impact of climate-related risks on our financial position and performance. While the effects of climate
change represent a source of uncertainty, there has not been a material impact on our financial judgements and estimates
due to the physical and transition climate-related risks in the short to medium term.

The Bank follows the Task force on Climate change-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) guidelines. Essentially the
four pillar points of:

Governance
The Bank’s Risk Committee is responsible for the oversight of climate-related risks, including alignment with regulatory

The Risk Committee is responsible for oversight of the risks the business faces from climate change. The Bank receives

to the financial risks from climate change.

OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE RISK

The risk that the Bank fails to design resilience into business operations, underlying infrastructure, and controls (people,
process, technical) to withstand external or internal events that could impact the continuity of operations or alternatively
the failure to respond to events in a way which meets stakeholder expectations and needs when the continuity of operations
is compromised.

Effective operational resilience ensures the Bank designs resilience into its systems, is able to withstand and/or recover
from a significant unexpected event occurring and can continue to provide services to its customers. A significant outage
could result in customers being unable to access accounts or conduct transactions, which as well as presenting significant
reputational risk for the Bank would negatively impact the Bank’s purpose. Operational resilience is also an area of
continued regulatory and industry focus, similar in importance to financial resilience.

Failure to manage operational resilience effectively could impact the following other risk categories:

* Regulatory compliance - non-compliance with new/existing operational resilience regulations, for example, through
failure to identify emerging regulation or not embedding regulatory requirements within the Bank’s policies,
processes and procedures or identify further future emerging regulation;

®* Operational risk - being unable to safely provide customers with business services;

* Conduct risk - an operational resilience failure may render the Bank liable to fines from the FCA for poor conduct.

Key mitigating actions

The annual self-assessment details the Bank’s approach to meeting the regulatory operational resilience requirements,
which includes the identification of important business services and its respective impact tolerances and testing thereon.
During 2025, the Bank undertook a live testing program to demonstrate its operational capabilities are in accordance with
documented expectations and took steps to address its key vulnerabilities which were identified as part of its self-
assessment in line with regulatory requirements.

1.1 BASIS OF CONSOLIDATION

The consolidated financial statements comprise the financial statements of the Bank and its subsidiaries (together known
as the 'Group’) as at and for the years ended 31 December 2025 and 2024. The financial statements of material subsidiaries
are prepared for the same reporting year as the Bank (unless stated otherwise within note 15), using consistent accounting
policies. Adjustments are made to bring into line any dissimilar accounting policies that may exist.

All material intra-group balances, transactions, income and expenses and profits and losses resulting from intra-group
transactions are eliminated in full on consolidation. Subsidiaries are fully consolidated from the date on which control is
transferred to the Group. Control is achieved when the Bank is exposed, or has rights, to variable returns from its
involvement with the investee and has the ability to affect those returns through its power over the investee. The results
of subsidiaries acquired or disposed of during the year are included in the consolidated income statement from the date
of acquisition or up to the date of disposal, as appropriate. No income statement is presented for the Bank as permitted
by section 408 of the Companies Act 2006. The principal subsidiaries are set out in note 15 for which the Bank holds
100% of the share capital issued.

1.2 BASIS OF PREPARATION

The consolidated financial statements have been prepared on a historical cost basis, except derivative financial
instruments and financial assets designated at fair value through either P&L (FVTPL) or OCI (FVOCD), all of which have
been measured at fair value. The consolidated financial statements have been prepared in accordance with UK adopted
International Accounting Standards in conformity with the requirements of the Companies Act 2006. The consolidated
financial statements are presented in US Dollars which is the Group’s functional currency, owing to this being the primary
currency that is used: (i) to fund the business, through share capital and deposits; (ii) for dividend distributions; and (iii)
the parent’s presentational and functional currency. All values are rounded to the nearest thousand (US Dollar thousand)
except where otherwise indicated. The US Dollar / Sterling foreign exchange rate applicable as at the reporting date was
1.3439 (2024: 1.25275).

In preparing the financial statements, the Group has considered the impact of the physical and transition risks of climate
change and identified this as an emerging risk as set out on pages 5 - 7 but have concluded that it does not have a material
impact on the carrying value of investments and the recognition and measurement of assets and liabilities in the financial
statements as at 31 December 2025.

A) INVESTMENT IN SUBSIDIARIES

The Group uses judgement in determining which entities are structured entities requiring consolidation. If the voting or

The Group sets up and uses structured entities to facilitate the conversion of legacy conventional loan portfolios and

B) FOREIGN CURRENCY TRANSLATION: TRANSACTIONS AND BALANCES

Transactions in foreign currencies are initially recorded in the functional currency at the rate of exchange prevailing on
the date of the transaction. Monetary assets and liabilities denominated in foreign currencies are translated into the
functional currency at the rate of exchange ruling at the balance sheet date. Any resulting exchange differences are
included in investment income in the consolidated statement of income. Non-monetary assets and liabilities that are
measured at historical cost in a foreign currency are translated using the exchange rates as at the dates of the initial
transactions. Non-monetary assets categorised as fair value through profit or loss and measured at fair value in a foreign

in the consolidated statement of income as part of the fair value adjustment of the respective item.

C) GROUP COMPANIES

Assets and liabilities of foreign subsidiaries whose functional currency is not US Dollars are translated into US Dollars
at the rates of exchange prevailing at the balance sheet date. Income and expense items are translated at the month end
exchange rates when the item is recognised in the income statement.

The classification of financial instruments at initial recognition depends on their contractual terms and the business model

All "regular way” purchases and sales of financial assets are recognised on the settlement date, i.e. the date that the Group

i) Overview of the ECL impairment principles

The ECL allowance is based on the credit losses expected to arise over the life of the asset (the lifetime expected credit

been a significant increase in credit risk are set out in Note 27.

The 12mECL is the portion of LTECLs that represent the ECLs that result from default events on AC assets that are
possible within the 12 months after the reporting date.

'L) HEDGE ACCOUNTING
The Bank enters into hedging instruments including foreign exchange forwards, profit rate swaps and forward commodity
contracts (“Islamic hedging financial instrument”) to manage exposures to profit rates and foreign currency risks,
including exposures arising from forecast transactions.

The Islamic currency swaps and forward commodity contracts are based on a Wa’ad (promise) structure between two
parties to buy a specified Shariah compliant commodity at an agreed price on the relevant date in future. It is a conditional
promise to purchase a commodity through unilateral purchase undertaking.

For Islamic profit rate swaps, counterparties generally exchange fixed and floating rate profit payments based on a
notional value in a single currency through series of transactions to buy a specified Shariah compliant commodity at an
agreed price on the relevant date in future based on Wa‘ad (promise) structure. The profit rate swaps, forward foreign
exchange and forward commodity contracts are being used for hedging purpose.

In order to manage particular risks, the Bank applies hedge accounting for transactions which meet the specified criteria.
Derivative financial instruments held for hedging purposes are stated at fair value.

At inception of the hedge relationship, the Bank formally documents the relationship between the hedged item and the
hedging instrument, including the nature of the risk, management objectives and strategy for undertaking the hedge. The
Bank performs statistical regression analysis to assess the effectiveness of the hedging relationship, which forms part of
the Bank's documentation. The Bank has adopted IFRS 9 for applying the requirements of hedge accounting.

For the purposes of hedge accounting, hedges are classified into two categories: (i) fair value hedges which hedge the
exposure to changes in the fair value of a recognised asset or liability; and (ii) cash flow hedges which hedge exposure to
variability in cash flows that is attributable to a particular risk associated with a recognised asset or liability, or a profit
transaction.

i) Fair value hedges

For fair value hedges which meet the conditions for hedge accounting, any gain or loss from re-measuring the hedging
instrument at fair value is recognised immediately in the consolidated statement of income. The hedged item is adjusted
for fair value changes relating to the risk being hedged and the difference is recognised in the consolidated income
statement.

If the hedging instrument expires or is sold, terminated or exercised, or where the hedge no longer meets the criteria for
hedge accounting, the hedge relationship is terminated. For hedged items recorded at amortised cost, the difference
between the carrying value of the hedged item on termination and the value at which it would have been carried without
being hedged is amortised over the remaining term of the original hedge. If the hedged item is derecognised, the
unamortised fair value adjustment is recognised immediately in the consolidated income statement.

In accordance with its hedging strategy, the Bank matches the principal of the hedging instruments to the principal of the
hedged items, including prepayment expectations. The Bank uses pay fixed/receive floating profit rate swaps to hedge
its fixed rate non-trading investments and financing receivables and pay floating/receive fixed profit rate swaps to hedge
its fixed rate liabilities.

The gains or losses on effective cash flow hedges recognised initially in equity are transferred to the consolidated
statement of income in the period in which the hedged transaction impacts the consolidated statement of income.

The possible sources of ineffectiveness for cash flow hedges are generally the same as those for fair value hedges,
described above. However, for cash flow hedges, prepayment risk is less relevant, and the causes of hedging
ineffectiveness arise from the changes in the timing and the amount of forecast future cash flows.

For hedges which do not qualify for hedge accounting, any gains or losses arising from changes in the fair value of the
hedging instrument are taken directly to the consolidated income statement.

Hedge accounting is discontinued when the hedging instrument expires or is sold, terminated or exercised, or no longer
qualifies for hedge accounting. In the case of cash flow hedges, the cumulative gain or loss on the hedging instrument
recognised in equity remains in equity until the forecasted transaction occurs, unless the hedged transaction is no longer
expected to occur, in which case the net cumulative gain or loss recognised in equity is transferred to the consolidated
income statement.

i) The Bank as a lessee

The Bank assesses at contract inception whether a contract is, or contains, a lease. That is, if the contract conveys the

activities are primarily in relation to agreements to rent office space for carrying out its business activities.

The Bank applies a single recognition and measurement approach for all leases, whereby the Bank recognises lease
liabilities to make lease payments and right-of-use assets representing the right to use the underlying assets.

The Bank recognises right-of-use assets at the commencement date of the lease. Right-of-use assets are measured at cost
which is the aggregate of the amount of lease liabilities recognised, less any lease incentives received, initial direct costs
incurred and an estimation of costs to be incurred to restore the underlying asset to the condition required by the terms of
the lease. Right-of-use assets are depreciated on a straight-line basis over the lease term, as follows:

Right-of-use assets are subsequently measured at cost, less depreciation, and tested annually for impairment in accordance
with IAS 36. Due to the nature of the underlying lease agreements, the Bank considers the probability of an impairment
event to be unlikely.

- Lease Liabilities

At the commencement date of the lease, the Bank recognises individual lease liabilities measured as the present value of
lease payments (excluding VAT) to be made over the lease term. Payments in this regard comprise solely lease payments

Where the lease contains a break or extension option clause, the Bank assesses whether it is reasonably certain that the
option would be exercised by the Bank, considering all relevant facts and economic conditions including market rates,
period remaining of the contract terms and any associated costs that would be incurred when triggering the option. A key
determination that an option would be triggered by the Bank would be a formal decision by either the Board or
Management Committee to trigger an option.

of its portfolios of financing receivables to customers to a structured entity it consolidates. However, as the Bank simultaneously re-acquires these rights through
a parallel agreement with a different structured entity it consolidates, it concludes that it retains substantially ail of the risks and rewards of ownership and
therefore the financial assets are not derecognised. In line with IFRS 9 requirements, the Bank does not recognise any new asset for the right to receive cashflows
from any instrument held which would be paid with cashflows received on the underlying assets considered as a failed sale (ie. the Bank applies net basis
accounting). Aligned with this, as any cash received as consideration for the transfer is immediately passed back to the SPV under parallel agreements, no
deemed liability with the structured entity is recognised.

24 DERIVATIVE FINANCIAL INSTRUMENTS

In the ordinary course of business the Bank enters into various types of transactions that involve derivative financial
instruments. A derivative financial instrument is a financial contract between two parties where its value changes in
response to the change in a specified profit rate, foreign exchange rate or other variable, to manage the risks, there is little or
no initial net investment, and settlement is based in the future.

Derivative financial instruments include financial forwards, profit rate swaps and currency swaps, which create rights and
obligations that have the effect of transferring between the parties of the instrument one or more of the financial risks
inherent in an underlying primary financial instrument. On inception, a derivative financial instrument gives one party a
contractual right to exchange financial assets or financial liabilities with another party under conditions that are potentially
favourable, or a contractual obligation to exchange financial assets or financial liabilities with another party under
conditions that are potentially unfavourable. However, they generally do not result in a transfer of the underlying primary
financial instrument on inception of the contract, nor does such a transfer necessarily take place on maturity of the contract.

Some instruments embody both a right and an obligation to make an exchange. Because the terms of the exchange are
determined on inception of the derivative instruments, as prices in financial markets change those terms may become either
favourable or unfavourable.

The Bank uses forward foreign exchange and swap contracts as part of its asset and liability management process to hedge
underlying on-balance sheet positions. The use of derivative financial instruments is controlled through the Bank's Risk
Management department which is an independent area of the Bank. The Risk Management department is responsible for
ensuring that all profit and exchange rate risk is undertaken within the policy guidelines set by the Board and the Asset and
Liability Committee.

Islamic forward agreements (Wa'ad)

The Bank enters into forward foreign exchange agreements (Wa’ad) to mitigate foreign currency risk. A Wa’ad is a unilateral
undertaking by one party in favor of the second party where the exercise of undertaking is dependent on the movement in
the price of one or more underlying financial instruments, reference rate or index in accordance with Islamic Shariah. The
notional amount, disclosed gross, is the amount of a Wa’ad’s underlying asset/liability and is the basis upon which changes
in the value are measured. The notional amounts indicate the volume of transactions outstanding at the year-end and are
neither indicative of the market risk nor credit risk.

Profit rate swaps

Profit rate swaps are contractual agreements between two parties and may involve exchange of profit or exchange of both
principal and profit for a fixed period of time based on contractual terms. The notional amounts indicate the volume of
transactions outstanding at the period-end and are neither indicative of the market risk nor credit risk. All of the Banks’s
profit rate swaps are held for hedging.

Profit rate risk

At 31 December 2025, the Bank had profit rate swap contracts in place whereby the Bank pays a fixed rate against underlying assets
(receives for underlying deposits) and thereon receives a variable rate of return (pays for deposits). The swaps are being used to hedge the
exposure to changes in the fair value of its fixed rate Sukuks, financing receivables and customer deposits.

Repricing risk arises from timing differences in the maturity of fixed rate assets and liabilities in comparison to the repricing floating rate
during the same period. Such repricing mismatches expose returns to abnormal fluctuations where there are unforeseen profit rates
movements. The Bank therefore manages such profit rate risk by entering into profit rate swap contracts on a micro basis, where on an
individual transaction level the critical terms of both the hedge item and hedging instrument match, including notional amount, maturity
date, fixed rate and coupon frequency. ;

The Bank's Risk management department monitor such risk within the policy framework of ‘profit rate risk in the banking book’ and the
results are reported to the Bank's Asset and Liability Committee (ALCO) on a monthly basis. In addition, any hedge ineffectiveness, both
cumulative life to date and current year, are also reported on a monthly basis to the Bank's ALCO. On the basis of the Bank's micro
hedging strategy, ineffectiveness is not considered to materially impact the hedging relationship, and as at 31 December 2025 represented
0.09% of gross profit rate swap notional.

27 CREDIT RISK

Credit Risk is the risk arising from a counterparty's failure to discharge on time and in full as contracted and cause the other party to
incur a financial loss. Credit Risk arises principally from financing but also from other transactions involving on and off balance sheet
instruments. The Risk Committee in conjunction with the Risk Management department have the responsibility for developing and
implementing policies to ensure that all exposures are properly pre-approved, measured and controlled. With the exception of certain
small retail financing receivables, all credits are independently pre-approved including, for credits above a set limit, by the Parent
Undertaking's Executive Committee. Credit proposals are put through a comprehensive risk assessment which examines the customer’s
financial condition and trading performance, nature of the business, quality of management and market position. In addition, an internal
risk rating model scores these quantitative and qualitative factors. The credit approval decision is then made and terms and conditions
set. Exposure limits are based on the aggregate exposure to the counterparty. All credit exposures (excluding residential property
backed exposures) are reviewed at least annually. The residential property backed exposures are reviewed on a portfolio basis.
Measurement of credits includes geographic, product, market and individual counterparty concentration.

All credit exposures are checked daily against approved limits, independently of each business unit, and are reported to senior
management. A thorough assessment of obligor's capacity to pay and robustness of security are evaluated prior to making real estate
financing receivables.

Financing receivables are analysed into the following categories:

~ Retail : principally individual customers’ consumer financing receivables and residential financing receivables.

- Corporate : principally financing receivables and other credit facilities for corporate and institutional customers.

The majority of financing receivables are secured on assets, for which the main geographical credit exposure is to the UK market.
Acceptable forms of collateral are defined within the credit risk framework and conservative valuation parameters are also pre-set and
regularly reviewed to reflect any changes in market conditions. Security structures and legal covenants are also subject to regular
review to ensure that they continue to fulfil their intended purpose and remain in line with market practice. Guidelines are in place
regarding the acceptability of types of collateral and valuation parameters.
The principal collateral types are as follows:

- financing receivables over residential properties;

- charges over business assets such as premises, plant, stock and receivables;

- charges over commercial properties being financed; and

- charges over financial instruments, such as debt securities and equities.

The Bank monitors the market value of collateral, periodically by type of asset using a variety of techniques including indexation.
Where appropriate, and in accordance with the underlying agreement, the Bank can request additional collateral.
In relation to financing receivable exposures, 2 possession orders were obtained and enforced during 2025 (2024: nil).

It is the Bank's policy to maintain consistent internal risk ratings across the credit portfolio. The credit quality of the portfolio of
financing receivables, debt instruments at amortised cost and deposits with banks, that are neither past due nor impaired can be assessed
by reference to the KFH plc's internal credit rating system. This facilitates focused portfolio management of the inherent risk across all
lines of business.

29 MARKET RISK

Market risk is the risk of potential financial loss that may arise from adverse changes in the value of financial
instruments or portfolios of financial instruments due to movements in profit rates, foreign exchange rates or other
prices and volatilities. This risk arises from asset-liability mismatches, changes that occur in the yield curve, foreign
exchange rates and changes in volatilities/implied volatilities in the market value of derivatives. The Bank classifies
exposures to market risk into either trading or non-trading portfolios. Given the Bank's low risk strategy, aggregate
market risk levels are considered low. The Bank utilises Value-at-Risk (VaR) models to assist in estimating potential
losses that may arise from adverse market movements in addition to non-quantitative risk management techniques. The
market risk for the trading portfolio is managed and monitored on a VaR methodology which reflects the inter-
dependency between risk variables. Non-trading portfolios are managed and monitored using stop loss limits and other
sensitivity analyses.

VaR is a technique which estimates the potential losses that could occur on risk positions taken due to movements in
market rates and prices over a specified time horizon and to a given level of confidence. VaR is calculated on a
historical simulation basis, using one-day movements in market rates and prices, a 99% confidence level and taking
into account the actual correlations observed historically between different markets and rates. The one-day movement
in market prices is calculated by reference to market data from a 260 trading day history. VaR should be viewed in the
context of the limitations of the methodology used.

- The use of a one-day holding period assumes that all positions can be liquidated or hedged in one day; this does not
fully capture the market risk arising from times of illiquidity, when one-day liquidation or hedging may not be possible.

- The use of a 99% confidence measure does not take account of any losses that might occur beyond this level of
confidence.

- The use of historical data as a proxy for estimating future events may not include all potential events, particularly
those that are extreme in nature.

- Correlations in the future may change from those correlations observed in the past.

~ VaR is calculated at the close of business with intra-day exposures not being subject to intra-day VaR calculations.

30 LIQUIDITY RISK

Liquidity risk is the risk that the Bank does not have sufficient financial resources to meet its obligations as they fall
due, or will have to do so at an excessive cost. This risk arises from mismatches in the timing of cash flows. Funding
risk arises when the necessary liquidity to fund illiquid asset positions cannot be obtained at the expected terms and
when required.

The Bank maintains a stable funding base comprising core retail and corporate customer deposits and institutional
balances, augmented by wholesale funding and portfolios of highly liquid assets which are diversified by currency and
maturity, in order to enable the Bank to respond quickly to any unforeseen liquidity requirements.

Treasury limits are set by the Asset and Liability Committee. Specifically the Asset and Liability Committee and the
Bank Treasurer are responsible for:

- projecting cash flows by major currency under various stress scenarios and considering the level of liquid
assets necessary in relation thereto;

- monitoring balance sheet liquidity ratios against internal and regulatory requirements;

- maintaining a diverse range of funding sources with adequate back-up facilities;

- managing the concentration and profile of debt maturities;

- managing contingent liquidity commitment exposures within predetermined caps;

- monitoring depositor concentration in order to avoid undue reliance on large individual depositors and ensure a
satisfactory overall funding mix; and

- maintaining liquidity and funding contingency plans. These plans must identify early indicators of stress
conditions and describe actions to be taken in the event of difficulties arising from systemic or other crises
while minimising adverse long-term implications for the business.

Market disruptions or a credit downgrade, for example, may reduce the availability of certain sources of funding. To
guard against this risk, management has diversified funding sources and assets are managed with liquidity in mind,
maintaining a healthy balance of cash, cash equivalents and readily marketable securities.

34. EMPLOYEE BENEFITS
Within the UK, the Bank operated a defined contribution pension scheme (“the Scheme”) which is open to current employees. Contributions to the
Scheme are charged directly to the statement of income.

The Bank also operates a funded defined benefit scheme (“the Fund”) for its employees who joined prior to 1 March 2001. The Fund was a final
salary scheme which became closed to future accrual with effect from 31 March 2010. The weighted average duration of the expected benefit
payments from the Fund is around 12 years.

The Fund is registered with HMRC for tax purposes, and is operated separately from the Bank and managed by a set of Trustees. The Trustees are
responsible for the payment of benefits and the management of the scheme’s assets which are held in a separate trustee administered fund. The
Fund is subject to UK regulations, which require the Bank and the Trustees to agree a funding strategy and contributions schedule.

The funding plan for the Fund is to hold assets equal to the value of the benefits earned by members of the Fund, based on a set of assumptions
used for funding the Fund. The funding assumptions differ from the assumptions used to calculate the figures in these accounts, and therefore
produce different results. If there is a shortfall against the funding plan, then the Bank and Trustees agree on deficit contributions to make good the
deficit over a period of time.

In 2023, the Trustees of the Fund changed the Fund’s investment strategy and invested the Fund’s assets in a buy-in insurance policy — an
insurance policy which pays an income to the Fund which matches the pensions to be paid to members. While the Bank remains legally
responsible for supporting the Fund, investing in this policy has removed the Bank’s exposure to investment and funding risks in the Fund. Based
on the Bank's Sharia conversion strategy, the Bank served notice on 15 January 2026 to the Trustees of the Fund to move to a buy-out phase and
begin the formal procedures to wind up the Fund in accordance with the scheme rules, which is anticipated to be fully completed in early 2027.

Contributions are set based on funding valuations carried out every three years. The latest completed triennial actuarial funding valuation was
carried out with an effective date of 31 December 2023, with the next valuation due as at 31 December 2026. The 2023 valuation indicated that on
a funding basis, there was a surplus of $1.0 million (£0.8 million translated at 2023 year-end FX rate of 1.2730). As a result, no additional deficit
contributions were required from 2024 onwards and the Bank does not expect any requirement for additional contributions in the future.

Virgin Media v NTL Pension Tmustees I Limited (and others)
On 16 June 2023, the High Court passed judgment in relation to the Virgin Media v NTL Pension Trustees II Limited (and others). This judgment

confirmed that certain rules of a contracted-out scheme cannot be altered without the statutory actuarial confirmation (“Section 37 confirmation”)
having been obtained and that non-compliant alterations would retrospectively become void. The decision was upheld on appeal in July 2024.

On 5 June 2025, the UK Government announced that it would introduce legislation that could enable Section 37 confirmations to be obtained
retrospectively. In September 2025 the UK Government subsequently followed up with an amendment paper for the Pension Schemes Bill 2025. In
this, the Bill is amended to include new clauses that seek to address the Virgin Media issue.

The Bank, in conjunction with the Trustees of the Fund and their legal advisers, have continued to monitor these developments. Reviews of the
amendments made to the Fund during the relevant period previously identified two deeds that would require actuarial confirmation and a further
three deeds where the legal advice is that the deed amendments would not require actuarial confirmation, but a court might rule that written
actuarial confirmation is required. Whilst searches for these missing confirmations have not yet been completed, given the developments described
above, the risk of any additional liabilities resulting from these so far missing confirmations is considered to be reduced. Further, any valuation
impact cannot be quantified.

Based on these developments and the information available, the Bank as concluded that it is not necessary to make any adjustments at the current
time as a result of the Virgin Media case.

GMP equalisation

benefits are covered by the insurance policy. In addition, the Trustees identified a small number of members who had previously left the Fund that
required top ups in respect of equalisation. All members requiring top ups who the Trustees have been able to trace have now been paid. No further
GMP equalisation top-ups are expected to be paid and therefore no allowance has been made in the LAS19 figures as at 31 December 2025 for

FINANCIAL ASSUMPTIONS - SUMMARY
The key assumptions at 31 December 2025 have been derived consistently with those adopted at 31 December 2024. The changes in financial assumptions
therefore reflect only changes in underlying financial conditions.

Key financial assumptions
Assumptions are set by reference to yield curves at the relevant accounting dates and reflect the Fund's projected benefit payment profile. The above assumptions
are the single equivalent assumptions that give the same value of the IAS 19 liabilities as if the figures were calculated using the yield curves described below.

Discount rate:

IAS 19 requires that the discount rate is set based on yields of high quality corporate bonds at the balance sheet date.

We have constructed a yield curve from high quality (AA rated) corporate bonds. To derive the discount rate, the yield curve is weighted according to scheme cash
flows, which have an average duration of about 12 years (31 December 2024: duration of 12 years). The approach used to set the discount rate is in line with the
“LCP Treasury Model”.

The yield curve used to set the discount rate has been constructed as follows:

* Data on corporate bond yields is obtained as at the accounting date;

* Bonds are included in the data set if they are classified as “AA” and “Corporate”, in line with the requirements of LAS19;

* Bonds are classified as AA if they are rated AA by the index provider;

+ Reasonableness checks are carried out on the data, in particular to ensure that we use bonds only where the prices supplied and yields are consistent;

* Ayield curve is fitted to the data using least squares optimisation techniques, such that the forward rate at year 30 and above is equal to the average forward rate
over the 15 to 30 year period.

Inflation and pension increase p
IAS19 requires that assumptions for inflation are best estimate, based on market expectations at the balance sheet date.

Benefits from the Fund depend on both the RPI and CPI price indices, which are constructed differently. The approach to setting the RPI and CPI inflation
assumptions is unchanged from the previous year-end.

The assumption for future RPI inflation is derived from the breakeven measure implied by the difference between nominal yields on fixed interest gilts and real
yields on RPI linked gilts. The single equivalent breakeven inflation rate, weighted by scheme cash flows, is 3.1% pa.

RPI inflation - The best estimate, market consistent assumption for RPI is derived by deducting an inflation risk premium from breakeven inflation. Consistent with
last year, we have allowed for an inflation risk premium of 0.2% pa. Overall, this results in a single weighted average RPI assumption of 2.9% pa (reflecting
breakeven inflation of 3.1% pa less an average inflation risk premium of 0.2% pa).

CPI inflation - The assumption for CPI inflation is set by deducting a “wedge” from the RPI inflation assumption, to reflect structural differences between the
indices, The CPI assumption is derived as follows:

» Pre 2030: a wedge of 1.0% is deducted.

+ 2030 onwards: a wedge of 0.1%. This reflects the small expected differences between the CPIH index, which will be used to calculate RPI from 2030, and the CPI
index. We assume that CPIH inflation will be higher than CPI inflation because CPIH includes an allowance for owner occupied housing costs, which tend to
increase at a higher rate than the general price level.

Overall, this results in a single weighted average CPI assumption of 2.1% pa, reflecting RPI inflation of 2.9% pa less an average wedge of 0.8% pa.

Pension increases - In setting the assumed level of future pension increases, we have adjusted the relevant inflation assumption to allow for the volatility of future
inflation and the maximum and minimum increases. For this purpose, we have used the Black model with an assumed volatility of 1.5% pa for both RPI and CPI
inflation. This is the same approach as was used at the previous year-end.

Demographic assumptions

Mortality: .

The assumption for post-retirement life expectancy is important and uncertain, particularly when considering the projection of future changes in mortality rates. The
widely used CMI model requires a number of judgements and parameters to be set. With the exception of the mortality projection assumptions, all demographic
assumptions are the same as used last year end.

The changes in life expectancy assumptions over the year has an impact on the value of the defined benefit obligation and annuity policy (there is a circa £0.4m
increase to both).

Transfer values:

The Bank has made no explicit assumption for how many members will transfer out of the Fund in future. This is because there is little relevant historical data on
past transfers on which to base a robust assumption and whether past experience is a reliable guide to the future. The figures implicitly assume that future transfer
values would be cast neutral on the accounting basis.

Commutation:

It is assumed that members commute 20% of pension at retirement based on current factors. This is consistent with the approach taken last year. The assumed
percentage commuted is consistent with the membership experience observed by the Trustees of the Fund over the three years to 31 December 2023 (the date of the
latest actuarial valuation) — members commuted on average 19% of their pension at retirement.

We have updated the terms under which members are assumed to commute pension at retirement to match the terms currently offered by Aviva ~ the Trustees have

agreed a policy of adopting Aviva’s terms to ensure that there is no mismatch between benefit payments from the Fund and Aviva’s payments to the Fund. As well
as affecting the value of the defined benefit obligation, the updates to the commutation factors changes the asset value by the same amount.

Operational risk
The Bank has adopted the Basic Indicator approach for operational risk. Under this approach, the regulatory capital requirement for operational
risk is calculated by applying a co-efficient of 15 per cent to the average gross income for the preceding three financial years.

Leverage Ratio
CRD IV requires firms to calculate a non-risk based Leverage Ratio, to supplement risk-based capital requirements. The leverage ratio measures

the relationship between the Tier | capital resources of the organisation and its total assets (pre credit risk mitigation). The purpose of monitoring
and managing this metric is to enable regulators to constrain the build-up of excessive leverage. There is a current minimum requirement of

3.25%.

Remuneration

The Bank's remuneration policy is approved by the Board of Directors on recommendation by the Remuneration Committee, on an annual basis.
The pool for KFH PLC’s discretionary performance-based annual cash bonus scheme is calculated based on profits and other KPI’s as budgeted
by the Board. This calculation ensures that any adjustments required to the fair valuation accounting model profit figure further to the regulatory
tules are made. Overall performance rating of employees via the appraisal system takes account both financial and non- financial criteria
(including compliance with regulatory requirements, compliance with the risk framework of the Bank, the conduct rules and internal policies and
procedures). The ratings are subject to a calibration review by senior management, with inputs from risk and compliance functions. The rating is
then linked to the proposed cash bonus to be awarded to each employee. The proposed bonus awards are reviewed and approved the Remuneration
Committee, The Committee can reduce the bonus pool and to increase or reduce individual bonus payments.
