3.1.7, Effective interest method

relevant period. The effective interest rate is the rate that exactly discounts estimated future cash receipts (including all fees paid or recenved
that form an integral part of the effective interest rate, transaction costs and other premiums or discounts) thraugh the expected life of the
financial asset, or. where appropriate, a shorter period. This effactive interest rate ig calculated on the gross carrying amount of a financial
asset except for financial assets that have subsequently become credit-impaired financial assets. For those financial assets, the Company
apphes the effective interest rate to the amortised cost less impairment of the financial asset in subsequent reporting periods.

of deferred tax assets is reviewed at each balance sheet date and reduced te the extent that it is no longer probable that sufficient taxable

The sensitivity analysis may not be representative of an actual change in the defined benefit obligation as it is unlikely that changes in

Funding of the Offshore scheme

The actual funding of the Offshore scheme is determined by the actuarial valuation, and this differs from the amaunt that is required to be
charged to the profit and loss account under IAS 19. In 2023, the estimated contributions will amount to £205k. As the Offshore scheme is
closed to new members from January 1999, under the projected unit method, the current service cost will increase as a percentage of salaries
as the members of the Offshore scheme approach retirement.

The Trustees invest in assets that are expected to be correlated to future inflation in the Jonger term (sometimes referred to as “real” assets).
This means that, over the longer term, such assets are expected to keep pace with inflation. Such assets include equities and index-linked
bonds. As the Offshore scheme holds index-linked bonds, it currently partially hedges its exposure to inflation nsk.

The Trustees consider the risk that falls in asset values might not be matched by similar falls in the value of the Offshore scheme’s liabilities
when determining the Offshore scheme's investment strategy. They consult with the Campany in order to understand the Company's appetite
for bearing this risk and take advice on the Company's ability ta make good any shortfall that may anse. To the extent that such falls in asset
values result in deficits at future valuations, the Company would be required to agree a recovery plan with the Trustees to restore full funding
over a period of me. The Offshore scheme currently hedges the majority of its interest rate risk by investing in a combination of corporate
bonds and gilts to match the duration of the Offshore scheme’s liabiites.

Some of the risks associated with the Offshore scheme are set out below:

» Asset volatility
The Scheme's liabilites are calculated using a discount rate set with reference to corporate band yields. If the Offshore scheme's assets
underperform this yield, this may lead to a worsening of the funding pasition of the Offshore scheme. The Offshare scheme holds a
proportion of equities and other return seeking assets, which are expected to outperfarm corporate bonds in the long-term but give
exposure to volatility and nsk in the shart-temm.

» Changes im bond yields
A decrease in corporate bond yields will increase the Offshore scheme’s liabilities, althaugh this will be partially offset by an increase in
the value of the Offshore scheme’s bond holdings and other assets designed to hedge inflation and interest rate risk

» Inflation risk
The majority of the Offshore scheme's DBO Is linked ta inflation (after members retire} where higher inflation will lead to a higher value
being placed on the D8Q. A small proportion of the Offshore scheme's assets are either unaffected by inflation or loosely correlated
with inflation (e.g. growth assets), meaning that an increase in inflation will generally increase the deficit.

» Life expectancy and concentration nsk
The majority of the Offshore scheme's obligations are to provide benefits for the life of the members. so increases in life expectancy wilt
resuit in an increase in the Offshore scheme’s liabilities, and furthermore, inflationary increases result in higher sensitivity to changes in
life expectancy. There is the risk that the members live longer than implied by current assumptions used. In particular. the majority of
the Offshore scheme’s liabilities are held by a smalt number of members. and if these members live longer than assumed this could put
a strain on the funding

1 INTRODUCTION

1.1, Framework for disclosures

This Report provides Pillar 3 disclosures for SG Kleinwort Hambros Bank Ltd at a UK Consolidation level in accordance
with the international regulatory framework established by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS),
also known as Basel III. In the European Union (“EU”), the Basel framework is implemented by the Regulation (EU)
575/2013 on prudential requirements (Capital Requirements Regulation or “CRR”) and the Directive (EU) 2013/36 on
access to the activity and the prudential supervision (Capital Requirements Directive or “CRD”). Articles 431 to 455 of
CRR specify the requirements of the Pillar 3 framework.

Pillar 3 requires firms to publish a set of disclosures which allow market participants to assess key pieces of
information on that firm's capital, risk exposures and risk assessment process. The disclosures contained in this
document cover the qualitative and quantitative disclosure requirements of Pillar 3, set out in the Capital
Requirements Regulation as implemented in the PRA Rulebook CRR Instrument and the PRA Rulebook CRR Firms:
Leverage Instrument (collectively known as “CRR”), and are based on data as of 31% December 2022 with comparative
figures for 31% December 2021 where relevant. These regulations came into force on 1 January 2022 and were
implemented by the PRA via the PRA Rulebook.

The aim of the capital adequacy regime is to promote safety and soundness in the financial system, It is structured
around three ‘pillars’;

» Pillar 1 sets the minimum solvency, leverage and liquidity requirements and defines the rules that banks must
use to measure risks and calculate the related capital requirements, according to standard or more advanced
methods;

«Pillar 2 concerns the discretionary supervision implemented by the competent authority, which allows them -
based ona constant dialogue with supervised credit institutions - to assess the adequacy of capital requirements
as calculated under Pillar 1, and to calibrate additional capital requirements taking into accaunt all the risks to
which these institutions are exposed;

"Pillar 3 encourages market discipline by developing a set of qualitative or quantitative disclosure requirements
which will allow market participants to better assess a given institution’s capital, risk exposure, risk assessment
processes and, accordingly, capital adequacy.

1.5. Calculation Approaches

The CRR determines minimum capital requirements predominantly by calculating RWAs for credit, counterparty
credit, market and operational risks. Various RWA calculation approaches are available to banks, with differing levels
of sophistication.

SGKH Bank Ltd uses the following approaches to calculate RWAs:
« Credit risk: The standardised (STD) approach is used for exposures across alt portfolios.

» Counterparty credit risk: The exposure amount is calculated using the mark-to-market (MTM) method for
derivative transactions.

« Market risk: The standardised approach is used for market risk in the non-trading book.
« Operational risk: The standardised approach is used.

The minimum capital requirement is calculated as a percentage of RWAs depending on the capital ratio being
calculated. On top of the minimum capital requirement, there are several other buffers including capital
conservation, countercyclicality and systemic importance. Further details on the constituents of capital and the
various buffers can be found in section 4 - Annex VII.

« Credit Risk
Credit risk appetite is managed through a system of credit policies, risk limits and pricing policies. When
assuming credit risk, SGKH focuses on medium- and long-term client relationships, targeting both
clients with which the bank has an established relationship of trust and prospects representing
profitable business development potential over the mid-term.

Acceptance of any credit commitment is based on in-depth client knowledge and a thorough understanding
of the purpose of the transaction. In a credit transaction, risk acceptability is based, first, on the borrower’s
ability to meet its commitments, particularly the cash flows for the repayment of the debt. For medium and
long-term operations, the funding duration must remain compatible with the economic life of the financed
asset and the visibility horizon of the borrower's cash flow. SGKH seeks risk diversification by controlling
concentration risk.

Counterparty ratings are a key criterion of the credit policy and serve as the basis for the credit approval
authority grid used in both the commercial and risk functions. The rating framework relies on internal
models,

Risk measurement for the credit portfolio is primarily based on the Basel parameters used to calibrate
capital requirements. The calculation of expected losses pursuant to IFRS 9.2, used to determine the level
of impairment on perfarming loans, is also helpful in assessing the portfolio’s risk.

In cooperation with the Risk Function, SGKH implement pricing policies which are differentiated based on
counterparty and transaction risk levels. Transaction pricing is designed to ensure acceptable profitability,
in line with the Return on Equity (“ROE”) goals of the Business Unit, after taking the transaction cost of risk
into consideration. Transaction pricing may nevertheless be adjusted in certain cases to take the overall
profitability and development potential of the client relationship into consideration. The intrinsic
profitability of client segments and products is periodically analysed to take changes in the economic and
competitive environment into account

Proactive management of impaired risks is key to containing the risk of final lass in the event of default of
a counterparty. In this regards, SGKH Graup has implemented procedures to monitor counterparties with
a worsening risk profile. Furthermare, the risk function has a dedicated bad debt specialist to assist with
asset recovery management to effectively preserve the Company's interest in the event of default.

investment pockets are mainly to facilitate exit of client accounts and to manage any operational
incidents.

Counterparty credit risk is the risk that the counterparty to a transaction (derivatives or repos) could
default or deteriorate in creditworthiness before the final settlement of a transaction’s cash flows. The
future market value of the exposure and the counterparty’s credit quality are uncertain and may vary
over time as underlying market factors change. The counterparty risk associated with the securities
portfolio and interbank balances are covered with the Credit Risk section.

SGKH Group uses the sensitivity of the net present value (NPV) to the risk of interest rate mismatch.
