Business Overview

Belmont Green Finance Limited (“BGFL”) and its subsidiaries (“the Group”) is focused on providing specialist mortgage
lending to selected customer segments who are typically underserved by the larger UK retail banks and building
societies. Our customers will typically have some element of complexity in their personal circumstances and we deploy
an efficient combination of modern technology and expert human underwriting to ensure we deliver the best possible
customer outcome.

All of our lending is secured against high quality residential properties, with every one of our customers receiving
regulated advice from an independent mortgage intermediary before selecting one of our purpose built product
solutions.

Whether we are serving owner-occupiers or Buy to Let (“BTL”) landlords, all of our mortgage solutions are designed to
be straightforward and offer real value to those borrowers who may otherwise find it difficult to secure the loans they
need.

BGFL has established the award winning Vida Homeloans as a strong and recognisable brand in the specialist mortgage
sector and by the end of 2019 had completed £1.8bn of new lending, helping almost 10,000 borrowers achieve their
ambition of home ownership.

Chairman’s Statement

It is my pleasure to introduce Belmont Green’s Annual Report and Accounts for the year ended 31 December 2019.
For the second year running, at a macro level, the year was characterised by continued uncertainty in markets and
only at the very end did the fog start to lift following the emphatic result of the General Election and the prospect of
some kind of resolution to the Brexit issue. Whilst overall, the housing market remained flat measured by transaction
volumes and prices, the specialist lending sector again increased its share of the pie as the shift from mainstream
lending towards the provision of more bespoke product continued. Given the changing demographic of the borrowing
public, we see no reason why the shift towards specialist lending becoming the new norm will not be continued in the
years ahead,

It was clear from early in the year that much of what we saw in 2018 would be repeated. Many of our competitors
continued to chase volume at the expense of margin and possibly credit quality and we took an early decision not to
follow suit. Our lending remained firmly within our risk and credit appetite and at margins that made sense.

Given that it was clear that the market dynamic was unlikely to be conducive to writing an increased volume of
business, the Board took the decision to reset the business in preparation for the time when more balanced market
conditions prevail. These steps included the rationalisation of our warehouse capacity, and reviewing and revising our
expected loan lives assumptions and their impact on effective interest rate (EIR) calculations, all of which inevitably
had a negative effect on bottom line profitability.

More positively, we also took the decision to strengthen the Board and Executive by hiring a new CEO and CRO and
adding two vastly experienced NEDs. The incumbent Executive team had done a great job in getting the business up
and running and establishing it as a significant player in the specialist lending market and it is appropriate at this point
that | should record my thanks to David Tweedy and his team for the work they did and to wish them well for the
future.

However, the Board recognised that a new approach and skill set was required to take us forward in the next stage of
our development and Anthony Mooney, an experienced retail banker and lender, was hired to lead this next chapter.
We strengthened our Board effectiveness considerably by the appointment of Carol Sergeant as Senior Independent
Director and Chair of the Board Risk Committee and Robin Churchouse as Chair of the Board Audit Committee and
these two individuals have already had a positive influence on the way the business operates and responds to the
many challenges that the market throws up.

We have been getting to grips with the impacts of the prolonged pandemic caused by the Coronavirus and how this
has manifested itself on global markets and the supply of both capital and liquidity. An early decision was taken to
temporarily cease new lending, whilst our other preparations have proven sound. Like all businesses we continue to
adapt to ever changing circumstances and the Board believes that we are well placed to respond appropriately in any
given scenario. Market turmoil and uncertainty are fast becoming the new norm but | believe that the quality of the
management team we now have in place sees us well placed to respond to these challenges and to be ready to
capitalise on new opportunities when they arise.

Strategic Review

Mortgage Market

2019 saw a continuation of trends seen in the previous year with political and economic uncertainty driven by Brexit
providing the backdrop to the mortgage market. According to UK Finance, gross mortgage lending in the UK was
£267.6bn in 2019, a decrease of 0.4% compared to £268.7bn in 2018, with remortgage activity forming a significant
portion of this lending at 38%. House price growth was limited at 2.2%, with growth in the South East being 1.2%’.

The mortgage market has continued to be very competitive. Overall margins within mortgage markets fell during
2019 as excess liquidity held by larger financial institutions was used to lend in, principally mainstream prime,
retail mortgage markets.

The specialist lending segment of the market has remained strong as demand has continued from customers not well
served by the mainstream. Government-backed initiatives have continued to stimulate demand from first time buyers
seeking to get on to the property ladder, driving £60.0bn of lending in 20197. Gross lending in the BTL mortgage market,
which is principally serviced by specialist lenders, increased to £41.2bn, 1.7% up on 2018’s total.

Within the specialist residential lending sector, Vida offers mortgages to first-time buyers, the self-employed and those
with complex credit histories. Vida launched its Help to Buy product in April 2019 and this was received well in the
market. In the BTL segment, portfolio landlords, non-portfolio landlords, limited companies and expats investing in the
UK are Vida’s main target customers. In 2019, of Vida’s total lending of £557m, £408m or 73% was in the BTL segment.

Vida has expanded its distribution, which included the launch of lending in Scotland in late 2018, and has approximately
13,000 registered brokers. Its latest broker survey for Q4 2019 gave a +28 Net Promoter Score.

Financial Results

BGFL continued its growth in 2019 with its new lending delivering strong underlying asset margins, whilst being
conservative in terms of credit risk appetite. Given the uncertainties in the economy, the Board took the decision to
constrain growth. As a result, new lending of £557m for the year was £226m lower than £783m of new lending in 2018.

The Board re-assessed and re-set some of the core accounting assumptions of its business model, partially to reflect
changing borrower behaviours and market dynamics, and this led to material adjustments to margins in the year.
These adjustments will not be repeated going forwards and, having been made, give the business a solid base from
which it can grow financially.

The statutory loss before tax for 2019 was £35.3m (2018: £9.2m). The underlying loss before tax, after excluding
exceptional items, was £33.9m (2018: £9.2m). The adjustments to core accounting assumptions have not been treated
as exceptional items and have had no impact on the cash position of the business.

Income Statement

Net Interest Income

Net interest income fell by £14.9m to £2.6m from £17.5m in 2018. Whilst underlying loan margins were
maintained and the average loan book grew 72% year-on-year, the one-off adjustments already noted were
responsible for the reduction in net interest income.

The most significant adjustment resulted from adopting a more conservative approach to the expected lives of loans
to customers. A reduction in those lives involved a lower run rate for interest income during the year compared to
2018. It also involved a charge of £7.7m for revenue brought to account in prior years.

BGFL successfully completed its fourth securitisation, Tower Bridge Funding No. 4 PLC (TBF4), in July 2019. The
securitisation, at £500m, was equal in size to the previous deal in September 2018, and had a pre-funding component

BGFL had access to four warehouse facilities during 2019. In order to provide sufficient liquidity in the uncertain

than anticipated involved a write-off of a further £1.6m of previously deferred costs.

In 2019, some issues were identified with previous lending (refer to “Major Developments and Initiatives” below).
As a consequence, a provision was made against income for a reduction in the effective interest rate for a cohort
of 115 loans. This provision amounted to £0.8m.

The adjustments referred to above have resulted in impacts on margins but going forward these remain at an attractive
level. When combined with the Board’s prudent risk appetite and resulting low expected credit losses, this means that
risk adjusted margins look healthy. A move into retail funding in the medium term would further enhance the
sustainability of this margin. This means that going forward lending volumes will be the key driver of profitability as
discussed later.

Fair Value Movements

Fair value movements reflect the notional impact of changes in underlying interest rates on the value of assets
and financial instruments. As BGFL intends holding these assets and financial instruments to maturity these
movements will unwind positively or negatively in future years and therefore are not viewed as part of the core
performance of the business. In 2019 a loss of £3.9m was recorded from fair value movements compared to a
loss of £0.5m in 2018.

Administrative Expenses

Administrative expenses increased to £31.6m in 2019 from £25.1m in 2018. As part of BGFL commencing its banking
licence application process, it started to build its banking capability during 2019. This involved recruiting experienced
staff and starting the projects to implement new systems. This drove a significant part of the increase in the cost base,
which, in 2019, did not lead to a benefit in income.

Aside from the banking licence activity, there was no significant increase in staff levels. The costs of outsourced loan
servicing increased as the loan book grew.

Balance Sheet

Net loans to customers grew by 34% in 2019 to £1,600m (2018: £1,198m) primarily due to an increase in BTL
lending. BTL loans made up 73% of the loan book at 31 December 2019.

As noted earlier, BGFL’s forward-looking margins and expenses mean that the key driver for future profitability will be
lending volumes. The segments that Vida focuses on, including portfolio landlords, specialist BTL properties, first-time
buyers and borrowers with complex credit histories, are forecast to grow in the medium term. In terms of distribution,
Vida is well-placed with an established brand and reputation in the intermediary channels where these segments are
largely served. BGFL’s market share of the specialist lending market will remain relatively modest even as it grows.

Wholesale funding totalled £1,618m at the end of 2019 (2018: £1,200m), of which £1,396m related to
securitisation funding (2018: £1,026m). Total share capital, 99.3% provided by BGFL’s private equity investor, Pine
Brook, stood at £168m (2018: £130m).

Major Developments and Initiatives

In 2019, some issues were identified with a previous tranche of lending to a small proportion of borrowers whose
loans ran beyond their retirement age. Management has worked internally and with the FCA to review all affected
and potentially affected cases to ensure, in the first instance, that no customer harm results. The Board is satisfied
that our current lending policies and procedures mean that no repeat issues will arise, and in the meantime, to ensure
no customer suffers financial disadvantage, a total provision of £1.5m has been made. This provision was set against
interest income, £0.7m, and also increased the impairment provision, £0.8m.

BGFL commenced the process of applying for a banking licence to enable it to accept retail deposits. A project team
was established and staff recruited to build a new Treasury capability and an enhanced risk management framework.
Much of the systems work on the Treasury side has been completed, and the risk management framework has been
significantly enhanced. The lending issue referred to above has impacted on the licence process, which is being delayed
whilst we resolve the issue to the FCA’s satisfaction.

Risk Management Developments

In preparation for the banking licence application, the Risk Management Framework was extensively revamped during
2019 to strengthen controls and to bring it up to “banking standard”. A comprehensive suite of policies has been
developed, covering the range of risks faced by BGFL to ensure that it operates in alignment with its vision and business
strategy and within its approved risk appetite. The policies set out the enterprise-wide risk management of BGFL’s
activities, its employees and 3rd party servicer firms operating on BGFL’s behalf. Risk governance has been restructured
with the establishment of an Executive Risk Committee, which has oversight of all risk types throughout the business.
Risk MI has been redeveloped to report comprehensive key risk indicators against all risk types.

In preparation for the banking application, draft ICAAP and [LAAP documents have been prepared, prospective risk
appetites for banking liquidity, funding and capital have been agreed and training has been completed for both Board
members and senior management.

Section 172 Statement

Section 172 of the Companies Act 2006 requires a director of a company to act in the way he or she considers, in good
faith, would be most likely to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole. In doing
so, he or she must have regard, amongst other matters, to:

e =the likely consequences of any decision in the long term;
e the interests of the company’s employees;

e the need to foster the company’s business relationships with suppliers, customers and others;

e the impact of the company’s operations on the community and the environment;

e =the desirability of the company maintaining a reputation for high standards of business conduct; and
e the need to act fairly as between members of the company.

The Board considers the factors set out above in discharging its duties under section 172. The Board also has regard to
other factors that it considers relevant to the decisions it makes and recognises that some decisions will not necessarily
result in a positive outcome for all the Group’s stakeholders. Engaging the Group’s stakeholders is key to the way the
Group runs its business and is an important consideration for the Board when making decisions.

The framework within which the Board operates and makes its decisions is set out under “BGFL Board” and “Board
Culture” in the Corporate Governance section on pages 9 to 11. The Board considers the Group’s key stakeholders to
be its customers, staff, key outsourced suppliers, its shareholders and its regulator, the Financial Conduct Authority.
Further information on how the directors have regard to these key relationships can be found in the Directors’ Report.

Corporate Governance

The Board of Directors of BGFL is responsible for the governance of the Group, setting strategic goals, providing the
leadership to put them into effect, supervising the management of the business and reporting to shareholders on their
conclusions. Embedding good corporate governance practice is fundamental! to the Group in creating and building an
internal structure supporting the governance and decision making across the business and in creating internal and
external stakeholder value. The BGFL Board ensures that the Group has sufficient resources to meet its objectives and
to comply with all legal, regulatory and contractual requirements.

Committee Structure

The Board recognises that an appropriate governance framework and the implementation of effective management
practices are integral to ensuring the proper functioning of BGFL. The governance framework is based on the principles
of UK best practice, for example, the Corporate Governance Code, the Walker Report and as expressed by the UK
Regulatory Authorities from time to time through direct interaction with them in respect of such matters. The
effectiveness of the governance structure is subject to periodic reviews at least annually.

The Group’s governance structure consists of the Board of Directors of BGFL, a BGFL Executive Committee with defined
roles and responsibilities and other committees of BGFL with roles and responsibilities delegated by the Board of BGFL
or by the Executive Committee. This structure helps ensure there is effective oversight of the activities undertaken by
the business.

Board Culture

The Board of BGFL recognises that its culture is a pre-requisite to good corporate governance. A key output from the
working relationship between the Board Chairman and the CEO is clarity about the definition and measurement of the
Board culture. The Board commitment to a culture of good corporate governance is evidenced in Board and sub-
committee minutes where discipline and challenge are highlighted.

Given Pine Brook’s majority shareholding, the Board is careful to form its own views of the challenges, risks and issues
that the business faces, ensuring that strategy and decisions are in the best interests of customers, employees,
regulators and other key stakeholders. The Board works consciously to ensure that the shareholder does not have
undue influence. All Committee Chairs are independent and have site visits and conversations with Executives, and in
some cases intermediaries and suppliers, to understand other perspectives in detail.

As part of developing a healthy Board culture, the Directors rely on the independence of Internal Audit, currently
provided by PwC on an outsourced basis. Board Audit Committee ensures that the Internal Audit Function is effective
and delivers independent and competent challenge to the business.

The Chairman and CEO are mindful of the need for the Executive and Board to maintain mutual respect and trust.
Management shares information openly, recognising that if all have the same information then challenge can be made
coherently with spirited debate becoming the norm. Respect and trust do not lead to ‘endless affability’ or absence
of disagreement but creates bonds that allow different viewpoints and challenging questions as evidenced in Board
minutes.

Board performance reviews are recognised as a valuable tool and the Board completed an effectiveness review in 2019
with an independent Board assessor. The review included an evaluation of the understanding and development of
strategy, Board composition, Board access to information, and levels of openness, challenge and energy. Board
members are also being asked to consider the use of their own skills, their knowledge of the company and the
mortgage industry to develop their general level of contribution.

Risk Management Framework

BGFL’s Risk Management Framework can be visualised as a pyramid with a top-down approach being taken to the
identification and management of risks to which BGFL is, or may potentially be, exposed. It provides robust policies,
controls, processes, procedures and reporting for effective risk management, delivered through appropriate risk
escalation and governance in a Three Lines of Defence model.

The Board is responsible for setting BGFL’s Vision and risk appetite, together with approval of a business plan (including
financial forecasts) which is consistent with that Vision and risk appetite. The business plan takes into account a wide
range of exogenous factors such as macroeconomics, market conditions and competition, internal operational
constraints/efficiencies and outsourcing. It is then the responsibility of the Executive Committee to execute against
that business plan in line with the Vision, risk appetite, and BGFL’s Values and Behaviours. As part of the FCA’s Senior
Managers and Certification Regime (SMCR) the management responsibilities map details accountability for risk at the
Board and Executive Committee.

A key part of the Risk Management Framework is its alignment with these Values and Behaviours and appreciation by
employees that they are all responsible for risk management and ensuring good customer outcomes. This is achieved
through an extensive programme of regular training for the Board and all employees, led by the HR Department,
working closely with the Risk Function.

Three Lines of Defence

BGFL has implemented a Three Lines of Defence model to ensure clear separation of risk management responsibilities
between operational management which owns, manages and controls risks in BGFL (the “First Line”); oversight, testing
and challenge of those operations and controls, together with Compliance oversight (the “Second Line”); and
independent assurance of the first two lines (the “Third Line”). This provides consistent, coherent and complete
coverage of all the risks to which BGFL is, or potentially could be, exposed. All three lines of defence are tasked with
supporting and developing a culture of risk awareness education throughout BGFL to create desired outcomes for BGFL
and its customers.

First Line of Defence — Business and support functions

The First Line of Defence comprises BGFL’s business and support units (and their individual staff) who are responsible
for day-to-day identification, mitigation, management and monitoring of all risks arising within their functions. In
addition, the First Line of Defence is responsible for developing and communicating appropriate policies, processes,
controls and procedures for managing risks in accordance with the Risk Management Framework and BGFL’s approved
risk appetite. To assist operational management in these tasks BGFL has developed RACI (Responsible, Accountable,
Consulted and Informed) charts to clarify and define roles across BGFL, identifying the accountable individual who is
ultimately answerable for the activity or decision, and the individual(s) who are accountable for completing the task.

Risks, Internal Risk Events (whether a loss was incurred or not) and Near Misses are included in management
information reporting and escalated, where necessary, through the Risk Governance Structure. The First Line of
Defence works with the Risk Function to implement remedial actions to investigate Internal Risk Events and Near
Misses and stop them happening again. They also work together to identify, assess and mitigate risks on proposed new
products, with approval being obtained through the Risk Governance Structure.

Second Line of Defence ~ Risk Department
BGFL’s Second Line of Defence is provided by the Risk Function whose primary responsibilities are as follows:

*® monitoring the effectiveness of the Risk Management Framework, Risk Governance Structure and Three Lines
of Defence model, reporting findings/recommendations to the Executive Risk Committee and the Board Risk
Committee, as required;

® monitoring and reporting BGFL’s exposures against approved risk appetite via the KRIs for each Risk Type;

BGFL’s policies, processes, controls and procedures;

*® working with the HR Department regarding risk management training for the Board and all employees;

The Risk Function is headed by the CRO, who is a member of the Board and ExCo, attends Board Risk Committee and

the FCA (SMF3 ~ Executive Director; SMF16 ~ Compliance Oversight; and SMF17 — Money Laundering Reporting

In anticipation of BGFL becoming a bank, staffing levels in the Risk Function have been reviewed to ensure it will have
sufficient resources, both in terms of number and calibre/experience of employees, to provide effective oversight. As
a result of this review, a Director of Prudential Risk joined BGFL in June 2019.

Third Line of Defence — Internal Audit

BGFL’s Third Line of Defence is its Internal Audit Function, outsourced to PwC, which provides the Board with
independent assurance regarding the suitability, operation and effectiveness of BGFL’s Risk Management Framework,
its Risk Governance Structure and the First and Second Lines of the Three Lines of Defence model. The Internal Auditor
attends of the Board Audit Committee and reports directly to the Committee’s chairman. Internal Audit’s annual audit
plan is approved by the Committee and completed audit work is reported to the Committee.

Principal Risks

Based on the Risk Management Framework and Vision set out above, BGFL identified the types of risk (“Risk Types”)
to which it is exposed. BGFL’s definition of each Risk Type together with its risk appetite, as approved by the Board, is
set out below. A key driver for the Board when setting risk appetite is alignment with BGFL’s strategic objectives. Risk
appetite was reviewed and reconfirmed by the Board in 2019.

Risk-taking is an inherent part of BGFL’s business in order to achieve its strategic objectives, but it must be adequately
compensated for taking those risks in order for the business model to be sustainable. Where BGFL is not being
compensated risks should be avoided, or where this is not practical, minimised or mitigated, e.g. through the use of
insurance. Given BGFL’s strategy and business model as a specialist mortgage lender, the only risk category in which
BGFL is explicitly compensated for taking risk is credit risk. Other risks should only be taken to the extent that they are
necessary so that BGFL can take credit risk and thereby achieve its strategic objectives.

The range of risks faced by BGFL and its appetite for these risks is reviewed annually, or more frequently if required,
by the Executive Risk Committee led by the CRO, with any recommended changes being reviewed at ExCo before being
presented to Board Risk Committee for review, challenge and final approval.

Conduct Risk

BGFL definition: The risk of customer detriment or poor outcomes through unsuitable products, poor service
or process failures.

BGFL has no appetite for offering products or services to its customers which are unsuited to their needs, or
which may result in poor customer outcomes or cause customer detriment. Whilst BGFL recognises that minor
operational or service issues may occur which could affect customers, these must not result in poor customer
outcomes or detriment for those customers.

In the event that failings occur, this risk is further managed through our commitment to ensure that no
customer harm results — we will manage issues to ensure that our customers are treated fairly and absorb any
impact.

As at 31 December 2019, BGFL was outside its risk appetite, as systemic failings had been identified in previous

affected customers. BGFL is in close dialogue with the FCA and currently awaiting FCA agreement to the
proposed remediation plan. The Board is satisfied that no repeat issues will arise.

As detailed in note 2 on page 42, ‘Significant accounting judgements, estimates

IFRS 9 requires management to estimate the credit losses that the group is

portfolio of financial assets, recognised at amortised cost.

Estimating these expected losses requires judgement and estimation on
assumptions relating to customer default rates, likelihood of repossession, future
property values, forced sale discounts and indicators of significant increases in
credit risk. These assumptions are informed using historical behaviour and
experience as well as credit bureau data. They are also affected by
management's consideration of the future economic environment including the
potential effect of the withdrawal of the United Kingdom from the European
Union. The Group have applied this key accounting estimate through its selection

Interest income and expense are recognised in the statement of comprehensive income for all financial instruments

Fee income is included in interest income and similar income and fee expense in interest expense and similar charges.

(f) Taxation

Tax on the profit or loss for the period comprises current tax and deferred tax. Tax is recognised in the income
statement except to the extent that it relates to items recognised directly in equity, in which case it is recognised in
other comprehensive income.

Current tax is the expected tax payable on the taxable profit for the year. Taxable profit differs from profit as reported
in the consolidated income statement because it excludes items of income and expense that are taxable or deductible
in future years and it further excludes items of income and expense that are never taxable or deductible. The Group's
liability for current tax is calculated using tax rates that have been enacted or substantively enacted at the balance
sheet date.

Deferred tax is the tax expected to be payable or recoverable on differences between the carrying amounts of the
assets and liabilities in the financial statements and the corresponding amounts used for taxation purposes and is
accounted for using the balance sheet liability method. Deferred tax liabilities are generally recognised for all taxable
temporary differences. Deferred tax assets are recognised to the extent that it is probable that taxable profits will be
available against which deductible temporary differences can be utilised.

Deferred tax is calculated at the tax rates that are expected to apply in the year when the liability is settled or the asset
is realised based on tax laws and rates that have been enacted or substantively enacted at the balance sheet date.

Deferred tax assets and liabilities are offset when there is a legally enforceable right to set off current tax assets against
current tax liabilities and the Group intends to settle its current tax assets and liabilities on a net basis.

(k) Financial assets and liabilities

On initial recognition, financial assets and liabilities are classified as measured at amortised cost, fair value through
other comprehensive income or fair value through profit or loss, depending on the Group’s business model for
managing the financial assets and whether cash flows represent solely payments of principal and interest.

The Group initially recognises loans and advances to customers and debt securities in issue when the Group becomes
a party to the contractual provisions of the instrument. Other financial assets and liabilities are recognised on trade
date, being the date, the Group is committed to purchase or sell an asset.

Financial instruments measured at amortised cost

Financial assets (predominantly mortgage loans to customers) that are held to collect contractual cash flows where
the cash flows represent solely payments of principal and interest are measured at amortised cost. A basic lending
arrangement results in contractual cash flows that are solely payments of principal and interest on the principal
amount outstanding.

Financial assets are initially recognised at fair value plus transaction costs that are directly attributable to that asset.
Subsequently, they are measured at amortised cost using the EIR method, less impairment losses.

Borrowings (predominately debt securities in issue) are recognised initially at fair value, being their issue proceeds net
of transaction costs incurred. These instruments are subsequently stated at amortised cost using the EIR method.

Financial instruments measured at fair value through profit or loss

Financial assets are classified at fair value through profit or loss where they do not meet the criteria to be measured
at amortised cost or fair value through comprehensive income or where they are designated at fair value through
profit or loss to reduce an accounting mismatch. All derivatives are carried at fair value through profit or loss.

Financial liabilities measured at fair value through profit or loss are recognised in the balance sheet at their fair value.

Derecognition

Financial assets are derecognised when the contractual right to receive cash flows from those assets has expired or
when the Group has transferred its contractual right to receive cash flows from the asset and either substantially all
the risks and rewards of ownership have been transferred or the Group has neither retained nor transferred
substantially all of the risks and rewards, but has transferred control.

(I) impairment of financial assets

The Group assesses on a forward-looking basis the expected credit losses {ECLs) associated with its financial assets
that are not measured at fair value through profit or loss.

For loans to customers, accounts are assigned to one of three stages which are intended to reflect the deterioration
in credit quality:

*® Stage 1 comprises loans that have shown no significant deterioration in credit quality since origination. They
are low risk loans and carry an impairment provision equivalent to the ECL from those default events that are
projected within 12 months of the reporting date (12-month ECL).

Page | 39 Belmont Green Finance Limited — 2019 Annual Report and Accounts
e Stage 2 comprises loans that have shown significant deterioration in credit quality since origination. These are
loans that are more than 1 month past due or where there has been a significant relative increase in the
lifetime probability of default (PD) compared to that at initial recognition, and where the PD for loans is derived
based on the customer’s credit quality, including analysis of behaviour score showing an increase in risk. These
loans carry an impairment provision equivalent to the ECL over the contractual lifetime of the loan (lifetime
ECL).

e Stage 3 comprises loans where there is objective evidence that the loan is credit impaired. The objective
evidence that is used to determine whether a loan is impaired is whether it is more than 3 months past due,
has been renegotiated for credit risk reasons or otherwise considered to be in default, including possession.
These loans also carry an impairment provision equivalent to the lifetime ECL, but with the PD set to 100%.

ECLs are calculated at individual loan level, with the calculations having 3 main components: PD, loss given default
(LGD) and exposure at default (EAD). The LGD represents losses expected on default, given the probability of a loan
moving from default to possession, the estimated recovery in the event of possession, costs incurred in the possession
and sale of security and discounting of recoveries to present value based on the time to sale. The EAD represents the
expected balance at the time of default, using the contractual amortisation of the loan equivalent to the account
balance at default with 3 months of non-payment and the associated accrued interest.

As the Group has limited internal data available to it given that the business was only launched in late 2016, its
statistical models use external data combined with expert judgement to assess the inputs into the ECL calculations.

IFRS 9 requires the estimates of PD and LGD to take into consideration the effects of variations in forward looking
macroeconomic variables, which include house prices, unemployment and interest rates. The Group integrates
probability-weighted macroeconomic forecasts into individual ECL calculations. The Group does not have an in-house
economics function and sources economic forecasts from an appropriately qualified third party. The Group considers
up to four macroeconomic forecast scenarios — base, upside, downside and stress.

Loans are written off, either partially or in full, when there is no realistic prospect of recovery. Where loans are secured,
this is after receipt of any proceeds from the realisation of security.

(m) Fair value measurements

Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction
between market participants at the measurement date. It is measured at a specific date and may be significantly
different from the amount which will be paid or received on maturity or settlement date.

Wherever possible, fair values have been calculated using unadjusted quoted market prices in active markets for
identical instruments held by the Group. Where quoted market prices are not available, fair values have been
determined using valuation techniques which, to the extent possible, use market observable inputs, but in some cases
use non-observable inputs. Valuation techniques used include discounted cash flow analysis and pricing models and,
where appropriate, comparison to instruments with characteristics similar to those of the instruments held by the
Group.

Assets and liabilities carried at fair value or for which fair values are disclosed have been classified into three levels
according to the quality and reliability of information used to determine the fair values.

Level 1

Level 1 fair value measurements are those derived from unadjusted quoted prices in active markets market for
identical assets or liabilities. The Group does not hold any financial instruments falling within level 1 of the hierarchy.

Level 2

Level 2 valuations are those where quoted market prices are not available, for example where the instrument is traded
in a market that is not considered to be active or valuation techniques are used to determine fair value and where
these valuation techniques use inputs that are based significantly on observable market data.

Page | 40 Belmont Green Finance Limited — 2019 Annual Report and Accounts
Level 3

Level 3 valuations are those where at least one input which could have a significant effect on the instruments’ valuation
is not based on observable market data. Such instruments would include the Group loans to customers as their
valuation uses unobservable inputs on collectability rates and redemption profiles.

The Group values loans to customers and debt securities in issue using level 3 valuation techniques. Loans to
customers fair value is measured using modelled receipts of interest and principal which are discounted at market
rates. The fair value of issued debt securities is calculated using modelled payments of interest and principal
discounted at market rates for similar instrument.

The fair values of derivative financial instruments, interest rate swaps, are calculated by discounting cash flows using
appropriate but unobservable market data. As such these instruments fall within level 3 of the hierarchy.

(n) Derivative financial instruments and hedge accounting

As permitted by iFRS 9, the Group continues to apply the requirements of IAS 39 to its hedging relationships. All
derivatives are recognised at their fair value. Derivatives are carried on the balance sheet as assets when their fair
value is positive and as liabilities when their fair value is negative. Please refer to 2. Accounting policies (m) Fair value
measurement for detail of valuation techniques and significant inputs to valuation models.

Hedge accounting allows one financial instrument, generally a derivative such as a swap, to be designated as a hedge
of another financial instrument such as a loan or a portfolio of such instruments. At the inception of the hedge
relationship, formal documentation .is drawn up specifying the hedging strategy, the hedged item, the hedging
instrument and the methodology that will be used to measure the effectiveness of the hedge relationship in offsetting
changes in the fair value or cash flows of the hedged risk. The effectiveness of the hedging relationship is tested both
at inception and throughout its life and if at any point it is concluded that it is no longer highly effective in achieving
its documented objective, hedge accounting is discontinued.

Fair value hedges

Changes in the fair value of derivatives that are designated and qualify as fair value hedges are recorded in the income
statement, together with the change in the fair value of the hedged asset or liability that are attributable to the hedged
risk. If the hedge no longer meets the criteria for hedge accounting, changes in the fair value of the hedge item
attributable to the hedged risk are no longer recognised in the income statement. The cumulative adjustment that
has been made to the carrying amount of the hedged item is amortised to the income statement using the effective
interest method over the period to maturity.

Provisions are recognised when the Group has a present obligation as a result of a past event, which is reliably
measurable and when it is probable that the Group will be required to settle that obligation. Provisions are measured
at the directors' best estimate of the expenditure required to settle the obligation at the reporting date.

In 2019, some issues were identified with a previous tranche of lending to a small proportion of borrowers whose
loans ran beyond their retirement age. BGFL has sought to ensure that no customer harm results and consequently
will take action that could involve a lower EIR from this cohort of loans. This action would be treated as an act of
forbearance and so places each of these loans at stage 3 for ECL calculation purposes. At 31 December 2019 a total

As there is a possibility that some of this cohort of loans will redeem before action is taken by BGFL, it is a matter of
judgement as to whether the Group will incur a reduction in income to the extent of the provision. Given that this
matter has yet to be fully resolved with the FCA, the Board is satisfied that the provision at 31 December 2019 is
appropriate.

IBOR Reform

In September 2019, the IASB issued interest Rate Benchmark Reform — Amendments to IFRS 9, IAS 39 and IFRS 7. These
amendments modify specific hedge accounting requirements to allow hedge accounting to continue for affected
hedges during the period of uncertainty before the hedged items or hedging instruments affected by the current
interest rate benchmarks are amended as a result of the on-going interest rate benchmark reforms.

The amendments are relevant to the Group given that it applies hedge accounting to its benchmark interest rate
exposures.

¢ The Group has fixed rate advances in the form of retail mortgage lending to customers which it includes in a

component was separately identifiable at the time of the initial designation, and as noted above, the
amendments permit continuation of hedge accounting even if in the future the hedged benchmark rate, GBP
LIBOR, may no longer be separately identifiable.

e The Group will not discontinue hedge accounting should the retrospective assessment of hedge effectiveness

20. Risk management and financial instruments

Financial instruments form the majority of the Group’s assets and liabilities. The Group manages risk on a consolidated
basis and risk disclosures are provided on this basis. The principal financial risks to which the Group is exposed are
credit, liquidity and market risks.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the risk that unexpected lasses may arise as a result of the Group’s borrowers or market counterparties
failing to meet their obligations to repay. The classes of financial instruments to which the Group is most exposed are
loans to customers and cash with other financial institutions. The maximum credit risk exposure equals the total
carrying amount of these categories plus off-balance sheet undrawn mortgage facilities.

Page | 54 Belmont Green Finance Limited - 2019 Annual Report and Accounts
Credit risk — loans to customers

Credit risk associated with mortgage lending is largely driven by the housing market and level of unemployment. A
recession and/or high interest rates could cause pressure within the market, resulting in rising levels of arrears and

repossessions.

All loan applications are assessed with reference to the Group’s lending policy. Changes to the policy are approved by
the Board Risk Committee (BRC), with mandates set for the approval of loan applications. BRC regularly monitors
lending activity, taking appropriate actions to adjust lending criteria in order to control risk and manage exposure.

Interest rate risk

To provide a broad indication of the Group’s exposure to interest rate movements, the notional impact of 0.50%
change in UK interest rates on the equity of the Group at 31 December 2019, and the notional annualised impact of
such a change on the operating profit of the Group, based on the year end balance sheet have been calculated.

Assuming all relevant interest rates move by the same amount in parallel and that all repricing takes place at the
balance sheet date, a 0.50% decrease in UK interest rates would increase the Group’s loss before taxation by £4,095k
and its equity by the same amount at 31 December 2019. A decrease in the interest rate would have an opposite effect.

It should also be noted that the changes in carrying value of derivative financial instruments designated as fair value
hedges from movements in interest rates have an immaterial effect on the Group income statement and equity due
to compensating adjustments in the carrying value of the debt.

27. Events after balance sheet date

Subsequent to year end, the COVID-19 pandemic has caused significant social and economic disruption. The Group has
sought to support both its customers and staff since the onset of the pandemic. As a direct result of the pandemic, the
Group is expected to be impacted in the year ahead by the contraction in available funding from the wholesale funding
markets, leading to a reduction in lending, together with increased ECLs from higher levels of loan arrears and defaults.

Whilst the overall financial impact of the COVID-19 pandemic cannot be reliably estimated at this time, the directors
have considered its impact on the carrying values of assets and liabilities in the Consolidated and Company Statements
of Financial Position. The directors have assessed that the key sensitivity is in relation to ECLs on loans to customers.

ECLs

The estimate of ECLs at 31 December 2019 was based on the reasonable and supportable information available at that
date. The Group has sourced revised economic forecast scenarios from its third-party economic adviser, reflecting
economic developments since the reporting date. Given the higher level of uncertainty in those forecasts, a range of
weightings, more broadly spread across the scenarios than those used at 31 December 2019, were considered. The
downside economic forecast was adjusted by the directors to give an extended, u-shaped recovery phase and has also
been used to assess the sensitivity of ECLs.

Calculated ECLs are most sensitive to forecast movements in the House Price Index (HPI) and unemployment. By way
of comparison, these are the weighted average annual HPI movements and unemployment rates at their forecast
trough/peak resulting from the economic downturn:

The deferred tax balance of £10.2m at 31 December 2019, disclosed in note 13, principally comprises tax losses that

28. Basis of preparation, significant accounting policies and critical judgements and estimates

(a) Accounting basis

The separate financial statements of Belmont Green Finance Limited (“the Company”) are prepared and presented in
accordance with International Financial Reporting Standards as issued by the International Accounting Standards
Board and as adopted by the European Union. The significant accounting policies are the same as set out in note 2 of
the consolidated financial statements except as noted below.

(b) Income statement

Under section 408 of the Companies Act 2006 the company is exempt from the requirement to present its own income
statement.

(c) Securitisation vehicles

The sale of the beneficial ownership of the loans to customers to the special purpose vehicles (“SPVs”) fails the
derecognition criteria and, hence, these loans remain on the statement of the financial position of the company. In
accordance with IFRS 9, the Company recognises the securitised assets as loans and receivables and consequently also
shows a deemed loan liability to the SPVs where the Company still holds an interest in the loans. An equivalent deemed
loan asset is recognised on the SPV’s statement of financial position. The deemed loans are repaid as and when
principal repayments are made by customers against their loans. The Company substantially retains the risks and
rewards of ownership and continues to bear the credit risk of these mortgage assets.

(d) Prior year adjustments

(i) The 2018 and 2017 accounts were restated to reflect the reassessment of loans to customers originated by BGFL
with subsequent contractual cashflows transferred to the SPVs. These SPVs are used by the Group for funding and
securitising assets. The loans were previously derecognised by the Company, however, following a thorough analysis
and in accordance with IFRS9, it has been established that BGFL retains substantial risks and rewards. The company
neither retains nor transfers substantially all risks and rewards but has a contractual right to repurchase the mortgages
at a later date and therefore records a continuing involvement in the mortgages equal to the amount that may be
repurchased. The proceeds received are recorded as a liability.
