Brown Shipley & Co. Limited Annual Report 2022

STRATEGIC REPORT

The Directors present their Strategic Report for Brown Shipley & Co. Limited (‘Brown Shipley’, ‘BSCO' or the
‘Company’) for the year ended 31 December 2022.

Brown Shipley’s principal activities

Brown Shipley’s principal activity is to provide wealth management services comprising investment
management, wealth planning, lending and other private banking services to its clients, which mainly consist
of private individuals. The Company aims to increase its client base and funds under management through
organic growth, marketing, intermediary development and acquisitions.

The Company’s vision is to be recognised as a trusted partner to its clients and as a leading private bank,
providing integrated wealth management solutions to those clients. The Company’s mission is to preserve
and grow each client’s wealth across generations.

Managing clients’ funds on a discretionary basis is at the heart of the Company’s offering. Having full discretion
with securities held by our nominee and cash held by us as an authorised bank, allows our investment
managers maximum flexibility and enables us to provide quality services efficiently and at a reasonable cost.
Increasingly the service provided to clients is that of Wealth Management, a combination of investment
management along with ongoing wealth planning. These services enable the Company to provide clients with
advice on all aspects of their financial affairs. The Company also provides advisory management and
execution only investment management services.

Brown Shipley is an authorised bank providing a range of services to its clients. Current accounts are offered
alongside term deposit facilities and offers lending as a complementary service to wealth management. Loans
are only offered to enhance the service provided to existing customers or to establish new private banking
relationships. Loans are typically made on a secured basis, for a maximum of five years and at conservative
loan to value ratios. Security held for loans comprises mainly investment management portfolios and UK
residential property assets.

Business review

Brown Shipley has remained profitable in 2022 despite the challenging market conditions discussed below.
Clients value the advice and guidance that we offer and given the economic, political, and market environment
they have faced through 2022, access to personal service and advice has helped us to strengthen
relationships and demonstrate value to the clients we serve. Coronavirus (‘COVID-19') drove us to adapt our
ways of working, and having maintained communications through various channels, we have set out to be as
active, and as accessibie as we could be, to address the needs, questions and concerns of our clients.

Clients will be happy to see 2022 in the rear-view mirror, as it brought together the worst equity performance
since the global financial crisis and the deepest bond bear market in 70 years. There were few places to hide
as central banks embarked on aggressive rate hiking campaigns to counter runaway inflation. Russia’s war
in Ukraine added to geopolitical tensions elsewhere and pushed Europe into an energy crisis.

Against this backdrop Brown Shipley has unfortunately seen client portfolios deliver negative absolute returns
across all 5 risk profiles. The three key elements of our core investment philosophy — to invest globally, to

' invest in quality, growth sectors, and to invest sustainably, meant that portfolios did not perform as well as UK
focused, value based investors with exposure to energy and fossil fuel sectors. Having adjusted portfolio
positions to stabilise returns, without.u-turning on these fundamental principles, we look ahead to 2023
confident that the combination of our investment approach and our personalised advice will continue to serve
the needs of our clients.

Financial review of the Company and key performance indicators (KPIs)

The Company’s reported profit before tax was £2.3m (2021: £5.3m). However there were a number of one-
off or non-recurring items both in the current and prior year, particularly around the accounting for the
acquisition in 2019 of NW Brown & Co (‘NWB’) and the loss incurred on the subsequent sale of a small non-
core book of those customer relationships in April 2022.

When these are stripped out or made comparable, the figures show an adjusted operating profit before tax of
£4.3m (2021: £5.2m). Given the difficult trading environment experienced during the year, it is a satisfactory
outcome for the year.

Net operating income

Total net operating income increased by 2.3% to £93.0m (2021: £90.9m). The growth includes one off gains
of £0.4m (2021: £3.6m) following receipt of the final deferred consideration payment for the sale of our pension
administration activities in January 2021 and of £1.0m (2021: £3.0m) from reducing the deferred acquisition
costs for the acquisition of NWB.

Net operating income also includes a loss of £0.9m from the sale of a portfolio of £94m of assets in April 2022.
These assets consisted of relatively small value client portfolios, held on external custody platforms, most of
these assets had been acquired as part of the NWB acquisition.

Transfer pricing income from the recharge to the parent company of staff employed by BSCO but undertaking
Quintet Private Bank (Europe) S.A. (‘Quintet’) Group roles increased to £9.2m (2021: £8.3m). Such staff in
the UK are employed by BSCO and recharged to the parent company with a cost mark up of between 5% and
15%, dependent on the service provided.

Fee and Commission income decreased by 8.9% to £57.6m (2021: £63.2m) as asset-based fees suffered
from the fall in Assets Under Management during the year.

The banking element of the business continues to grow in line with the Company’s strategy. After many years
of very low interest rates, the Bank of England actions in 2022, to increase the UK Base Rate from 0.25% to

3.5% by December has had a strong positive impact on interest margins in 2022 with margins up 93% to
£18.0m (2021: £9.3m), as spreads widened from historic lows.

Net gains from financial instruments at fair value through profit and loss also increased to £7.7m (2021:
£3.6m), as the rise in interest rates led to an increase in the fair value of interest rate swaps held by the
Company for hedging purposes of £1.9m. This was also coupled with increased FX trading volumes of £1.9m
due to volatile market conditions and gains of £0.3m from the sale of part of our holding of Visa inc preference
shares during the year (see Note 6).

Costs

In headline terms costs rose by 3% to £88.1m (2021: £85.5m) largely due to a further increase of £2.6m in
the transfer pricing expense charged to Brown Shipley from Quintet to £12.4m (2021: £8.9m), to reflect the
services received by Brown Shipley during the year and its share of group-wide project costs.

Average staff numbers increased by 2 during the year, with average staff FTE’s increasing to 388 (2021: 386).
As a result staff costs were relatively stable during the year, increasing by only 1.7% year on year to £53.7m
(2021: £52.8m) despite external wage inflation pressures.

Impairment of intangible assets

As set out in the Company’s accounting policies (see Note 1d), the Company undertakes an annual review of
impairment of all intangible assets held on the balance sheet to assess whether the fair value of each asset
is greater than the carrying value of the asset on the balance sheet. As a result of the fall in AUM during the
year, the Company assessed that the fair value of the intangible asset held in relation to NWB customer
relationships was less than the carrying value and therefore recorded an impairment charge of £2.4m (see
Note 20). In addition, the goodwill held on the balance sheet in relation to asset management businesses
acquired in the early 2000’s was also deemed to be impaired and a charge of £0.1m was taken to the income
statement (see Note 20).

Section 172(1) Statement - Stakeholder engagement
Section 172(1) of the Companies Act 2006 requires the Directors to act in a way that they consider in good
faith, would most likely promote the success of the Company for the benefit of its members as whole. In doing
this, Section 172(1) requires the Directors to have regard, amongst other matters, to:

(a) the likely consequences of any decision in the long-term;

(bo) the interests of the Company’s employees;

(c) the need to foster the Company's business relationships with suppliers, customers and others;

(d) the impact of the Company’s operations on the community and the environment;

(e) the desirability of the Company maintaining a reputation for a high standard of business conduct; and

(f) the need to act fairly as between members of the Company.

The Directors have acted in the way that they believe promotes the success of the Company for the benefit
of its stakeholders as a whole and maintaining a reputation for high standards of business conduct. In carrying
out this duty, the Directors have had regard for the matters set out in section 172(1)(a-f) of the Companies
Act 2006, as outlined above, in the decisions taken during the year ended 31 December 2022.

The Board's key stakeholders, and a summary of how we engage with them, are set out below. Further
information is also provided in the Directors report on pages 11 to 25 of these financial statements:

events and we run client surveys and round tables periodically to learn what we do well and what we could
improve on.

Different ways of communicating have developed since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic and we are

Outlook

Risk Philosophy and Risk Management Framework

The philosophy of the Company is that all members of staff have a responsibility for risk. The Board carries
the ultimate responsibility, as stated in the Enterprise Risk Management Framework, and is supported by the
Risk Control function. The Framework also describes the “Three Lines of Defence” model operated by the
Company and the responsibilities at the various levels within the organisation:

« The first line of defence consists of business line management and staff who are responsible for the risks
inherent in their business area activities and who, supported by the first line Business Risk Management
(BRM) function, ensure that appropriate systems and controls are in place to effectively mitigate the day-
to-day risks in the Company. They are also responsible for compliance with policies and procedures and
for the escalation of issues to senior management and risk governance functions, where relevant.

« The second line of defence is provided by the Company’s Risk Control and Compliance functions. These
functions set the overarching risk and compliance frameworks, monitor adherence to the firm’s approved
risk appetite, and are responsible for providing support and challenge to the first line in their risk
management activities.

¢ The third line of defence is the independent assurance provided by Internal Audit.

Risk Appetite

Risk appetite defines the amount and type of risk, both financial and non-financial, that the Company is able
and willing to accept in the pursuit of its business objectives and strategies. The overall objective is to protect
the Company from unacceptable levels of risk, while supporting and enabling the Company to implement its
overall business strategy (including the assessment of new business opportunities). The Company’s core
franchise is as a wealth manager, and consequently, the Company adopts a conservative appetite for non-
strategic risk, to ensure high confidence that it can survive any potential threats in both normal and stressed
conditions.

To support this objective, Brown Shipley has developed individual risk appetite statements for all of the
Company's key risks, which are reviewed and approved at least annually by the Board. The individual risk
appetites define the risk boundaries for the Company's services and operating parameters. Adherence to the
risk appetites is monitored via Key Risk Indicator (KRI) metrics. These metrics have formal risk appetite limits
and lower level triggers for management action, to address any causes of heightened risk that may threaten
the stated appetite.

By setting explicit thresholds for the key risk areas, the Board can clearly document what level of risk it is
prepared to accept in normal business activities, and conversely, what level of risk is unacceptable. The setting
of both a limit and trigger provides more granular guidance for management, with the limit being a hard stop
but the trigger being an early warning notification prompting corrective action.

Individual risk appetite limit and trigger breaches that occurred during the year were promptly identified and
reported to the ERC and BRCLC, together with the associated remediation plans which were then tracked to
resolution. Throughout the year and at year end, the Company remained within its overall risk appetite,
however was above the trigger threshold for part of the year. This was driven by factors such as investment
performance, discretionary outflows and levels of net new money.

Risk Control Function

Overseen by the CRO, the Risk Control function comprises of Financial, Operational and Credit Risk teams.
The Risk Control teams provide advice and challenge to the first line business areas and monitor risks,
providing regular independent reporting to the ERC and BRCLC, including adherence to approved risk
appetite.

The Risk Control function has a close working relationship with Finance, especially through the management
and oversight of financial risks. The teams work closely together in the production and review of the annual
capital and liquidity regulatory assessment processes. This is also illustrated in the Asset and Liability
Management Policy, which describes the responsibilities at a detailed level and the escalation processes to
the Asset and Liability Committee.

The management of non-financial risks is undertaken within the business areas throughout the organisation,
with support and challenge from the Operational Risk team. A key component of the firm’s non-financial risk
framework is the Risk and Control Self-Assessment (‘RCSA’) process. This process is central to the
identification, assessment and management of the Company’s risks, by leveraging the collective knowledge
and experience of its management and staff, overseen by the Operational Risk team. The RCSA process
delivers an assessment of these inherent risks, identifies the controls which mitigate them and assesses the
effectiveness of those controls.

The Risk Control function is responsible for implementing and maintaining risk related policies and procedures
to ensure robust risk management and independent oversight. Risk Control is also responsible for monitoring
adherence with the Risk Management Framework, policies and exposures against risk limits across the
Company.

Risk Control undertakes the Company's Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (‘ICAAP’) on an
annual basis, to document for the Board, the Management team and regulatory bodies of the ongoing
assessment of the Company's risks, how those risks are mitigated, and to recommend how much current and
future capital is required to support the delivery of the Company’s strategic business plan. The Company uses
the ‘standardised’ approach to calculate its Pillar 1 Credit Risk and Operational Risk capital requirement. The
Company conducts regular stress testing as part of its capital management framework via the ICAAP process.
The ICAAP was last reviewed by the PRA in 2021.

The Company also produces an Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process document (‘ILAAP’) on an
annual basis. The Company operates a number of liquidity monitoring metrics to ensure that it maintains high
levels of liquidity at all times. The ILAAP was reviewed by the PRA in the first quarter of 2022.

Whenever the Company is involved in acquisition activity it considers the impact on capital and liquidity by
incorporating the acquisition into its ICAAP and ILAAP on an ad hoc basis where relevant, in addition to the
usual annual assessment.

Regulatory risk

The banking and financial services sector in which we operate is heavily regulated. Failure to comply with
regulatory requirements could lead to censure, fines or other disciplinary action. Financial crime remains a
material risk, which is likely to increase with the higher profile of the Company as it grows. There is also a risk
that new or updated regulation could adversely affect profitability. To mitigate these risks, we maintain robust
financial crime controls and monitor proposed changes to regulation internally and through industry bodies,
to assess the impact any changes may have on our business and always ensure we have sufficient resources
to meet the requirements.

People risk

The Company is a people focused business and so we are exposed to risks relating to the attraction and
retention of appropriately skilled colleagues to deliver our business objectives. Risks have‘ evolved in the
period with a greater focus on flexible working and employee wellbeing, accelerated by experiences in the
pandemic, and enhanced regulatory focus on workplace diversity and inclusion. The Company mitigates these
risks by seeking to maintain competitive remuneration packages, with long term incentives to align key
colleagues with the values and success of the Company, supported by clear HR policies. The Company has
actively supported colleague engagement and continues to promote diversity and inclusion initiatives.

Technology risk

The continuing delivery of high quality services to clients is to a large extent dependent on a robust and flexible
IT infrastructure. Failure of an IT strategy or implementation would have an adverse impact on the Company.
Monitoring, improving and supplementing existing systems is a high priority. Additionally the Company keeps
abreast of cyber-crime and information security developments and works closely with its parent company
Quintet, which provides core system and network infrastructure to the Company.

Outsourcing risk

A key activity or service contracted out by us to a third party will be defined as outsourced where it is deemed
to be critical or important to the performance of the Company’s business. Outsourcing risk is the risk of third
party or supplier failure on a scale that can cause disruption, financial costs and reputational damage. The
Company outsources elements of its day to day operational activities to various third parties, including its
parent company Quintet. All material outsourcing relationships are closely monitored, with formal oversight
structures in place, and are subject to an annual due diligence process.

Operational risk

Operational risk is the risk of direct or indirect losses resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes,
people and systems, or from external events. This includes fraud risk, legal risk, business continuity risk and
data security risks. It also includes the risks associated with the outsourcing of critical activities and in
particular IT and Operational Hub services through our parent company Quintet and the Group’s outsourcing
partner Lombard Odier.

The Company actively manages these risks through the implementation of sound policies and robust first line
procedures and controls, supported by a strong risk culture and oversight from the second line of defence.

Group Risk

As part of a European banking group, the Company is exposed to risks relating to its relationships with other
entities within the Quintet Group. These risks are managed through transparency of reporting and the
engagement of ExCo and senior colleagues in Quintet management and project committees. Formal
intragroup services agreements, with associated Service Level Agreements (‘SLAs’) and structured oversight
models, are in place for services provided by other group entities.

Reputational risk

Brown Shipley has a reputation as a provider of high quality investment management and wealth management

Energy efficiency actions

Brown Shipley is committed to responsible energy management and practices energy efficiency throughout
the organisation. The Company recognises that climate change is one of the most serious environmental
challenges currently threatening the global community and it has a role to play in reducing greenhouse gas
emissions.

When combined our Scope 1, 2 & 3 emissions show that we have made progress in reducing our reported
emissions. In 2022, our combined emissions were down 18.8% on 2019 from 559kg of CO2e to 454kg of
CO2e and as a result our full time equivalent intensity ratio was 1,167kg of CO2e which represents a 6.7%
reduction from 1,340kg of CO2e in 2019.

; Comparing our current 2022 emissions data for Scope 1 and 2 to 2019, highlights the positive impact of
estate changes and energy efficiency initiatives undertaken by the company. Scope 1 gas emissions were
down 3.3% on 2019 from 123kg of CO2e to 119kg of CO2e and Scope 2 purchased electricity emissions
showed a 31.3% reduction in the same period from 355kg of CO2e to 244kg of CO2e. This was largely
driven by a relocation of the Nottingham and Manchester offices to more sustainably managed facilities and
a smaller estate of 58,082 sq ft, (20% less than 2019) also moving to a policy in 2022 of setting heating to
21 degrees as standard across all locations.

In addition, as current leases come up for renewal, energy efficiency will be a key criteria in the decision to
extend a lease or relocate to a more suitable location. Hence in Nottingham, the Company moved to a building
that utilizes a Photovoltaic (solar power) array on the roof to reduce the requirement to source electricity from
the national grid, whilst the June 2021 move to the new Manchester office enabled the Company to move to
a Building Research Establishment’s Environmental Assessment Method (‘BREEAM’) Certified building. This
will enable Brown Shipley to track and monitor the buildings environmental, social and economic sustainability
performance using an internationally recognized standard. BREEAM certification means Brown Shipley are
in a more sustainable environment that enhances the well-being of our people who work there and helps
protect natural resources, by responsibly sourcing partners and suppliers in a coordinated manner with the
landlord. The move to new premises also demonstrated a positive environmental impact in terms of energy
use, thus providing a saving in cost and carbon emissions through lower use.

In September 2016, the group entered into a partnership with Egencia, a business travel solutions provider.
This partnership helps us to deliver on our aim of promoting business-related travel by public transportation
wherever possible, to reduce our Scape 3 grey fleet emissions and to monitor the impact of business travel
on our carbon footprint.

In 2022 the data collected on Scope 3 emissions identified a 13.8% increase in our grey fleet emissions from
80kg of CO2e to 91kg of CO2Ze although it should be noted that this is partly due to improved data quality. In
2022, we have made improvements in our data collection methods; by conducting a ‘Staff Commuting Survey’
enable us to capture more accurately the detail of our grey fleet emissions and colleague commuting practices,
by type of vehicle, whilst 2019 used an estimated vehicle size in making the calculation. Looking forward,
given the improvements we have made in data quality, our 2022 Scope 3 emissions will be used as a baseline
to identify and set targets for future reductions.

Like all wealth managers, in meeting the needs of our clients, we need to find the balance between face to
face and technology enabled meetings and as a result some business travel will continue to be required.
However, it is clear that business travel patterns will change as all COVID-19 restrictions have been lifted.
Brown Shipley remains committed to addressing climate change through promoting more sustainable
business practices and we will continue to encourage the use of virtual meetings both with clients and
internally, whilst promoting the use of lower carbon methods of travel wherever possible.

The Company also has a long-term commitment to reduce paper usage; in line with best practices, printers
are preconfigured to print in black and white and recto-verso, reducing paper and ink usage. We only use
FSC-certified paper, ensuring that the wood within the product is from FSC-certified material, recycled material
or controlled wood. The group has also developed and established a long-term internal awareness campaign
to reduce unnecessary and/or excessive printing. As part of this campaign the Company has continued to

36%) of clients were registered on the platform and 33% (2021: 30%) were receiving all documents digitally.

i. Impairment of financial assets

Under IFRS 9 the Company’s loan loss impairment method uses a forward-looking ECL approach. The
Company has been recording the allowance for expected credit losses for all loans and other debt financial
assets not held at FVPL, together with loan commitments and financial guarantee contracts, in this section all
referred to as ‘financial instruments’.

The ECL allowance is based on the credit losses expected to arise over the life of the asset (the lifetime
expected credit loss or LTECL), unless there has been no significant increase in credit risk since origination,
in which case, the allowance is based on the 12 months’ expected credit loss (12mMECL) as outlined below.
The Company’s policies for determining if there has been a significant increase in credit risk are set out in
Note 36 (Risk management)

The 12mECL is the portion of LTECLs that represent the ECLs that result from default events on a financial
instrument that are possible within the 12 months after the reporting date.

Both LTECLs and 12mECLs are calculated on either an individual basis or a collective basis, depending on
the nature of the underlying portfolio of financial instruments.

The Company performs an assessment, at the end of each reporting period, of whether a financial
instrument's credit risk has increased significantly since initial recognition, by considering the change in the
risk of default occurring over the remaining life of the financial instrument. This is further explained in Note 36.

Based on the above process, the Company groups its loans into Stage 1, Stage 2 and Stage 3, as described
below:

- Stage 1: When loans are first recognised they are categorised as Stage 1 and the Company recognises
an allowance based on 12mECLs. Stage 1 loans can also include facilities where the credit risk has
improved and the loan has been reclassified from Stage 2.

- Stage 2: When a loan has shown a significant increase in credit risk since origination, the Company
records an allowance for the LTECLs. Stage 2 loans can also include facilities, where the credit risk has
improved and the loan has been reclassified from Stage 3.

- Stage 3: If a loan is subsequently considered credit-impaired (as outlined in Note 36). The Company
records an allowance for the LTECLs.

To assess whether the credit risk of a financial instrument has increased significantly, a combination of
quantitative and qualitative factors is considered. These include the analysis of the term structure of the
probability of default as well as watchlist surveillance based on reasonable and supportable information. The
credit risk assessment is symmetrical in nature, allowing financial assets to move back to stage 1 if the

m, Property and equipment
The cost of an item of property and equipment shall be recognised as an asset only if it is probable that future
economic benefits associated with the item will flow to the Company and the cost can be measured reliably.

Property and equipment are recognised at cost less accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment
losses. Depreciation is charged on such assets so as to write off the cost of assets over their estimated useful
lives, using the straight-line method. In practice, leasehold improvements are depreciated over the remaining
life of the relevant lease, office equipment and computer hardware is depreciated over two to five years and
right of use (leased) assets are depreciated over the remaining lease term applicable to each property.

At each year end, the Company reviews the carrying amount of its tangible assets. Impairment is recognised
if the carrying value of an asset exceeds the recoverable value (the higher of the value in use or the net selling
value). Impairment is recognised in the income statement.

Gains and losses on disposal are determined by comparing the proceeds on sale with the carrying amount of.
the asset, and are recognised in the income statement.

Purchased goodwill represents the excess of the cost of an acquisition over the Company's interest in the fair
value of identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed. Goodwill is capitalised at cost and reviewed for
impairment at least annually. Any impairment is recognised immediately in the income statement and is not
subsequently reversed.

p. Taxation

Current tax assets and liabilities for the current and prior years are measured at the amounts expected to be
recovered from or paid to the taxation authorities. The tax rates and tax laws used to compute the amount are
those that are enacted or substantively enacted at the statement of financial position date.

Deferred tax is provided on temporary differences at the statement of financial position date between the tax
bases of assets and liabilities and their carrying amounts for financial reporting purposes.

Deferred tax liabilities are recognised for all taxable temporary differences, except where the deferred tax
liability arises from the initial recognition of goodwill or from an asset or liability in a transaction that is not a
business combination and, at the time of the transaction, affects neither the accounting profit nor taxable profit
or loss and, when temporary differences are associated with investments in subsidiaries and associates,
where the timing of the reversal of the temporary differences can be controlled and it is probable that the
temporary differences will not reverse in the foreseeable future.

Deferred tax assets are recognised for all temporary differences, carry forward of unused tax credits and
unused tax losses, to the extent that it is probable that taxable profit will be available against which the
deductible temporary differences, and the carry forward of unused tax credits and unused tax losses can be
utilised. The only exception is where the deferred tax asset relating to the deductible temporary differences
arises from the initial recognition of an asset or liability in a transaction that is not a business combination and,
at the time of the transaction, affects neither the accounting profit nor taxable profit or loss and, when
temporary differences are associated with investments in subsidiaries and associates.

The carrying amount of deferred tax assets is reviewed at each year end and reduced to the extent that it is
no longer probable that sufficient taxable profit will be available to allow all or part of the deferred tax asset to
° be utilised. Unrecognised deferred tax assets are reassessed at each year end and are recognised to the
extent that it has become probable that future taxable profit will allow the deferred tax asset to be recovered.

q. Income recognition
Revenue is measured at the fair value of the consideration received or receivable and represents amounts —
receivable in the normal course of business net of value added tax.

Under IFRS 9, Interest income and expense is recognised in the income statement for all instruments
measured at amortised cost and certain loans to customers purchased at a discount to nominal value. Interest
is calculated using the effective interest method (‘EIR’) or on a straight line basis where this approximates to
EIR. The effective interest rate is the rate that exactly discounts estimated future cash flows through the
expected life of the financial instrument to its net carrying amount. When calculating an effective interest rate,
the Company estimates the future cash flows considering all contractual terms of the financial instrument but
does not consider future credit losses. The calculation includes all fees and commissions paid or received
between the parties to the contract that are an integral part of the effective interest rate, transaction costs and
all other premiums or discounts.

r. Pensions and other employee benefits

The Company operates a defined contribution scheme for employees, which most employees are members
of. In anumber of instances it makes contributions to employees’ self-invested personal pensions. In addition,
it has a legacy defined benefit scheme which was closed to the future accrual of benefits in 2005.

Defined contribution schemes - a defined contribution scheme is one under which the Company pays a fixed
contribution into a separate entity, usually a privately administered pension insurance plan. The Company has
no legal or constructive obligations to pay further contributions to such schemes under any circumstance.
Contributions are recognised as an employee benefit expense when they are due.

Defined benefit scheme - a defined benefit scheme is one that defines an amount of pension benefit that an
employee will receive on retirement, usually dependent on one or more factors such as age, years of service
and salary. The pension expenses and obligations are calculated according to IAS19. Actuarial gains and
losses are recognised by the Company in full through the statement of comprehensive income. The
surplus/(deficit) recognised in the statement of financial position is the fair value of the scheme assets less
the present value of the defined benefit obligation at the year-end. The present value of the defined benefit
obligation is calculated using the Projected Unit Credit Method with actuarial calculations carried out on an
annual basis.

Under IAS 19, other short-term employee benefits (to be settled within 12 months) are recognised when the
employee has rendered the service and are measured at the undiscounted amount of benefits expected to be
paid in exchange for that service. This applies to the employee's salary and associated employer social
security costs, other benefits provided by the companysuch as the annual discretionary bonus, life insurance,
private health and medical insurance.

Long term employee benefits arrangements have conditions attached to secure their vesting. This typically
applies to employee bonus schemes not wholly settled within 12 months after the period in which the employee
renders the related service. The Company offers several types of bonus schemes that fall into the category
of long term benefits and recognises these benefits over the vesting period of the bonus scheme. For the
Retention and Long term Incentive plan awards, this is on a straight line basis over the vesting period (typically
three years). For the Material Risk Takers (MRT) scheme, which is paid in tranches, the Company applies the
‘benefit formula’ option and recognises the amounts in the Income Statement of the years in which they vest.
This equates to 60% recognition in the year awarded, with the remaining 40% recognised over three years on
a straight line basis. For further information on the vesting period of the MRT scheme refer to page x of the
Directors’ Report.

Ss. Leases

IFRS 16 sets out the principles for the recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure of leases and
requires lessees to account for all leases under a single on-balance sheet model similar to the accounting for
finance leases under IAS 17. The standard includes two recognition exemptions for lessees — leases of ‘low-
value’ assets (e.g. personal computers) and short-term leases (i.e. leases with a lease term of 12 months or
less).

» At the commencement date of the lease, a lessee recognises a liability to make lease payments (i.e. the lease
liability) and an asset representing the right to use the underlying asset during the lease term (i.e. the right-
of-use asset). Lessees are required to separately recognise the interest expense on the lease liability (see

>. Note 3) and the depreciation expense on the right-of-use asset (see Note 19).

Lessees are also required to remeasure the lease liability upon the occurrence of certain events (e.g. a change
in the lease term, a change in future lease payments resulting from a change in an index or rate used to
determine those payments). The lessee recognises the amount of the remeasurement of the lease liability as
an adjustment to the right-of-use asset.

The Company elected to use the exemptions applicable to the standard on lease contracts for which the lease
terms ends within 12 months as of the date of initial application, and lease contracts for which the underlying
asset is of low value, as a result the company has expensed £33k (2021 £34k) in relation to photocopiers
within the Income Statement.

received no such rent concessions in 2021 or 2022 on any of its leased properties and so this amendment

15 _ Derivative financial instruments — Held for trading

The Company uses derivative instruments only in limited circumstances and for economic hedging purposes
only; therefore it has not adopted hedge accounting in accordance with IFRS 9.

Currency forwards represent commitments to purchase foreign and domestic currency, including undelivered
spot transactions.

Currency and interest rate swaps are commitments to exchange one set of cashflows for another. Swaps
result in an economic exchange of currencies or interest rates (for example fixed to floating rate) or a
combination of these (i.e. cross currency interest rate swaps). No exchange of principal takes place, except
for certain currency swaps. The Company's credit risk represents the potential cost to replace the swap
contracts if counterparties fail to perform their obligation. The risk is monitored on an ongoing basis with
reference to the current fair value, a proportion of the notional amount of the contracts and the liquidity of the
market. To control the level of risk taken, the Company assesses the counterparties using the same
techniques as for its lending activities.

Measurement of Expected Credit Losses (‘ECLs’)

The Company has adopted ECL modelling which has been developed by and is maintained by its parent
company, Quintet, and the process is summarised below. The Company provides input to the ECL model and
receives the output from Quintet, which is used to compute ECL amounts.

For the most material exposures, being the investment portfolio and the customer loan portfolio, the ECL is
calculated by breaking down the cash flow structure of the exposure and assuming a number of defaults
along its lifeline.

The Company’s definition of ‘default’ is when either or both of the following have taken place:

e the Company considers that the borrower is unlikely to pay its credit obligations to the Company (or
to Quintet), in full and without recourse by the Company to actions such as realising security (for
example, insolvency of the borrower), and/or

« the borrower is past due more than 90 (consecutive) days on any material credit obligation to the
Company (or to Quintet), including the payment of interest or principal and including failure of the
borrower to repay or refinance the loan in full at maturity and which remains expired for a period
exceeding 90 days.

ECLs are calculated based on probability-weighted scenarios to measure the expected cash shortfalls,
discounted at an approximation to the Effective Interest Rate (‘EIR’).

ECLs are measured as the probability-weighted present value of expected cash shortfalls over the remaining
expected life of the financial instrument. Three quantities mainly drive this measure of loss:

(i) the instrument's probability of default (‘PD’),
(ii) the loss given default (‘LGD’), and
(iii) the exposure at default (‘EAD’).

IFRS 9 requires that estimated ECLs reflect an unbiased and probability-weighted amount that is determined
by evaluating a range of possible outcomes.

The Company incorporates three forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios in its ECL calculation process,
namely a base scenario, an upside scenario and a downside scenario. These scenarios consider the impact
of various factors such as changes in interest rates, inflation and GDP growth on financial asset and property
valuations in the calculation process. Probability weights are attributed to each scenario by a committee of
experts, under the review of senior management. This assessment is performed on a quarterly basis.

When relevant, the assessment of multiple scenarios also incorporates how defaulted loans are expected to
be recovered, including the probability that the loans will be recovered and the value of collateral or the amount
that might be received for selling the asset.

Provisions for ECLs relating to undrawn loan commitments are assessed as set out in Note 27. The calculation
of ECLs (including the ECLs related to the undrawn element) in relation to revolving facilities is explained
below.

The mechanics of the ECL method are summarised below:

Stage 1: The 12 month ECL is calculated as the portion of Lifetime ECLs (‘(LTECLs’) that represent the
ECLs that result from default events in a financial instrument that are possible within the 12 months after
the reporting date. The Company calculates the 12 month ECL allowance based on the expectation of a
default occurring in the 12 months following the reporting date. These expected 12-month default
probabilities are applied to a forecast EAD and multiplied by the expected LGD rate and discounted by an
approximation to the original EIR.

- Stage 2: When a loan has shown a significant increase in credit risk since origination, the Group
records an allowance for the LTECLs. The mechanics are similar to those explained above, including the
use of multiple scenarios, but PDs and LGDs are estimated over the lifetime of the instrument. The
expected cash shortfalls are discounted by an approximation to the original EIR.

- Stage 3: For loans considered credit-impaired the Group recognises the lifetime expected credit losses
for these loans. The method is similar to that for Stage 2 assets. The default PD for Stage 3 assets is set
at 100%, although specific impairments for these assets are subject to Group Credit Committee
judgement, based upon ‘dedicated in-depth’ analysis.

- Loan commitments and letters of credit: When estimating LTECLs for undrawn loan commitments, the
Group estimates the expected portion of the loan commitment that will be drawn down over its expected
life. The ECL is then based on the present value of the expected shortfalls in cash flows if the loan is
drawn down, based on a probability-weighting of the three scenarios. The expected cash shortfalls are
discounted at an approximation to the expected EIR on the loan. For credit cards and revolving facilities
that includes both a loan and an undrawn commitment, ECLs are calculated and presented together with
the loan. For loan commitments and letters of credit, the ECL is recognised within Provisions.

Forward looking information incorporated in the ECL models

Both the assessment of credit risk (for non-low risk exposures) and the estimation of the expected credit
losses (ECL) rely on the term structure of the cumulative default probability that can be constructed from a
migration matrix, computed on the basis of three scenarios representing favorable, baseline and unfavorable
market conditions, i.e. forward-looking assumptions.

The relative weights given to these scenarios, by a dedicated committee of experts, is in turn used to compute
the average migration matrix from which the expected term structure of cumulative probability of default is
computed. On that basis, the credit risk will be assessed and the ECL calculated. The weights will be refreshed
on a quarterly basis.

Liquidity risk and funding management
Liquidity risk is the risk that the Company is unable to meet its financial obligations as they fall due. These
obligations include customers’ deposits, both on demand and those with a contractual maturity date.

In accordance with the PRA's Liquidity Adequacy Standards, the Company produces an ILAAP, which is
presented and reviewed by the Board on at least an annual basis.

The Company’s strategy is to maintain a high-level of liquidity to ensure that it can meet its obligations as they
fall due in all circumstances. Investments are funded by own funds and client deposits; the Company does
not borrow from the wholesale money markets.

The Company manages its liquidity risk using a series of Board approved metrics (as adopted annually in the

ILAAP) and these are calculated and monitored daily. Adherence to these limits is monitored closely and any

material breaches are reported to senior management.

In addition to the daily liquidity/cashflow monitoring the Company also monitors its Liquidity Coverage Ratio
(‘LCR’) on a monthly basis and undertakes periodic stress testing of its liquidity position. This includes taking
reasonable steps to identify an appropriate range of realistic/adverse circumstances and events that might
occur and measuring the impact of those on the Company's cash flow. In the light of this analysis, the
Company's contingency funding arrangements are regularly reviewed and tested to ensure that it could
survive different liquidity stresses.

Reporting also includes forecasts for liquidity (i.e. a warning of potential breaches). If any metrics were
breached then approved crisis management procedures would be invoked.

Market Risk

Market risk is the risk of adverse financial impact on the Company resulting from changes in the levels of
interest rates and exchange rates as well as from changes in the prices of securities and other financial
contracts held or entered into.

The Company does not take proprietary positions with the intent of trading.

Foreign Exchange Risk

The Company has no significant subsidiary operations with a functional currency. other than sterling although
a small proportion of the Company's assets and liabilities are denominated in foreign currencies. Overnight
currency positions are small. The risks arising from exposure to foreign currency movements are therefore
limited. There were no significant net open positions in any currencies at 31 December 2022.

Currency derivatives other than forward foreign exchange contracts are not normally used to mitigate currency
exposures.
