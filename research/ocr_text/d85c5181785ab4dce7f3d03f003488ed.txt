BANKING IN A DEPRESSED ECONOMY

Although 1975 saw a slight overall improvement in the
fortunes of the financial sector from the very difficult
position of twelve months ago, the deterioration
throughout the year in the general economic situation
created considerable problems for banks. The growth
rate of deposit resources entering the domestic banking
system declined while advances remained subdued.
In contrast, internal operating costs rose sharply again
and, together with support operations for the less stable
areas of the financial sector, acted as a dampener on
earnings.

The overall rate of domestic economic activity fell back dramatically during 1975, and recorded the
sharpest reduction since 1931. Unemployment rose steadily throughout the year to fresh post-war
peaks and, despite a slowing down towards the end of the year in the rate of price increases,
inflation remained at historically high levels. On a more optimistic note, the massive balance of
payments deficit of 4974 was halved, although this was probably more a reflection of the depth of
the recession than an indication of any structural improvement. in contrast with the self-generated
near-hysteria of late 1974, the Stock Market regained its confidence during the year, with sharp
improvements in most indices, and the amount of new capital raised reached record levels.
The contribution of the equity market to financing domestic industrial activity remained sadly
lacking, however, and recent moves towards the creation of a more efficient flow of funds in this
area must be welcomed.

Within the Co-operative Movement, we feel that this type of ” capital-market ” activity functions
rather well, with the Bank Group playing an increasingly important role as collector of surplus
funds, both from members of the Movement and elsewhere, and providing financial facilities in
appropriate cases where requested. In 1975 the Movement continued to expand strongly as a direct
challenge to the economic circumstances and the Bank re-agserted its key role as a financial
intermediary.

Monetary policy during 1975 remained stringent, at least where the money supply was concerned,
and with the expressed prime aim to keep its growth below the actual rate of increase in money
national income. Jn the successful achievement of this objective, the Authorities made massive sales
of giit-edged stocks and Treasury Bills outside the banking system. In consequence, the enormous
Public Sector Borrowing Requirement, which had appeared as a problem of considerable proportions
earlier in the year, was easily financed, although the prospects of a repeat performance in 4976/77
are not too hopeful.

In contrast, with demand for bank finance at low levels, the Authorities announced in February
the suspension “ for the time being of the operation of the supplementary special deposits
scheme. | Qualitative guidance was, however, maintained with a continuing emphasis on finance
or facilities for the expansion of exports, savings of imports, domestic industrial investment and

working capital. Restraints on lending to persons, for purely financial transactions and, to 4 limited
extent, to property companies, were also kept in force.

Interest rates, which began the year at very high levels, fell away slowly during the first half of
1975, but the need to maintain dornestic rates at a premium relative to overseas markets resulted in
a reversal almost to previous levels later in the year. Bank base rates, including that of the Co-oper-
ative Bank, foliowed this broad pattern, and were adjusted in line with prevailing money market
conditions on six separate occasions during the year. Rates available on funds placed on deposit
with the banks were also adjusted in the same directions, but the differential between Base and
Deposit rates was widened from 23 to 4 percentage points during the year. Early in the year, when
Deposit rates had already fallen below that level, the Bank of England withdrew its request to
banks not to pay more than 94% on deposits of under £10,000.

The continuing depression in the property sector has perpetuated the problems of the so-called
fringe" banks. With particularly heavy commitments in this area, several of these institutions
have announced huge trading losses and the Bank of England's ” lifeboat ” seems set for a muc
longer voyage than was originally intended. In retrospect, one cannot help admiring both the way
in which the Bank of England organised its rescue operations and the willingness of members of
the banking community to rally round. One of the more acceptable consequences of these actions is
that the overall integrity of the United Kingdom banking system has been maintained. The
Co-operative Bank's own support operations have continued throughout the year with a steady
reduction in the level of involvement.

The Bank of England took steps during the year to increase its supervision of the banking system
as a whole, with a specific emphasis on establishing criteria for sound banking operations. An
Official White Paper on the subject is expected in the near future as a prelude to legislation, which
will provide a closer definition of the regulating powers of the Bank of England than exists at the
moment. Through our contact with colleagues in the large Co-operative banking sector within
the European Community, we have seen both the benefits and handicaps of a regulated banking
system, From experiences in the United Kingdom in the past two years, however, there clearly
exists a case for closer central control of banking activity, perhaps on the lines being worked out
in Brussels, and for more public accountability. Control of this nature may be preferable to
nationalisation of all or part of the banking sector,

Trading activity for the banks was a frustrating «perience in 1975, Although the savings ratio of
the general public reached extremely high levels, only a small portion of the increase was placed
with the banking sector. Total deposits of the major banks at the end of the year were only marginally
higher than twelve months previously; the Co-operative Bank Group achieved a comparatively
greater rate of expansion. _ Employment of those funds which were available created further problems
in the depressed economic situation. Contrary to popular opinion and criticism, the banks—and in
this respect our own bank was no exception—would have been only too pleased during the year
to increase their lending for viable industrial and commercial propositions. We have, however
further developed the extent of our lending to Co-operative Societies and have been intimately
involved in the financial aspects of many major retail store developments, Reserve assets of the
banks remained well above the accepted minimum of 124% of eligible liabilities.

In view of the difficult circumstances which prevailed throughout the year, the trading results of the
Co-operative Bank Group give cause for reasonable satisfaction. After applying the co-operative
principle of minimising charges to customers and also after making full provision for bad and
doubtful debts, the Group's accounts showed an increase in pre-tax profits,

GROUP ACTIVITIES

Co-operative Bank Limited

For the parent Bank, the highlight of the year was the acceptance into membership of the Bankers
Clearing House, the first bank for almost forty years to gain this recognition. Membership establishes
the Bank for the first time as a truly independent member of the banking community. To mark the
eecasion the Bank changed the style of its cheques, and incorporated unique security features in
both the new design and the printing process, to produce a distinctive and attractive cheque.

Indicative of the Bank's increased involvement in the wider financial community, a new City
Office—designed to house the Bank's Monay Department and City operations—was Opened on
1st May at Cornhill in the City of London, As part of its programme of astablishing regional offices
in all appropriate centres of population, the Bank also opened new premises at Bolton, Coventry,
Glasgow, Reading, Romford and Stockport during the year, while the well-established Durham
branch was re-located in larger modern premises to provide for expansion and new business.
Several more major branches will be opened in 1976, and plans are well in hand to extend the
network of offices in England, Wales and Scotland even further in future years.

A unique feature of the banking “ package” from the Co-operative Bank is the service provided
by local Co-operative Retail Sacieties in around 4,000 stores throughout the United Kingdom.
The expanding regional branch network of the Bank forms an increasingly efficient base to
co-ordinate and pracess day-to-day activities and support the excellent work conducted by Society
officials on behalf of the Bank. The considerable importance we place on these in-store banking
facilities was demonstrated again during the year by the continuing detailed examination of new
ideas for further improvement and support. A pilot exercise held in the stores of the Bolton and
Wigan Regional Co-operative Society was highly successful and plans are well advanced for
further development, in line with the ideas revealed to Retail Society Chairmen and Chief Officials
at their Conference in Manchester in October.

With the dynamic nature of these developments and the excellent press which the Bank's range
of services continues to atiract, it is not surpiising that 1975 saw a tapid expansion in the number

of people taking advantage of our services. Indications are that the Bank's rate of growth was
considerably in excess of that of its major competitors, -

Whilst continuing to offer traditional overdraft, term loan and credit, facilities, the provision of
finance for activities in the Co-operative sector has meant the Bank's intimate involvement in the
establishment of large-scale retail developments, such as hypermarkets and superstores. Tie Bank
has also continued to extend its involvement with corporate sector customers, both in traditional
areas and in the number of new fields which are opening up for development.

The Bank has also developed closer relationships with the “ Clubs “ sector and has assisted in a
large number of new developments, particularly in Yorkshire and the North-East, A Co-operative
Bank ’ Stand” at the National Clubs Exhibition offering attractive terms and loan facilities received
considerable attention, including a brief visit from the former Prime Minister, Sir Harold Wilson.

Participation at conferences organised by the Labour and Co-operative Movements has always
been a feature of Co-operative Bank service and this year, the Bank provided a service as usual at
the Labour Party and TUC Conferences and at a large number of other national and regional
meetings. With a very high proportion of national Trae Union accounts, the Bank has developed
expertise in advising individual Unions on their financial affairs and particularly in the employment
of their considerable reserve resources,

On the international front, the Bank was again prominent in the affairs of the Association | of
Co-operative Savings and Credit Institutions of the EEC, being particularly involved in assisting
with the establishing of ” view-points “” on proposed policies for the harmonisation of banking
regulations and consumer credit. We were also pleased to be invited to join the International
Confederation of Popular Credit (CICP), based in Paris, which brings together a large number of
banks operating under a Co-operative philosophy in Europe, Africa and North America and offers
a service specifically geared to the needs of small and medium sized businesses. Considerable
rewards in the way of business and exchange of staff experiences and knowledge are anticipated
from these new relationships, to supplement the already useful assistance deriving from membership
of the International Co-operative Alliance and a shareholding in the International Co-operative
Bank based in Basle.

During the course of the year the Bank completed its top management structure with the recruitment
of Mr. W, J. Luxton and Mr, E, J. Hewitt as Directors and Jojnt General Managers with responsibility
for Administration and Financial Control respectively.

Co-operative Commercial Bank Limited

During the year this Bank further consolidated its position in the City of London, in preparation
for a more definitive role in the medium and longer term markets. The level of dealing operations
in the sterling inter-bank and other domostic markets was again maintained, while further finks
were established with corporate customers in traditional markets and beyond,

F, C. Finance Limited

Throughout 1975 F, C, Finance Limited concentrated on the retrenchment necessary to withstand
the serious effects of the continuing property crisis, and the reduced market available in its traditional
fiald of both personal and industrial instalment credit, Despite these problems, the company was
able to return to profit in the year with an operating profit, before exceptional provisions and taxation,
of £1,205,000 compared with £1,632,000 for 1974. Exceptional provisions made against loans
and land held for development totalled £664,000 with resultant net profit before taxation of
£541,000. This compares with a loss of £127,000 for 1974 and enables a dividend of 6% to be paid.

With the worst of the property situation behind it and with some benefit from improved money
market conditions which is reflected in the return of depositors’ funds, the company is looking more
optimistically to the future. There is greater confidence that the anticipated upturn in the economy
will facilitate worthwhile improvement in turnover and continued profitability in 1976,

Agricultural Finance Federation Limited

This company had a successful year praducing a substantial increase in turnover, whilst maintaining
a satisfactory profit margin. Quality of business has been the foremost consideration during the
year and this is reflected in a healthy collection position.

Staff

During the year the number of staff employed at branches has remained virtually constant. This
was achieved notwithstanding a significant increase in the amount of work processed at branch
level and reflects a very real effort on the part of all those concerned. Overall, the average number
of staff employed has increased, largely as a result of entry into the London Clearing and the
consequent centralised handling of transactions passed through our in-store banking points.
During the year our staff have worked with enthusiasm and a devotion to duty, without which it
would not have been possible to maintain the Bank's expansion and their contribution is gratefully
acknowledged.
